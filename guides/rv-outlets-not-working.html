RV outlets not working: The 120 volt side

RV outlets run on 120 volts from the shore pedestal or the generator, not on the 12 volts that most of the coach runs on. That one difference explains a lot: when shore power dies, the outlets, the microwave and the air conditioner die with it, while the lights, the vent fan and the furnace carry on from the battery, and the refrigerator keeps cooling by switching itself to propane. The two sides fail differently, and only one of them can kill you.

By OriginRV. Last reviewed on Oct 23, 2026.

Can injure you This is not the 12-volt side, and it can kill you. The 120 volts in an RV is the same voltage as the wiring in a house, and the shore connection is usually larger than a house circuit: 30 amp or 50 amp service, which can deliver far more current than the 15 or 20 amp circuit behind a household outlet. Treat all of it the way you would treat your home's electrical panel. Do not open a panel or work inside an outlet with the shore cord connected. If you feel any tingle from the vehicle body, unplug immediately and treat it as a serious fault rather than something to live with. Opening a panel or an outlet is only safe with the power off, and off means the shore cord unplugged at the pedestal rather than the breaker switched off, with the voltage confirmed absent at the terminals before you touch them. If the job is more than resetting a breaker or a GFCI, stop and hand it to a certified RV technician or electrician.
The short version: outlet circuits are daisy chained, and a ground fault outlet at the head of the chain protects every outlet after it. So one tripped GFCI can kill a whole string of outlets while the others carry on working. Find it and reset it before you test anything. If it will not reset, there are three likely reasons, and if none of them applies, the device may be due for replacement.

Start here: Which RV outlets are dead, and why the 12 volt side still works

Every outlet in the whole RV is dead

Look upstream: the pedestal, the shore cord, the main breaker on the panel. If you have a 50 amp RV, remember it has two 120 volt legs, and one leg being dead takes out roughly half the coach while the other half works normally, which looks like a partial fault but is really a supply fault.

A string of outlets dead, others fine

Three things do this: a ground fault outlet that has tripped at the head of that chain, a branch breaker that has tripped, or a connection that has loosened at one of the outlets feeding the rest. Outlets on another branch can keep working either way, which is what makes the fault look partial.

One outlet dead, neighbours fine

That points at the outlet rather than the supply: a connection that has loosened, a push-in terminal that has worked free, or corrosion in a damp location. It is also worth confirming the outlet is on the circuit you think it is, because a dead outlet beside a live one may simply be fed from a different branch.

Outlets work on shore power but not on the inverter

Usually not a fault. An inverter feeds a subset of outlets rather than all of them.

Outlets dead but all the 12-volt things work

Confirms the fault is the 120 volt side, and the two systems are separate except at the converter.

Any shock or tingle from the bodywork

Stop here and read the hot skin section. That is a different class of problem.

The power path: pedestal, breakers, GFCI

The pedestal

Before anything inside the RV, confirm the pedestal itself is supplying power. At a campground, check the breaker at the post first; if it is tripped, that one is the park's to fix.

The easier version of that check is the one you can do every time you plug in. A protector with a built-in tester, or an electrical management system, reads the pedestal before it lets any power through, and refuses to connect on an open ground, an open neutral, reversed polarity, or a voltage outside its range. Not every device sold as a surge protector does that. The units that do disconnect also pause for a minute or two before reconnecting, so a protector that looks dead after a fault may simply be counting down.

The main breaker

The main breaker is the largest switch on the panel, usually a wider one at the top or at one end, with the branch breakers in a row beside or below it. On a 50 amp coach you will usually see two main handles tied together, because a 50 amp service arrives as two 120 volt legs. It is the switch between the shore cord and everything else: the cord lands on it, and every breaker below it is fed through it. Throwing it cuts the whole 120 volt side at once, which is what makes it the first thing to check when nothing in the coach works.

You cannot tell whether a breaker is tripped by looking at it. To reset one you have to push it firmly to off and then firmly back on, not just flick it. If it will not stay on, stop, because there is a real fault on that circuit and repeatedly resetting it is not a fix.

The branch breakers are the rest of the breakers on the panel: one per circuit, each named on the panel's own label. Same reset action. A dead branch kills everything on it. A chain of outlets is not a series circuit: the receptacles are fed one from the next, but they are wired in parallel, so an outlet that has failed does not by itself take out the outlets after it. What takes them out is a tripped GFCI at the head of the chain, or a connection that has loosened at one of the outlets feeding the rest. RV outlets are self-contained devices that make their connections when the back is closed, with no junction box behind them, so a loose feed-through is a real failure mode here rather than a rare one.

The GFCI

RVs put a ground fault outlet at the head of a chain, normally near a sink or in a wet location, and it protects every outlet downstream of it. When it trips, the whole downstream string goes dead at once. The practical consequence is that the outlet you are testing may be perfectly healthy and simply not receiving any power. Check every ground fault outlet in the coach rather than only the nearest one, because each one protects the outlets downstream of it, and the outlet you are testing may be downstream of a GFCI in another room. Reset any that has tripped, then test again.

The inverter

An inverter feeds only some of the outlets, not all of them, so an outlet that works on shore power and not on the inverter is usually a design decision rather than a fault. Do not assume an outlet is dead just because you switched the inverter off: while shore power is connected, the inverter passes it through to the outlets it serves.

Which outlets share a breaker

The outlets in an RV are not all on one circuit, and how they are split is not the same from one coach to the next. A common arrangement is most of the general outlets on one breaker, the ground fault outlets on another, and the kitchen counter outlet on a third of its own, because that is the one most likely to meet a kettle or a coffee maker. Your coach may group them quite differently, and the panel's own label is the only reliable guide to yours.

Which outlets are dead, and which breakers they sit on, tells you where to look. If every dead outlet is on one breaker, the fault is that breaker or something on its circuit. If the dead outlets sit on different breakers and all those breakers are still on, the fault is further upstream, near the pedestal or the shore cord. And if the only dead outlet is the kitchen one, look at what is plugged into it before you look anywhere else.

It also decides how you plug in. Two high-draw appliances sharing one circuit will trip it even when both are working perfectly, which is a load problem rather than a fault, and a 30 amp coach has only one 120 volt leg to spread everything across. A 50 amp coach has two, which is why the same appliances can behave differently in the two.

Why a GFCI will not reset

A ground fault outlet is not a fuse and it is not a breaker. It compares the current leaving on the hot wire with the current returning on the neutral, and it trips when the two stop matching, which is what happens when current finds a path it should not have: through water, or through a person. That comparison is also why one of them sits at the head of a chain rather than at every outlet, because it protects everything downstream of it.

A GFCI that will not reset is a specific diagnostic rather than a vague problem. The device has a lockout feature that prevents reset in two situations.

One, there is no power reaching the outlet

A GFCI cannot reset without power, so a dead upstream breaker presents as a failed GFCI. Check the supply before condemning the device.

Two, it has failed its own internal test

A GFCI that has failed this way may no longer be able to protect you in a ground fault. This is the one that matters for safety, because it still looks fine.

When to replace it

The instruction is direct: if the red indication continues, or the GFCI will not reset, replace it, after ruling out moisture as the reason it is refusing. That instruction comes with the device and it is blunt: if the indicator light will not turn green when you press and release reset, or the device cannot be reset at all, it has to be replaced rather than lived with.

Water in the box is one reason a GFCI will not reset. Unplug the shore cord at the pedestal first, as the safety note at the top says, then dry the outlet and try the reset again.

Testing an outlet

The outlet tester, and what it reads

The simplest test needs no instrument at all. Plug in something you can see or hear working, a phone charger or a lamp, and that answers the first question on its own: whether the outlet has power at all. It will not tell you whether the wiring behind it is correct, so treat it as a starting point rather than a diagnosis.

A multimeter tells you whether voltage is present. Hold the probes only by their insulated handles with your fingers behind the finger guards, and keep the two tips from touching each other or anything else while the circuit is live. A tester tells you how the outlet is wired: whether the ground is connected, whether hot and neutral are reversed, whether the neutral is open. None of those show up in a voltage reading. Any of those three readings is a wiring fault rather than a supply fault, so stop using that outlet and have the wiring corrected before you plug anything back in.

Test a known good outlet first

Every tester has its own pattern of lights and the charts assume a particular model. Plug it into an outlet you know works so you can see what a good result looks like on your tester before you try to interpret a bad one.

Press the test button on the GFCI and listen

A working GFCI clicks off when you press test, and clicks back on when you press reset. If test does nothing, that is another sign the device is not functioning.

What actually causes dead outlets

The problems behind dead outlets are a tripped GFCI, a tripped pedestal breaker, a loose connection at an outlet where the wiring is push-in rather than screwed down, and corrosion at outlets in damp locations.

Hot skin

If you feel a tingle or a shock when you touch the vehicle body, or when you touch a metal part of it while standing on wet ground, that is a condition commonly called hot skin. It means voltage is present on the chassis that should not be there, and that is a possibly deadly shock hazard.

What to do

Can kill you HOT SKIN IS A SHOCK HAZARD TO ANYONE TOUCHING THE VEHICLE, AND IT CAN KILL. UNPLUG FROM SHORE POWER IMMEDIATELY. Do not keep using the RV while it is doing this, and do not treat it as something to tolerate. It has to be found and fixed before the RV gets plugged into shore power, or generator power, again. If you are not certain what you are doing, this is the point to stop and call a certified RV technician.

Why it happens

The first reason is a compromised safety ground: the path that should carry stray current safely back to the source is broken, loose or corroded somewhere between the pedestal and the frame, whether in the shore cord, an adapter, the pedestal outlet, or a ground screw inside the coach. The second is current reaching the chassis at all, which comes from a failed water heater element, the filter capacitors in a converter, a screw or a staple driven through a wire, or any contact between a hot conductor and the frame.

With the ground path intact, that leakage drains away and the chassis sits at earth potential. With it broken, the chassis floats, and the leakage has nowhere to go except through whoever touches the vehicle. Normal leakage on its own can put 40 to 80 volts on the frame; a real fault can put the full 120 there, and that is the difference between a tingle and a fatality.

Reverse polarity is a separate fault and it does not, by itself, put voltage on the chassis. It is the thing people blame for hot skin, and it is a real hazard in its own right.

Note that this is the 120 volt grounding and bonding system, which is a completely different thing from the 12-volt chassis return. They share the word ground and nothing else.

A cheap multimeter cannot meaningfully assess the grounding and bonding path, which is why a hot skin problem is best handed to someone with the right instrument.

What it costs

A GFCI is a cheap part, and a weather-resistant one costs a little more, which is what you want for an outdoor location.

Professional diagnosis is charged as its own fee, and labor by the hour, and both are the reason to do the two free checks first. Finding out whether it is a tripped breaker or a reset GFCI before anyone is paid to look takes a few minutes rather than a callout.

Where it gets expensive

A hot skin fault or a damaged run inside a wall is a different scale of job, and neither is something you can estimate from an article. If the safety tests point at either, the next call is to a certified RV technician rather than to a parts supplier. The RV repair directory lists mobile technicians and service centers by state.

Why are some of my RV outlets dead and others fine?

Because the outlets on a branch circuit are wired in a chain rather than individually, and a ground fault outlet protects the ones after it. If the first outlet in that chain has tripped, everything downstream of it is dead while outlets on a different branch carry on working. Find the GFCI outlet on that circuit and reset it before assuming anything is broken.

Why won't my RV GFCI outlet reset?

There are three reasons a GFCI will refuse to reset, and they are worth taking in order. There is no power reaching it. It has been miswired with the line and load leads reversed. Or it has failed its own internal test, which means it may no longer be able to protect you. If it still will not reset after you have confirmed it has power and is wired correctly, replace it.

The pedestal breaker looks fine. Can it still be tripped?

Yes. You cannot tell whether a breaker is tripped by looking at it. Push it firmly to the off position and then firmly back on. If it will not stay on, you have a real fault on that circuit, and resetting it repeatedly is not the answer.

Some outlets work on battery and some don't. Why?

Because an inverter only feeds a subset of the outlets, not all of them. An inverter/charger has an AC pass-through circuit with internal relays that feed those outlets from shore power when it is present and from the batteries when it is not. So outlets that work on shore power but not on the inverter are simply not on the inverter circuit. It is a design choice, not a fault.

I can feel a tingle when I touch the RV. What should I do?

Stop and treat it as a serious fault. A shock or tingle from the vehicle body is usually called hot skin, and it means something is putting voltage onto the chassis. Unplug from shore power first. Do not keep using the RV while it is doing this. The causes include a fault in the shore outlet, a missing or broken ground, or reverse polarity at the pedestal, and it needs to be found properly rather than lived with.

How do I test an RV outlet properly?

A plug in outlet tester is the quickest tool and it will tell you about missing grounds, reversed wiring and open neutrals by which lights illuminate. A multimeter tells you the voltage but not the wiring arrangement, so for outlets the tester is the better instrument. Test the outlet you know works first so you can see what a good result looks like on your own tester.

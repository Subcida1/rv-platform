RV black tank clogged: How to unclog it, and when to call a tech

A black tank typically clogs from a mound of solids that built up under too little water. The makers document what puts water back in, what the enzyme treatment needs to work, and the order to dump in. None of them publishes a chemical drain-clearing procedure.

By OriginRV. Last reviewed on Nov 4, 2026.

The short version: A clogged black tank is almost always a mound of solids that built up because the tank ran on too little water. The makers' instructions for removing it are water, an enzyme treatment and time, in that order: fill the tank through the toilet, let it stand, dump, and repeat. Driving will not shake it loose, probing the outlet does damage, and stacked chemicals kill the enzyme treatments that do the work. If the buildup does not budge after multiple soaks and dumps, or the valve itself will not open, stop and call an RV technician.

Why a black tank clogs

A black tank does not suddenly clog from solids arriving all at once. It clogs from a mound that forms under the toilet, and the mechanism fits in one sentence: "If solids collect directly under the toilet due to insufficient water, they form a mound." The mound builds over time, and the tank keeps working the whole time, which is why the first symptom is usually a dump that runs noticeably slow rather than a dump that stops.

There is one exception worth naming: if the clog arrived all at once, something was dropped into the tank. Construction debris, a dropped object or a wad of wipes blocks the outlet the moment it arrives, and no amount of soaking moves it. Wipes are the common one, and the label is the problem: the ones sold as flushable are not biodegradable, whatever the package says. Camco's tank advice: "avoiding non-biodegradable items (looking at you, wet wipes!)". If you know what went down and roughly when, stop flushing and look first: an object that lodges in the toilet neck or the pipe above the tank can be fished out before it reaches anything worse. That is a different problem with a different fix, and the giveaway is timing: a mound takes weeks, an object takes one flush.

The gauge is not the tank. A probe coated with waste or residue can "falsely complete that circuit" and read full on an empty tank, so a full reading is a reason to look rather than a diagnosis. Where the toilet drops straight into the tank, the honest check is physical: hold the pedal down and look down the bowl with a flashlight, careful not to drop anything in. Not every toilet drops straight into the tank, so this works on some coaches and not others.

How to unclog a black tank

The makers document one sequence for a clog, and every direction points at the same one: put water in, add an enzyme treatment, give it time to work, and dump. The order matters, each step is short, and patience is doing most of the work.

1. Fill the tank with water

For a clog, the water is doing two jobs: it re-floats what has settled, and it gives the treatment something to work in. Add it through the toilet with the pedal held halfway, which fills the bowl without opening the valve, and flush it through until the tank is nearly full.

Jayco's manual gives a smaller number, "Add no more than 5 gallons of water at this time," with "at least 2 inches of water in the bowl" so the ball valve seal stays wet. That is the prevention dose for a fresh tank at the start of a trip, not the clearing dose. A clog needs the tank filled, not sprinkled.

2. Add an enzyme treatment, and give it time

Thetford describes what these products do: "These treatments consist of blends of enzymes and beneficial bacteria, which break down waste and toilet paper into smaller particles." The documentation behind this page puts a clock on the work: tank additives "can take up to 48 hours after use to fully oxygenate a standard holding tank." So this is measured in days rather than minutes.

Temperature decides how well any of this works. Thetford: "In hot weather, the odors overpower the enzyme product, while in cold weather, the enzymes slow down to the point they don't work." Unique publishes the working range: "Optimal temperatures for our products are between 45-85 degrees Fahrenheit." A cold coach in January and a hot one in July are both slow places for a clog to clear, and the answer in both cases is the same: more time.

If you have been running a chemical treatment, it has to be out of the tank before the enzymes can do anything. Fill and rinse the tank a few times until the water runs clear: the residues that kill the bacteria sit in the sludge, and one rinse does not reach them.

3. Dump

Dump in the makers' order: Lippert's Waste Master manual says to "empty the black (waste water) tank first, followed by the gray (sink and shower water) tank(s)," because the gray water flushes the hose. Listen while the tank drains, or fit a clear elbow so you can see what is coming out, and let it run until it stops. A dump that starts strong and then slows is telling you the mound is still there.

4. Repeat

If the first round moves some and then stops, the answer is the same sequence again: water, treatment, time, dump. A mound that has been building for a while does not dissolve in one pass, and each pass takes some of it out. Jayco's manual gives the rule that keeps it from coming back: "Use only septic-safe toilet paper to avoid clogs and maintain proper function."

Other ways to unclog a black tank

Water, enzymes and time are the sequence the makers document, but they are not the only tools. These are the others, in rough order of how directly they attack the clog.

A tank wand

A wand is a tube with a spray head that goes down through the toilet and works on the tank walls and the mound directly. Camco's Flexible Swivel Stik is the shape to look for: a flexible shaft that reaches offset tanks, with a shutoff valve at the handle. The flexible version reaches tanks that sit offset from the toilet; a straight wand suits a tank directly below. Run it with the dump valve open and the sewer hose connected, and never leave it running unattended: the water has to have somewhere to go.

A backflush fitting at the dump outlet

These fit between the tank outlet and the sewer hose and push water backwards, up the drain pipe and into the tank. Valterra's Flush King says it "Removes clogs in drain pipes" and "Refills and flushes tank at any height or bend of plumbing," and it carries an anti-siphon valve so the dirty water cannot reach the fresh supply. Camco's Rhino Blaster Pro does the same job and adds a clear elbow "so you can see when your tank is clean." Use a dedicated hose for this, never the one you drink from, and work in short bursts with the valve open between them.

A clog-specific treatment

The regular tank treatments are maintenance products; at least one maker sells a version formulated for a clog. Unique Camping + Marine: "Clear-It Black Tank Unclogger is formulated to clear clogged black tanks." It is still a bacteria and enzyme product rather than a chemical, so it wants the same things the sequence above does: water in the tank, a temperature in the working range, and time.

The ice trick

You will hear that a bag of ice down the toilet, then a drive around the block, scrubs the tank clean. The people who have tested it are not impressed, including the company that sells tank-cleaning tools: Camco calls it a myth, because "RV holding tanks aren't built for physical scrubbing inside, so this trick is more of a gimmick than a real solution." If the tank is already blocked, ice is not the tool. If you want the agitation, the water you add with it is doing the work.

This is a job a shop may decline: it is liability and it is unpleasant work, so a working DIY method matters more here than on most repairs.

Check the tank vent

A holding tank has to breathe. The roof vent is what lets air in and odors out, and it can be blocked from the outside as easily as damaged: a mud dauber nest, leaves or debris packed into the cap all keep the tank from venting the way it should. There is a quick test for it. Start a dump, then press the toilet flush pedal and hold it: that opens a second air path into the tank. If the flow speeds up when the toilet opens, the tank was pulling its air through the toilet instead of the vent, and the vent is where to look. Check the roof vent for any obvious obstruction before you decide the tank is the only problem. The black tank page covers the rest of the tank's hardware.

What not to do when the tank is clogged

Do not fill it full and drive it to shake the clog loose. RVs travel with water in their tanks all the time: the fresh tank carries your water in, and the waste tanks carry it out when there is nowhere to dump on the way. What does not work is the road as a clog-clearing tool. A clog is a slow problem: unless the coach has been parked since the mound formed, it has already ridden along on every drive since, and it will not pop loose from one more. What the drive does add is the weight of a full tank moving against the valve, and that weight differs from coach to coach; a broken valve on the road is a fast problem.

Do not probe the outlet. The valve body and its seals are plastic, and a rod or a wand worked blindly into the outlet is how a clog becomes a leak. Nothing in the documents behind this page suggests poking at it.

Do not stack chemicals. Beyond the enzyme problem above, none of the documents behind this page publishes a chemical drain-clearing procedure, and this page will not invent one. A tank full of the wrong chemistry is harder to clear than a tank full of water.

Sewer gas safety

Can injure you Keep your face away from the open outlet and from the toilet when the tank is open or being dumped. Hydrogen sulfide, the gas that gives sewage its smell, is heavier than air and collects in low, enclosed spaces (OSHA, ATSDR), and the smell stops being a warning at around 100 parts per million, where the sense of smell fatigues within minutes (NIOSH, ATSDR). The fatal cases on record are people who entered tanks and enclosed spaces. Work from the side, not over it. Lippert's Waste Master manual asks for "proper personal protective equipment" and names gloves and goggles.

When to call a technician

The sequence above is the owner's half. These are the technician's half:

  • Nothing drains at all with a full tank and the valve open. That points at the outlet or the valve body itself, which is work under the coach.
  • The gate valve will not open or close. Forcing a gate valve breaks its seal, and a replacement is a technician's job.
  • The tank, a fitting or the valve body leaks or is cracked. A repair, not a clearing.
  • The water-and-time sequence has been performed and nothing has moved.

What it costs

The sequence itself costs water and one enzyme treatment, and the treatment is the only purchase. The tools in the second half are one-time buys that stay in the sewer kit. A technician visit for a blocked outlet or a failed valve is a service call plus labor, and what drives the labor is access to the valve. The routine page covers what to do every time you dump, which is the version of this job that never needs a rescue.

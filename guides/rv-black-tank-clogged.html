RV black tank clogged: How to clear it, and when to call a tech

A black tank clogs from a mound of solids that built up under too little water, and it clears the same way it formed: water, treatment, time, and a dump. This is the sequence the makers document, the three things that make it worse, and the point where the job stops being yours.

By OriginRV. Last reviewed on Oct 15, 2026.

The short version: A clogged black tank is almost always a mound of solids that built up under too little water, and it clears with water and time, in that order: fill the tank through the toilet, let it stand, dump, and repeat. Never drive it to shake it loose, never probe the outlet, and never stack chemicals, because the residues kill the enzyme treatments that do the work. If nothing moves after two rounds, or the valve itself will not open, stop and call a technician.

Why it clogged

A black tank does not clog from solids arriving all at once. It clogs from a mound. Walex Products, which makes tank treatments and sensors, calls it "Mounding" or Pyramid Formation and describes the mechanism in one sentence: "If solids collect directly under the toilet due to insufficient water, they form a mound." The mound builds over weeks, and the tank keeps working the whole time, which is why the first symptom is usually a dump that runs slow rather than a dump that stops.

The other half of the picture is the sensor. The same guide explains why a coated tank lies: "When waste or residue coats a probe, it can falsely complete that circuit" even when the tank is empty. A gauge that reads full on an empty tank and a dump that runs slow are the same story from two ends: solids are sitting where water should be.

The sequence, in order

Everything the makers publish about a clog points the same way: put water back in, give the treatment time to work, and dump. The order matters, and each step is short.

1. Add water through the toilet

Jayco's towable manual gives the number for a fresh tank: "Add no more than 5 gallons of water at this time," and it asks you to keep "at least 2 inches of water in the bowl" so the ball valve seal stays wet. For a clog, the water is doing two jobs: it re-floats what has settled, and it gives the treatment something to work in. Add it through the toilet with the pedal held halfway, which fills the bowl without opening the valve, and then flush it through.

2. Add an enzyme treatment, and give it the time the makers say

Thetford, which publishes more about tank chemistry than most, describes what these products do: "These treatments consist of blends of enzymes and beneficial bacteria, which break down waste and toilet paper into smaller particles." Two limits come with that, both from the same page: "In hot weather, the odors overpower the enzyme product, while in cold weather, the enzymes slow down to the point they don't work," and "Remnants of chemical-based products will kill enzymes and reduce the performance of natural products in your holding tank." The vent maker puts a clock on the work: tank additives "can take up to 48 hours after use to fully oxygenate a standard holding tank."

So this is measured in days rather than minutes. If you have been running a chemical treatment, it has to be out of the tank before the enzymes can do anything.

3. Dump, in the order the makers use

Lippert's Waste Master manual states the order and the reason in one line: "Common industry practice is to empty the black (waste water) tank first, followed by the gray (sink and shower water) tank(s). This order allows the gray water to flush out the sewer hose and helps prevent sewage from remaining in the hose after emptying the tanks." If the black tank runs slow, let it run slow, and give the sequence its time.

4. Repeat

If the first round moves some and then stops, the answer is the same sequence again: water, treatment, time, dump. A mound that has been building for weeks does not dissolve in one pass, and each pass takes some of it out.

The vent, while you are up there

A holding tank has to breathe, and a blocked vent holds pressure that slows every drain. Lippert's 360 Siphon vent cap describes its own job as drawing odors out of the tank and "fostering an oxygen-rich environment" that speeds waste breakdown. Check the roof vent for a mud dauber nest, a crushed cap or debris before you decide the tank itself is the only problem. The black tank page covers the rest of the tank's hardware.

Do not do these three things

Do not drive it to shake it loose. Jayco's manual says to avoid traveling with the tanks full, for weight and handling. A clog is a slow problem; a broken valve on the road is a fast one.

Do not probe the outlet. The valve body and its seals are plastic, and a rod or a wand worked blindly into the outlet is how a clog becomes a leak. Nothing in the documents behind this page suggests poking at it.

Do not stack chemicals. Beyond the enzyme problem above, none of the documents behind this page publishes a clog-clearing chemical procedure, and this page will not invent one. A tank full of the wrong chemistry is harder to clear than a tank full of water.

When to stop and call a technician

The sequence above is the reader's half. These are the technician's half, and they are worth naming so nobody spends a weekend on a job that needs a shop:

  • Nothing drains at all with a full tank and the valve open. That points at the outlet or the valve body itself, which is work under the coach.
  • The gate valve will not open or close. Forcing a gate valve breaks its seal, and a replacement is a technician's job.
  • The tank, a fitting or the valve body leaks or is cracked. A repair, not a clearing.
  • Two full rounds of water-and-time have changed nothing.

One safety note, and why it is here

Can injure you Keep your face away from the open outlet and from the toilet when the tank is open or being dumped. Hydrogen sulfide, the gas that gives sewage its smell, is heavier than air and collects in low, enclosed spaces (OSHA, ATSDR), and the smell stops being a warning at around 100 parts per million, where the sense of smell fatigues within minutes (NIOSH, ATSDR). The documented deaths in tanks and sewers are the same pattern: someone opened it and put their face near the opening. Work from the side, not over it.

What it costs

The sequence itself costs water and one enzyme treatment, and the treatment is the only purchase. A technician visit for a blocked outlet or a failed valve is a service call plus labor, and what drives the labor is access to the valve. The routine page covers what to do every time you dump, which is the version of this job that never needs a rescue.

RV furnace will not light: Propane pressure and the furnace's own sequence

A furnace that will not light while the stove burns fine is the most misleading fault in an RV, because the stove is not evidence that the gas supply is healthy. Every appliance draws from the same tank through the same regulator at the same very low pressure, and the furnace asks for more gas than anything else in the coach. So there are two questions, not one: is enough gas reaching the furnace, and does the furnace's own ignition sequence run to the end? This guide answers them in order.

By OriginRV. Last reviewed on Oct 14, 2026.

Can kill you You will smell propane in an RV from time to time, and usually it means nothing. When a burner lights, not all of the gas lights at once and a little escapes, and in a space this small you will notice it. A whiff that clears in a second is normal, and so is a smell right after something has lit. A smell that lingers, or one you notice when nothing has just lit, is not normal. For that: put out any flame, do not touch a switch, a phone or an appliance, get everyone out, and turn the tank valve off from outside if you can reach it safely. Let the coach air out. If the smell is still there after that, leave the area and call 911.
The short version: A furnace needs gas arriving at the right pressure and in enough quantity, and it needs its own ignition sequence to complete. The stove only proves the first half of the first one. Most furnaces that will not light are short of gas flow rather than short of a furnace, and the two things a reader can fix in a minute are a tank valve that is only part way open and an excess-flow device that has tripped.

Start here: What your symptom means

The blower runs and nothing else happens

The furnace has started but its safety circuit has not closed, so it never tries to light. The usual cause is the sail switch itself: a switch that has failed, or debris caught in it so the paddle cannot move. After that, look at what would stop it being tripped, which is not enough air moving. That means a blocked return air grille, a crushed duct, or a battery too flat to spin the blower to full speed. Clear the returns and charge or plug in, then try again.

The blower runs, you hear clicking, and it never lights

The furnace is trying. The spark is firing and the gas valve is opening, but there is no flame to prove. Either no gas is arriving, or what arrives is air. Check the gas side first.

It lights and then shuts off after a few seconds

The flame lit but the furnace did not sense it, so it closed the valve as a safety measure. The sensor is a probe in the flame and the signal it sends is tiny, so a dirty probe or a poor ground will do this on its own.

It lights, runs, then short cycles

Almost always overheating from restricted airflow rather than a gas fault. Open every register, clear the returns, and look for a crushed or disconnected duct. A failing sail switch will cause it as well, because the furnace loses its proof of airflow part way through a cycle.

Nothing happens at all

The furnace is not being asked to run, or has no power. Check the thermostat, the furnace's own on and off switch, its fuse or breaker, and the battery. A furnace that is completely silent is a 12-volt question rather than a gas question.

It worked last season and will not light now

Dirt and insects rather than wear. A furnace that has sat unused is more likely to need cleaning than one that runs every weekend.

The stove burns yellow or weak as well

That points at the gas supply rather than the furnace, and it moves the regulator up the list. A healthy propane flame is blue.

How the gas gets to your furnace

It is a short path, and every part of it can be the reason your furnace is cold. Liquid propane sits in the tank under pressure. A short hose called a pigtail carries vapor from the tank to the regulator. The regulator drops that pressure twice, in two stages. From there a pipe runs under the coach and branches to each appliance: the stove, the furnace, the water heater, the refrigerator.

Two things about that path explain most of the faults on this page. The first is that every appliance draws from the same regulator at the same pressure, so nothing is dedicated to the furnace and nothing is protected from a supply problem. The second is that the pressure is very low, and the amount of gas that can flow through a regulator and a pipe is limited. A system that is weak rather than dead will still run a stove burner and starve a furnace.

The pressure, and why the trade writes it in inches

Your appliances run on about four tenths of one psi. That is not a typo. A car tire holds roughly eighty times that, and the pressure reaching your furnace is closer to what you could produce blowing through a straw than to anything in a compressed air line.

Because the number is so small, the propane trade does not measure it in psi. It measures it in inches of water column, which is exactly what it sounds like: the pressure it takes to hold a column of water one inch high. One psi is about 27.7 inches of water column, so your appliance pressure of about 0.4 psi is written as 11 inches of water column, usually shortened to 11 inches WC or 11" WC. That is the number you will find printed on the regulator and the number a technician will quote at you.

Two figures matter. The first is 11 inches, which is the standard setting for the second stage of the regulator. The second is 14 inches, which is the most pressure that may be delivered into any appliance, and it is a code limit rather than a maker's preference. A regulator is meant to hold 11 under load and to close off before it ever reaches 14. A regulator that cannot do either is out of specification whether or not an appliance has complained yet.

The test itself is a manometer, which is a gauge built for pressures this small. An ordinary psi gauge is the wrong instrument for it, because at this scale the needle barely moves. That test is a technician's job, but it is a cheap one and it settles the question in minutes rather than by guesswork.

Why the stove can burn while the furnace will not

The stove is a small load. A single burner draws a few thousand BTU an hour, while a furnace draws several times that, and a furnace is the largest gas user in the coach by a wide margin. So a system with a partial blockage or a regulator that is past its best can still feed one burner while the furnace, which needs far more gas at the same moment, never gets enough to light and stay lit.

The other half of the answer is a safety device you probably have not thought about. Inside the pigtail, or at the tank fitting, there is an excess-flow device: a spring-loaded valve that slams shut when gas starts moving faster than it should, which is what a broken hose would do. It is there to stop a full tank emptying into the air. It does not seal completely, and it resets when the pressure on both sides of it evens out, which is why a stove can still light through a device that has tripped.

That device is a common reason a furnace will not light after a tank swap or a refill, because opening the tank valve quickly is what trips it. The reset is to close the tank valve completely, wait half a minute, and open it again slowly.

The furnace's own sequence, and why the order matters

A furnace does not simply open a valve and light. It runs a fixed sequence, and each step has to finish before the next one is allowed to start. That is a safety design, and it is also the best diagnostic tool you have, because where the sequence stops tells you which part to suspect.

It goes like this. The thermostat calls for heat. The blower starts and comes up to speed, and a paddle called the sail switch is pushed by the moving air, which proves to the furnace that air is actually flowing before it allows any gas. A limit switch sits in the same circuit as an overheat guard. Once both are satisfied the control board waits a short purge period to clear the combustion chamber, then opens the gas valve and fires a spark at the burner at the same time. If a flame appears, a probe in the flame tells the board so and the spark stops. If no flame is proved within a few seconds, the board closes the gas valve and shuts down.

Three things follow from that order, and they are worth more than any parts list. The sail switch proves airflow, not temperature, so anything that reduces airflow stops the sequence at step two and looks nothing like a gas fault. Low battery voltage stops ignition, because a slow blower never trips the sail switch. And the furnace locks itself out after repeated failed attempts, which means the fault you are looking at may be the second one, with the first already cleared. Turning the thermostat off and back on is the reset on most furnaces, and some also need the power switch cycled.

Checking the gas side

Is the tank valve actually open

Not almost open. A valve that is part way open can pass enough for a stove and not enough for a furnace. Open it fully, then run a stove burner for half a minute to clear any air that got into the line, and try the furnace again two or three times.

Is there enough propane in the tank

A low tank vaporizes more slowly, so the pressure sags exactly when the furnace asks for the most gas. This is worse in cold weather, which is when you are running the furnace. If the tank is under about a quarter full, fill it before you spend anything on parts.

Has the excess-flow device tripped

Close the tank valve, wait thirty seconds, then open it slowly. If the furnace lights after that, the device was the fault and the cause was opening the valve too fast.

Is the regulator vent clear

The regulator has a small vent, and it has to breathe. Dirt, ice, snow or an insect nest in that vent will upset the pressure it holds. The vent should point downward and be clear.

Is the pigtail sound

The hose between the tank and the regulator ages, cracks and can be kinked or collapsed. Squeeze it along its length and look for cracks. Hoses have a shorter life than the metal parts they connect.

Is the regulator itself past its best

A regulator that has failed usually shows it everywhere: weak yellow flames on the stove, and a furnace that will not stay lit. Makers do not agree on a replacement interval, and a study for the Propane Education and Research Council found no engineering support for the shortest intervals in circulation, so treat age as a reason to have it tested rather than as a reason to replace it on sight. A manometer test at load is what actually decides it.

Checking the furnace side

Battery voltage first

A furnace needs a healthy 12 volts to spin its blower fast enough to trip the sail switch, and a flat battery accounts for a real share of these calls. Charge the battery or plug into shore power and try again before anything else.

The airflow path

Clear every return air grille, open the registers, and look for a duct that has come off or been crushed. Restricted airflow stops the sequence at the sail switch, and it is also what makes a furnace short cycle.

The outside vent and the burner

Insects nest in furnace intakes. Which ones depends on where you are, and in some regions a wasp or a mud dauber will fill an intake in a single season. Look into the outside intake and exhaust for nests, webs or debris and clear anything you find. Do not put a screen over the vent to keep them out. The maker of one common furnace states plainly that screens must never be fitted, because they restrict the airflow and make the furnace unsafe.

The lockout

If the furnace has tried and failed several times it may be sitting in lockout rather than still trying. Reset it by turning the thermostat off, waiting fifteen to thirty seconds, and turning it back on, or by cycling the furnace's power switch the same way. Then listen again.

What you can do, and where to stop

Owner-level: opening the tank valve properly, resetting a tripped excess-flow device, filling a low tank, clearing the regulator vent, charging the battery, replacing a fuse or resetting a breaker, clearing the return air and the ducts, removing an insect nest from the outside intake, and resetting a lockout.

Technician-level: anything involving the gas valve, the burner and its orifice, the electrode or the control board, and anything that means disconnecting a propane line. Regulator adjustment is on that list too, because it has to be set with a manometer rather than by feel. If your furnace has no outside access door, then even reaching the burner means pulling the furnace out, which is the same job as disconnecting the gas.

The line is drawn there for a reason rather than for caution's sake. Everything above it is either a valve you can see or a filter you can clean. Everything below it is either a pressurized gas connection or a part whose failure mode is a leak or a flame where there should not be one. A certified RV technician can be found through the RV repair directory, which lists mobile technicians and service centers by state.

Two things people get wrong

Screens on the furnace vent. The size of the mesh is what matters, and the argument against screens is really an argument against a fine one. A coarse screen that keeps wasps and mud daubers out does not restrict the vent the way a fine mesh does, and plenty of owners run one without trouble. One furnace maker does state plainly that screens must never be fitted, because a restricted vent makes the furnace unsafe. If you fit one, keep the mesh coarse, and check that it has not clogged.

Spiders are drawn to propane. This is repeated constantly and there is no evidence for it. Insects do nest in furnace intakes, which is real and is why you clean them, but entomologists report no association between spiders and gas, and the attraction is far better explained by warmth and by the other insects already there. The nest is the problem. The smell is not the reason.

If you smell gas

Propane is given an added smell so you can detect it, and a smell inside the coach is not a fault to investigate later. Put out any flame, do not operate switches, appliances or a phone, get everyone out, turn the tank valve off from outside if you can reach it safely, and call 911 from a distance.

For a routine check with no smell present, soapy water on the joints will show a leak as bubbles, and a large leak bubbles hard and fast. It finds leaks at fittings and nothing else: it will not find a regulator that is set wrong, and it will not find a flow problem that only appears when an appliance is running. Never use a flame to look for a leak, and never keep tightening a joint until the bubbles stop. If a fitting leaks, the system needs testing rather than another quarter turn.

What it costs

Four of these cost nothing to put right: a tank valve that was not fully open, a tripped excess-flow device, a flat battery, and a blocked return air grille. Between them they account for a good share of furnaces that will not light.

A manometer test is a small job. It is charged as a service call rather than as a repair, and it is the only thing that separates a supply fault from a furnace fault with any certainty. Have it done before you buy a furnace part.

Where it gets expensive

A regulator replacement is a modest part and a short job. A control board, a gas valve or a furnace that has to come out of the coach is a different scale entirely, and it is the reason the free checks and the pressure test come first. If the manometer says the supply is right and the furnace still will not light, the money is going into the appliance, and that is the point to get a second opinion rather than a second part.

Related guides

If the burner lights but you can smell exhaust inside the coach, or a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, stop using the furnace and read what the manuals warn about carbon monoxide. That is a combustion fault rather than a gas supply fault, and it is the one fault on this page you should not keep running to see what happens.

If the furnace lights and then half the coach goes dark at the same moment, the furnace is probably not the cause. Start at two appliances stopped at once, which is usually one shared 12-volt fault taking both down.

If it lights and runs but the coach never gets warm, the flame is fine and the air is not reaching you. That is a ducting and airflow question rather than a gas one, and the furnace troubleshooting guide works through it.

Why does my furnace not light when the stove works?

Because the stove is a small load and the furnace is the largest one in the coach. A system that is weak rather than dead can still feed a burner while the furnace, which needs several times as much gas at the same moment, never gets enough to light and stay lit. A tank valve that is only part way open and a tripped excess-flow device are the two most common versions of this.

What is an inch of water column?

It is the pressure it takes to hold a column of water one inch high, and it is how the propane trade measures appliance pressure because the numbers in psi are too small to work with. One psi is about 27.7 inches of water column, so the 11 inches your appliances run on is roughly 0.4 psi. It is the number printed on the regulator.

How do I reset my RV furnace after a lockout?

Turn the thermostat off, wait, and turn it back on, or cycle the furnace's own power switch if it has one. The furnace locks itself out after several failed attempts to light, so a reset is often the first thing to try and also the thing that hides the original fault. If it locks out again straight away, the fault is still there.

Why does my furnace run for a few seconds and then shut off?

It lit but did not prove the flame, so it closed the gas valve as a safety measure. The sensor is a probe sitting in the flame and the signal it returns is very small, so a dirty or carboned probe, or a poor ground, will do this on its own. Cleaning the probe is the first move and it is a technician's job rather than an owner's.

Can I fit a screen over the furnace vent to keep insects out?

It depends on the mesh. A coarse screen that keeps wasps and mud daubers out does not restrict the airflow the way a fine mesh does, and plenty of owners run one. One furnace maker states plainly that screens must never be fitted, because a restricted vent makes the furnace unsafe, so if you fit one keep it coarse and keep it clear.

Is it true that spiders are attracted to propane?

No evidence supports it, and it is repeated far more often than it is checked. Insects do nest in furnace intakes and that is a real problem worth cleaning, but the attraction is better explained by warmth and by the other insects already there. Entomologists report no association between spiders and gas.

How to tow a travel trailer: Hitching up, dealing with sway and tricks for reversing

Towing is a sequence, and eleven steps get a trailer attached to a truck. The wheels are always chocked before the tongue moves, the breakaway cable goes on before the chains, and the jack comes all the way up before the setup moves anywhere. A jack left part way down is the one that will soon be replaced.

By OriginRV. Last reviewed on Oct 14, 2026.

Can injure you Hitching your RV is one job you must ensure is done correctly. A coupler that is not fully seated and latched can come off the ball, and a breakaway cable that is not attached means the trailer cannot apply its own brakes if it does separate. Instead, it coasts freely. Always attach and couple an RV the same way, in the same order, every single time, and then inspect the hitch, ball, coupler, tongue latch, and the chains before you pull away. Being nervous about towing is normal.

The hitch: The ball, the coupler, and the latch

Three parts do the holding, and the sizes have to agree.

  • The ball. A travel trailer takes a 2 inch or a 2-5/16 inch ball, depending on the model. It has to be the size the coupler was built for, because the wrong size will not let the coupler lock down. A travel trailer also needs a vehicle equipped with a frame-mounted hitch rather than an accessory receiver, which is a ball bolted through a hole in the bumper. The vehicle's hitch package should be rated to match or exceed the travel trailer's gross weight and its hitch weight.
  • The coupler. This is the cup under the trailer's tongue that sits down over the ball. It seats properly only when the trailer is lowered straight onto the ball. Drag it across the ball instead and the latch will not close all the way.
  • The latch. The latch locks the coupler onto the ball. It goes closed only after the coupler is completely seated, and the coupler is only seated when it is all the way down over the ball. The latch should take firm effort to close. If it drops shut with no resistance, the coupler is not on the ball.

Height matters too. Set the hitch, or the weight-distribution hitch, so the trailer rides level once it is coupled and sitting in its driving position, with the tongue retracted and ready to go. A hitch set too high can make the trailer fishtail, and one set too low throws too much weight onto the tongue, which lifts the front of the tow vehicle and costs it steering traction. The maker of your hitch gives the adjustment procedure for its own hardware; follow it rather than guessing.

Hitching up, in eleven steps

Work through these in order, every time, even when someone is watching and you feel rushed.

  • 1. Chock the trailer wheels. The trailer must always be chocked when it is not in the tow position. Never rely on the tongue jack to hold a trailer in place.
  • 2. Raise the trailer tongue above the tow vehicle's ball. Use the tongue jack crank, or the EXTEND or UP button on an electric jack, until the coupler sits higher than the ball.
  • 3. Open the coupler latch. Open it fully so the ball can drop in.
  • 4. Back the tow vehicle into position. Put the ball directly under the coupler. A helper or a rear camera makes this easier.
  • 5. Lower the coupler onto the ball. Use the jack, or the RETRACT or DOWN button, until the coupler comes down completely over the ball.
  • 6. Close the coupler latch once the coupler is completely seated.
  • 7. Fit the weight-distribution bars. These are the spring bars, or equalizers, and they go on as the hitch maker directs. They put part of the tongue weight back onto the front axle of the tow vehicle and onto the trailer axle, so the combination sits level. Fitting them often means raising the tongue jack again once the tow vehicle is attached, so the bars can be loaded. Follow your hitch maker's manual for the bar procedure on your hardware.
  • 8. Attach the breakaway cable to a solid, permanent part of the tow vehicle, not to the hitch ball or any other removable piece. Leave enough slack in the cable to reach through a full turn.
  • 9. Attach the safety chains. Cross the left chain under the coupler to the right mounting point, and the right chain to the left mounting point, so the chains form an X. Leave each chain the same amount of slack, just enough to let the combination turn at full lock without the chains dragging on the road.
  • 10. Put the tow vehicle in park and set its parking brake, then retract the trailer's tongue jack all the way. Remove any dolly wheel or platform first, then wind the jack up to its highest point off the ground.
  • 11. Plug in the trailer harness. Push the connector home until it latches, and leave enough cable to follow the trailer through a turn.
Can injure you The spring bars store energy. A weight-distribution bar under load can snap back as it comes off, so release the tension the way the hitch maker specifies, keep your hands and face out of its path, and never lever a bar off while the trailer's weight is still on it. Fitting and removing the bars for your setup is set out in your weight-distribution hitch maker's instructions.

Checking the lights before you pull away

The harness carries power from the tow vehicle to the trailer's brakes, tail lights, clearance lights, turn signals and brake lights, so a plug that is not fully home leaves some of them dark. Push the connector in until it latches, and leave enough cable to follow the trailer through a turn.

Then walk the whole trailer with the tow vehicle's lights on and check each one in turn:

  • Tail lights and clearance lights. Switch the tow vehicle's running lights on. The tail lights, and the marker lights down both sides and across the top, should all come on together.
  • Brake lights. Press the brake pedal and have someone watch, or line the trailer up against a wall or a window and watch the reflection. The trailer's stop light and its turn signal share one wire on each side, so a side that turns also brakes.
  • Turn signals. Left, then right, watching the matching side of the trailer. If a side stays dark, you have found that side's brake light as well.
  • Hazard lights. Both sides flash together, which makes this the one test you can run and watch on your own.

A 12 volt circuit tester takes the guesswork out of a side that stays dark. Probe the connector and it tells you whether power is arriving from the tow vehicle or whether the fault is past the plug, in the trailer's own wiring.

The plug sits in the weather, and corrosion builds on the contacts over time. Wipe it clean and cover it between trips, and push it fully home every time you hitch.

Remove the wheel chocks last, once every light answers.

Loading the trailer so it does not sway

How you load the trailer decides how it tows, often more than any piece of hardware.

See what the load does. U-Haul's safe trailering demonstration loads the same trailer twice, once with the weight forward and once with it at the back, and shows what each one does behind the tow vehicle.

U-Haul's rule of thumb is 60 percent of the load ahead of the trailer's axle and 40 percent behind it. That is the same aim as the tongue weight range below, reached a different way.

  • Aim for a tongue weight of 10 to 15 percent of the loaded trailer weight. That is the downward weight at the front of the trailer that the hitch carries. An improperly loaded trailer, with too little weight on the tongue, is one of the ways sway starts.
  • Keep heavy items low, and over or near the axles. Load heavy objects on the floor or as low as possible. Weight placed high raises the trailer's center of gravity and makes it less stable.
  • Balance the load side to side. Spread cargo so neither side of the trailer carries more than its share, and so no axle is overloaded on one side.
  • Do not put heavy items at the back. Weight behind the axle lifts weight off the hitch, and a light tongue is what lets a trailer sway.
  • Secure everything inside. Loose items shift on the first corner. Tie down appliances and furniture if they can move.

Water is cargo

A full fresh tank is payload, not a free extra. Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard 120, the rule behind the weight label, treats on-board potable water as cargo, so every gallon in the tank comes out of the trailer's cargo carrying capacity the same way a suitcase does. Water weighs 8.3 pounds a gallon, which puts a 40 gallon tank at 330 pounds of capacity gone before anything is packed.

Two habits follow from it. Fill the fresh tank at the campground rather than at home where you can, and empty the waste tanks before you fill the fresh one. A full holding tank and a full fresh tank at the same time take a large bite out of the payload without carrying anything you meant to bring.

Weigh it, do not guess it

The ratings on the label are ceilings, and the only way to know where you sit under them is a scale. A public scale reads each axle separately, which is what you need, because the tow vehicle's front axle, its rear axle and the trailer's axle each carry a rating of their own. It is entirely possible to be under the trailer's total weight and still be over on one axle.

Where a scale needs a little arithmetic is the tongue weight. Weigh the tow vehicle alone, loaded exactly as it will be for the trip, then weigh it again with the trailer hitched. The difference is what is resting on the hitch.

If a scale is not worth the trip, a tongue scale gets you close. Measure at the hitch if you can, and if you measure at the jack instead, work out the ratio between the two readings once and apply it from then on. Take the reading with the weight-distribution bars released. The bars redistribute that weight rather than change it, so a reading taken with them loaded is not the trailer's tongue weight.

If the trailer has a garage or a rear ramp

Toy haulers are built around the weight that will sit behind the axles. A rear-load toy hauler leaves the factory deliberately heavy on the tongue, because the toys go in the back and bring it back into balance. A front-load toy hauler is the reverse: light on the tongue empty, and heavy once it is loaded, which is where the receiver and the tow vehicle's rear axle come under pressure. Both kinds change what they need from the hitch between loaded and empty, so set up for the way you are actually traveling.

Check the load again shortly after you set off, and again at fuel stops. Restraints settle and loads shift, and a load that was tight in the driveway is not always tight ten miles later.

The ratings behind all of this, what you can tow, what the trailer may weigh, and how the tongue weight is measured, are worked through one at a time in the towing capacity guide and the pin weight and payload guide. Read those before your first trip.

Sway, and what to do when it starts

Sway is the trailer swinging side to side behind you. It is caused by crosswinds, by the air pushed ahead of a passing vehicle, by poor weight distribution, or by simply driving too fast for the conditions.

A downgrade belongs on that list too, where the trailer is running downhill against the hitch, and so does a road surface that jolts the trailer sideways.

Sway is not one fixed speed for a given trailer. It depends on the trailer, on the tow vehicle and on how the load sits, and it comes on at a lower speed as the weight moves back. Speed is the one variable the driver holds, and a downgrade raises it without touching the pedal.

Once it starts, the movement can grow rather than settle. Each swing pushes the back of the tow vehicle sideways, and that steers the trailer again, so the trailer's swing can come back larger than the push that started it. That is the reason for the rule below: the corrective input your hands want to make is the one that feeds the swing.

If it starts, stay calm and do the opposite of what your hands want to do.

  • Ease off the accelerator gradually. Slowing down is what settles a sway.
  • Keep the steering still. Do not steer hard to correct it, because each steering input can feed the swing rather than stop it.
  • Do not brake hard. A hard stop on the tow vehicle can make a sway worse. If you need to slow further, apply the trailer brakes gently. That helps settle the trailer rather than straightening it, so make the correction small and wait for the sway to die down.
  • Stop somewhere safe once it has settled and work out the cause: the load, the speed, or the wind. Fix that before you carry on.

If the tow vehicle has trailer sway control, it is part of the stability control system, and it works by braking individual wheels and reducing engine power. It catches a sway that is starting and flashes a warning when it does, but it is a backstop rather than a fix. When it lights up, stop as soon as it is safe and check the tongue weight and the way the load is sitting.

Whether to carry on

The question is whether the sway settled by itself. A single push from a gust or a passing truck that damps out is normal, and the answer is to slow down and carry on. Sway that comes back at the same speed every time is the load telling you it is wrong: run well below the speed where it first appeared, and stop at the first safe place to move weight forward.

After any sway that made you slow down, walk the trailer before you go on. Look for a load that has shifted, a suspension that has sagged, a tire that has lost pressure or a wheel that has loosened, and a hitch that is still tight.

A weight-distribution hitch with a sway-control device does two jobs. The bars move part of the tongue weight onto the front axle of the tow vehicle and back onto the trailer's axle, so the two vehicles sit level. The sway control dampens the side to side movement when traveling. Both help, but neither one fixes a poorly loaded trailer. A trailer with too little tongue weight will misbehave no matter what combination of preventative measures you take.

Brakes, and the controller in the cab

The trailer's brakes are electric, and they work alongside the tow vehicle's brakes rather than instead of them. Use them together. A trailer this size needs a controller mounted in the cab, and the controller has to be matched to the trailer's brake system.

  • Set the gain. The gain is how hard the trailer brakes at a given application. Set it so the trailer pulls its own weight under braking without locking its wheels. The full setting procedure is in the trailer brake guide.
  • Test it at walking pace. With the trailer connected and everything switched on, creep forward and squeeze the controller's manual override. You should feel the trailer pull back against the tow vehicle. If nothing happens, the trailer brakes are not working, and the tow vehicle is doing all of the braking.
  • Attach the breakaway cable. The breakaway switch on the trailer's frame is a safety system. If the trailer ever separates from the tow vehicle, the cable pulls a pin and the switch applies the trailer brakes. The cable has to be attached to a permanent part of the tow vehicle, and the switch needs a charged battery on the trailer to work at all.

Which trailers must have brakes, how to test the breakaway battery safely, and the detail behind setting the gain are all in the trailer brake guide.

Tires and bearings before you pull out

  • Check the tire pressure cold, on the tow vehicle and on the trailer. Use the pressure on each vehicle's certification label rather than the number on the sidewall, and check both spares while you are down there.
  • Look at each tire. Cracks, bulges and uneven wear all matter. A trailer tire can look perfectly fine and still be too old to trust, which is why they are replaced by age rather than by tread depth. That rule, and how to read a tire's age, is in the tire guide.
  • Watch your speed. Check the speed rating molded on the sidewall, and do not assume yours is rated above 65 miles per hour. Pushing a loaded trailer past its rating is asking for trouble.
  • Periodically check the lug nuts. Wheel lug nuts settle and loosen, so they are re-torqued after the first few short distances and then checked periodically. A loose wheel can leave the trailer.
  • Maintain the bearings. Bearing wear shows up as wobble or uneven tire wear. Whether your axle bearings need grease or are sealed units that cannot be serviced, and how often they need attention, is set out in the trailer wheel bearing guide.

Driving with a trailer behind you

The first time you tow should not be the trip itself. Find an empty parking lot or a quiet road and practice there first: drive slowly, turn both ways, stop from low speed, and get the feel of how the trailer follows before you do any of it in traffic.

  • Turn wider than you think. The trailer cuts inside the path of the tow vehicle, so the rear of the trailer can run over a curb on a normal turn. Go further into the intersection before you begin the turn, and take it wide enough to clear.
  • Do not cut corners. Cut the corner in the tow vehicle and the trailer's wheels go over the curb.
  • Leave more room in front of you. The combination of the two vehicles is heavier and takes longer to stop, so following distance has to grow.
  • Expect other traffic to push you around. A large vehicle passing is a moving wall of air against the side of the trailer. Grip the wheel, hold your line, and slow a little if it helps. The same goes for crosswinds, where slowing down is the best defense there is. Try not to fight the push and pull.
  • Keep the speed down. Speed makes sway worse.
  • Take the hills gently. On long climbs the combination will be slow, so use turnouts to let traffic pass, and keep an eye on transmission temperature. On long descents, use a lower gear and let the engine hold the speed rather than constantly riding the brakes, because overheated brakes fade and stop working when you need them most. Use the brakes in short applications instead, hard enough to feel the speed drop and then off again, so they have time to cool.

Some states require a towing vehicle to stay in the right hand lane and to hold a lower top speed. California's handbook, for instance, sets both in law, so check the rules for the states you plan to tow through.

Reversing, where the steering is backwards

Because the trailer pivots at the hitch, reversing is counterintuitive. You steer one way and the back of the trailer swings the other way, which is completely unnatural at first. There is a trick that makes it click.

  • Put your hand at the bottom of the wheel. Hold the wheel at six o'clock. Now, whichever way you move your hand is the way the trailer goes. Move your hand left and the trailer backs to the left. Move it right and the trailer backs to the right.
  • Or steer towards the problem. If the hand position does not stick, drop it and think about it the other way round. Pick whatever you are worried about hitting and steer towards it: with a tree on your left, steer left as you reverse and the trailer's tail swings away from the tree. It works because the back of the trailer moves opposite to the way you turn the wheel.
  • Go slowly and steer in small amounts. The trailer does not react at once, so a quick corrective flick usually makes things worse and can jackknife the combination. Small inputs, made early.
  • Use a spotter. Agree on the hand signals before you start and keep the spotter in your mirror the whole time. If you cannot see the spotter, stop and let them know.
  • Plan to pull forward. If the trailer gets out of line, pulling forward and straightening up is a normal part of backing, not a failure. Reset and try again rather than fighting it.
  • Use your mirrors. A trailer this size blocks the view straight back, so set the mirrors before you leave and back in by them.
  • Reversing in the dark. Put a flashlight or two on the ground where you want the trailer to end up, and back towards them. In low light a marker on the ground is far easier to judge than a shadowed bumper, so you are aiming at something you can see.

Arriving, and unhitching

Can injure you Nothing gets unhooked until the trailer cannot roll. A trailer that is not chocked, or still resting on the ball, can move while you are behind it, and a tongue that comes off a jack drops fast. Parking brake on, wheels chocked, and stay clear of the tongue while it comes down.

Pick a site that is roughly level and back in slowly, checking for obstacles as you go. Before anything else, set the tow vehicle's parking brake and chock the trailer wheels. Nothing gets unhooked until the trailer cannot roll.

Level it side to side while it is still on the ball, by driving the low side's wheels up onto a ramp or a stack of blocks. That is the half of leveling that cannot wait, because once the trailer is off the hitch, moving it sideways means hitching up again. Front to back leveling is the easier half, and it comes later on the tongue jack.

Then, in order:

  • Fit the dolly wheel or platform if the jack has one.
  • Lower the tongue jack to the ground and let it take the trailer's weight.
  • Unhook the safety chains and detach the breakaway cable. Stow both clear of the tow vehicle. They come off here rather than first, because the trailer is chocked and the jack has it.
  • Release the weight-distribution bars. Raising the tongue jack makes this easier, and on some setups the tow vehicle comes up with it. Follow the hitch maker's procedure for your hardware.
  • Check that the coupler is not lifting the tow vehicle. If the back of the tow vehicle has come up off its own suspension, the jack has the trailer too high. Lower it until the trailer sits on the jack and the tow vehicle is back on its springs.
  • Open the coupler latch.
  • Raise the tongue jack until the coupler lifts clear of the ball.
  • Unplug the trailer harness. Leave it plugged in until this point if your jack is electric, so the jack draws on the tow vehicle rather than on the trailer's battery. Plugging the trailer in works just as well.
  • Pull the tow vehicle away.
  • Level the trailer front to back with the tongue jack.
  • Extend the slide-outs, then lower the stabilizer jacks. Stabilizers settle the trailer once it is level. They are not for lifting or leveling it.

Before you drive the tow vehicle away, do a walkaround and look at what you are leaving: the wheels of the RV chocked, the tongue jack on a firm block stand, the weight bars off and stowed, the wiring and chains and breakaway cable clear of the vehicle, and the trailer's coupler separated and above the hitch ball.

The mistakes that damage things

Almost all of the expensive damage from a first trip comes from a small handful of things left in the wrong position.

  • Driving with the tongue jack down. A jack that is still extended will catch on the first speed bump, driveway or dip. It is retracted to its highest point as part of the hitching procedure, and that step is not optional.
  • Driving, or leveling front to back, with the stabilizers down. Stabilizer jacks hold the trailer steady once it is parked and leveled. They are there to take the wobble out, not to carry the trailer's weight. Retract them fully before the trailer or the tongue jack moves, or they will be destroyed in short order.
  • Forgetting the breakaway cable. If it is not attached, the trailer has no emergency braking at all if it separates. Attach it to a permanent part of the tow vehicle every time. Never use the breakaway switch as a parking brake, because it is not built for that and the constant current can damage the wiring and the trailer's brakes.
  • Forgetting the antenna. A television antenna left standing is the most common thing left up on a roof, and it is the last thing you see as you hear it snap on an overhanging branch or a low structure. It is one line on a pre-trip checklist for a reason. The full walkaround that catches it, and the other things that are easy to leave up or down, is in the pre-trip walkaround checklist.

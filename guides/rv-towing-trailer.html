How to tow a travel trailer: hitching, sway and reversing

Towing is a sequence, and every step in it is there because something went wrong for somebody. Ten steps get a trailer attached to a truck. The wheels are chocked before the tongue moves, the breakaway cable goes on before the chains, and the jack comes up before either of them. A jack left down is the one that catches on the first speed bump.

By OriginRV. Last updated on 2026-10-08.

Can injure youThe hitch is the one job you cannot get roughly right. A coupler that is not fully seated and latched can come off the ball, and a breakaway cable that is not attached means the trailer cannot brake itself if it does separate. Couple it the same way, in the same order, every single time, and then put a hand on the latch and the chains before you pull away. Being nervous about this is normal. Skipping a step is not.

The hitch: the ball, the coupler, and the latch

Three parts do the holding, and each one has to match.

  • The ball. A travel trailer takes a 2 inch or a 2-5/16 inch ball, depending on the model. It has to be the size the coupler was built for, because the wrong size will not lock down. A travel trailer also needs a frame-mounted hitch rather than an accessory receiver, and the hitch package should be rated to match or exceed the trailer's gross weight and its hitch weight.
  • The coupler. This is the cup under the trailer's tongue that sits down over the ball. It seats properly only when the trailer is lowered straight onto the ball, not dragged across it.
  • The latch. The latch locks the coupler onto the ball. It goes closed only after the coupler is completely seated, and it should take firm effort to close. If it drops shut with no resistance, the coupler is not on the ball.

Height matters too. Set the hitch so the trailer rides level once it is coupled. A hitch set too high can make the trailer fishtail, and one set too low can throw too much weight onto the tongue. The maker of your hitch gives the adjustment procedure for its own hardware, and it is worth following rather than guessing.

Hitching up, in ten steps

Work through these in order, every time, even when someone is watching and you feel rushed.

  • 1. Chock the trailer wheels. The trailer must not move while you back under it.
  • 2. Raise the tongue above the ball. Use the tongue jack crank, or the EXTEND or UP button on an electric jack, until the coupler sits higher than the ball.
  • 3. Open the coupler latch. Open it fully so the ball can drop in.
  • 4. Back the tow vehicle into position. Put the ball directly under the coupler. A helper or a rear camera makes this easier.
  • 5. Lower the coupler onto the ball. Use the jack, or the RETRACT or DOWN button, until the coupler comes down over the ball.
  • 6. Close the coupler latch once the coupler is completely seated.
  • 7. Fit the weight-distribution bars. These are the spring bars, or equalizers, and they go on as the hitch maker directs. They put part of the tongue weight back onto the front axle of the tow vehicle and onto the trailer axle, so the combination sits level.
  • 8. Retract the tongue jack all the way. Remove any dolly wheel or platform first, then wind the jack up to its highest point off the ground.
  • 9. Attach the breakaway cable to a solid, permanent part of the tow vehicle, not to the hitch ball or any other removable piece.
  • 10. Attach the safety chains. Cross the left chain under the coupler to the right mounting point, and the right chain to the left mounting point, so the chains form an X. Leave each chain the same amount of slack, just enough to let the combination turn at full lock without the chains dragging on the road.
Can injure youThe spring bars store energy. A weight-distribution bar under load can snap back as it comes off, so release the tension the way the hitch maker specifies, keep your hands and face out of its path, and never lever a bar off while the trailer's weight is still on it.

Three more jobs finish the hookup. Plug in the trailer harness, then walk around the trailer with the lights on and check that the tail lights, brake lights, turn signals and markers all answer the tow vehicle. Remove the wheel chocks last.

Loading the trailer so it does not sway

How you load the trailer decides how it tows, often more than any piece of hardware.

  • Aim for a tongue weight of 10 to 15 percent of the loaded trailer weight. That is the downward weight at the front of the trailer that the hitch carries. An improperly loaded trailer, with too little weight on the tongue, is one of the ways sway starts.
  • Keep heavy items low, and over or near the axles. Load heavy objects on the floor or as low as possible. Weight placed high raises the trailer's center of gravity and makes it less stable.
  • Balance the load side to side. Spread cargo so neither side of the trailer carries more than its share, and so no axle is overloaded on one side.
  • Do not put heavy items at the back. Weight behind the axle lifts weight off the hitch, and a light tongue is what lets a trailer sway.
  • Secure everything inside. Loose items shift on the first corner. Tie down appliances and furniture if they can move.

The ratings behind all of this, what you can tow, what the trailer may weigh, and how the tongue weight is measured, are worked through one at a time in the towing capacity guide and the pin weight and payload guide. Read those before your first trip.

Sway, and what to do when it starts

Sway is the trailer swinging side to side behind you. It is caused by crosswinds, by the air pushed ahead of a passing vehicle, by poor weight distribution, or by simply driving too fast for the conditions.

If it starts, stay calm and do the opposite of what your hands want to do.

  • Ease off the accelerator gradually. Slowing down is what settles a sway.
  • Keep the steering still. Do not steer hard to correct it, because each steering input can feed the swing rather than stop it.
  • Do not brake hard. A hard stop on the tow vehicle can make a sway worse. If you need to slow further, apply the trailer brakes gently. That helps settle the trailer rather than straightening it, so make the correction small and wait for the sway to die down.
  • Stop somewhere safe once it has settled and work out the cause: the load, the speed, or the wind. Fix that before you carry on.

A weight-distribution hitch with a sway-control device does two jobs. The bars spread the tongue weight across the axles so the combination sits level, and the sway control damps the side to side movement. Both help, but neither one fixes a badly loaded trailer. A trailer with too little tongue weight will still misbehave no matter what is bolted to the hitch.

Brakes, and the controller in the cab

The trailer's brakes are electric, and they work alongside the tow vehicle's brakes rather than instead of them. Use them together. A trailer this size needs a controller mounted in the cab, and the controller has to be matched to the trailer's brake system.

  • Set the gain. The gain is how hard the trailer brakes at a given application. Set it so the trailer pulls its own weight under braking without locking its wheels. The full setting procedure is in the trailer brake guide.
  • Test it at walking pace. With the trailer connected and everything switched on, creep forward and squeeze the controller's manual override. You should feel the trailer pull back against the tow vehicle. If nothing happens, the trailer brakes are not working, and the tow vehicle is doing all of the braking.
  • Attach the breakaway cable. The breakaway switch on the trailer's frame is a safety system. If the trailer ever separates from the tow vehicle, the cable pulls a pin and the switch applies the trailer brakes. The cable has to be attached to a permanent part of the tow vehicle, and the switch needs a charged battery on the trailer to work at all.

Which trailers must have brakes, how to test the breakaway battery safely, and the detail behind setting the gain are all in the trailer brake guide.

Tires and bearings before you pull out

  • Check the tire pressure cold, against the pressure on the trailer's certification label rather than the number on the sidewall, and check the spare while you are down there.
  • Look at each tire. Cracks, bulges and uneven wear all matter. A trailer tire can look perfectly fine and still be too old to trust, which is why they are replaced by age rather than by tread depth. That rule, and how to read a tire's age, is in the tire guide.
  • Watch your speed. Check the speed rating molded on the sidewall, and do not assume yours is rated above 65 miles per hour. Pushing a loaded trailer past its rating is asking for trouble.
  • Re-check the lug nuts. Wheel lug nuts settle and loosen, so they are re-torqued after the first few short distances and then checked periodically. A loose wheel can leave the trailer.
  • Mind the bearings. Bearing wear shows up as wobble or uneven tire wear. Whether your axle bearings need grease or are sealed units that cannot be serviced, and how often they need attention, is set out in the trailer wheel bearing guide.

Driving with a trailer behind you

The first drive should not be the trip itself. Find an empty parking lot or a quiet road and practice there first: drive slowly, turn both ways, stop from low speed, and get the feel of how the trailer follows before you do any of it in traffic.

  • Turn wider than you think. The trailer cuts inside the path of the tow vehicle, so the rear of the trailer can run over a curb on a normal turn. Go further into the intersection before you begin the turn, and take it wide enough to clear.
  • Do not cut corners. Cut the corner in the tow vehicle and the trailer's wheels clip the curb.
  • Leave more room in front. The combination is heavier and takes longer to stop, so following distance has to grow.
  • Expect to be pushed by other traffic. A large vehicle passing is a moving wall of air against the side of the trailer. Grip the wheel, hold your line, and slow a little if it helps. The same goes for crosswinds, where slowing down is the best defense there is.
  • Keep the speed down. Speed makes sway worse.
  • Take the hills gently. On long climbs the combination will be slow, so use turnouts to let traffic past. On long descents, use a lower gear and let the engine hold the speed rather than riding the brakes, because overheated brakes fade and stop working when you need them most.

Some states require a towing vehicle to stay in the right hand lane. California's handbook, for instance, sets that requirement in law, so check the rules for the states you drive through.

Reversing, where the steering is backwards

Because the trailer pivots at the hitch, reversing is counterintuitive. You steer one way and the back of the trailer swings the other way. That is normal, and there is a trick that makes it click.

  • Put your hand at the bottom of the wheel. Hold the wheel at six o'clock. Now, whichever way you move your hand is the way the trailer goes. Move your hand left and the trailer backs to the left. Move it right and the trailer backs to the right.
  • Go slowly and steer in small amounts. The trailer does not react at once, so a quick corrective flick usually makes things worse and can jackknife the combination. Small inputs, made early, work.
  • Use a spotter. Agree hand signals before you start and keep the spotter in your mirror the whole time. If you cannot see the spotter, stop.
  • Plan to pull forward. If the trailer gets out of line, pulling forward and straightening up is a normal part of backing, not a failure. Reset and try again rather than fighting it.
  • Use your mirrors. A trailer this size blocks the view straight back, so set the mirrors before you leave and back in by them.

Arriving, and unhitching

Can injure youNothing gets unhooked until the trailer cannot roll. A trailer that is not chocked, or still resting on the ball, can move while you are behind it, and a tongue that comes off a jack drops fast. Parking brake on, wheels chocked, and stay clear of the tongue while it comes down.

Pick a site that is roughly level and back in slowly, checking for obstacles as you go. Before anything else, set the tow vehicle's parking brake and chock the trailer wheels. Nothing gets unhooked until the trailer cannot roll.

Then, in order:

  • Unplug the trailer harness, unhook the safety chains, and detach the breakaway cable. Stow all three out of the way.
  • Fit the dolly wheel or platform if the jack has one.
  • Release the weight-distribution bars.
  • Open the coupler latch.
  • Raise the tongue jack until the coupler lifts clear of the ball.
  • Pull the tow vehicle away.
  • Level the trailer front to back with the tongue jack.
  • Extend the slide-outs, then lower the stabilizer jacks. Stabilizers settle the trailer once it is level. They are not for lifting or leveling it.

Before you drive the tow vehicle away, look at what you are leaving: the wheels chocked, the tongue jack on a firm block, the weight bars off and stowed, the wiring and chains clear of the ground, and the trailer level. Then check the inside of the trailer before the next move, because anything left loose travels on the first corner.

The mistakes that damage things

Almost all of the expensive damage from a first trip comes from a small handful of things left in the wrong position.

  • Driving with the tongue jack down. A jack that is still extended will catch on the first speed bump, driveway or dip. It is retracted to its highest point as part of the hitching procedure, and that step is not optional.
  • Driving with the stabilizers down. Stabilizer jacks hold the trailer steady once it is parked, not while it travels. They are fully retracted before the trailer moves, or they bend and tear at the frame when they hit something.
  • Forgetting the breakaway cable. If it is not attached, the trailer has no emergency braking at all if it separates. Attach it to a permanent part of the tow vehicle every time. Never use the breakaway switch as a parking brake, because it is not built for that and the constant current can damage the wiring.
  • Forgetting the antenna. A television antenna left standing is the most common thing left up on a roof, and it is the last thing you see as you hear it snap on an overhanging branch or a low structure. It is one line on a pre-trip checklist for a reason. The full walkaround that catches it, and the other things that are easy to leave up or down, is in the pre-trip walkaround checklist.

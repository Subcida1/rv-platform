RV refrigerator not cooling: The complete troubleshooting guide

An RV fridge is not a house fridge. An absorption unit runs on heat, from propane or an electric element, and it behaves differently the moment the RV is tilted, the back of the unit is starved of air, or the day is hot. A 12-volt compressor unit works like the fridge at home and does not care about level. The two types fail in different ways, and knowing which one you have decides what you check first.

Last reviewed: Sep 23, 2026, against the sources listed below.

Stop here if you smell ammonia or see yellow staining. Either one means the sealed cooling unit has lost its charge, and a leaking absorption unit is a fire risk rather than a repair job. Turn the fridge off, run it on electric only if you must keep food cold, and have it inspected before you trust it again. No amount of leveling or vent clearing brings a leaked charge back.
The short version: first, which fridge do you have? An absorption unit runs on heat, so it must be level: level the RV, clear the rear vents, check the door seal, and confirm the burner or element is actually firing. A 12-volt compressor runs on the battery and does not care about level: check the battery voltage under load, not at rest. On an absorption unit, if the boiler at the back runs hot while the inside never cools, the charge is likely gone, and that is the expensive failure.

First, identify your RV refrigerator type

The door tag or the model number on the frame settles it, and so does the back of the unit. An absorption fridge has a visible rear vent on the outside wall and a sealed cooling unit behind it with no compressor. A 12-volt compressor fridge has a compressor, like the fridge at home, and no rear vent requirement. If you cannot tell, assume absorption first and work through the checks for it.

Absorption refrigerators: The RV classic

The absorption fridge uses a sealed ammonia and water solution and a flame or heating element to drive a cycle with no moving parts in the cooling unit itself. It runs on propane, on 120-volt shore power, and on some models 12-volt while driving. It must be level, needs good rear ventilation, and it is the type in the recall history.

12-volt compressor refrigerators: The modern alternative

A compressor unit pumps refrigerant mechanically, like the kitchen fridge, and runs on the house battery through a 12-volt DC supply. It does not care about level, does not need the same venting, and its most common failure is electrical: a low or sagging battery voltage that stops the compressor. Many owners moving toward boondocking install these to replace an aging absorption unit.

The troubleshooting and power math below are different for each, which is why identifying yours is not a formality.

Why an absorption RV fridge must stay level

An absorption fridge works by gravity. The ammonia solution flows downhill through the loop, and the burner heat drives the cycle. Park the RV noticeably tilted, and the flow stalls, which does two things: the fridge stops cooling properly, and the heat keeps pouring into a system that can no longer circulate it. If the RV is allowed to run that way repeatedly, the repeated overheating destroys the cooling unit from the inside out.

This is the safety angle that matters. Norcold publishes the working limit in its own manuals: an absorption unit is built to run within 3 degrees off level side to side and 6 degrees front to back, and past that the cooling system can be damaged. Dometic's instruction is to park the vehicle level whenever the fridge is running. The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration recalls for Dometic and Norcold refrigerators trace fires back to cooling units that developed a fatigue crack in the boiler tube and released pressurized, flammable cooling solution. Dometic's recall covers two-door units built from April 1997 through September 2006, and Norcold's covers the 1200, 1201, 1210 and 1211 models built through October 2010, by serial number. Running an absorption fridge off level is how a failed cooling unit turns into a fire instead of a repair job.

Check your model against the recalls. Find the exact brand and model number, usually on a tag inside the fridge door or on the frame, and search it against the current NHTSA recall list. Norcold's remedy for the 1200 series is a retrofit kit, part number 634737, fitted to the cooling unit. If your unit is on a recall list and was never reworked, that is the first thing to resolve, before any troubleshooting.

The free checks for an RV fridge that is not cooling

Level the RV

For an absorption unit, leveling is not optional. Level side to side and front to back with the blocks or stabilizers, then give it hours, not minutes: Norcold's manuals say to let the unit cool for eight hours before loading food, and Dometic's say the freezer compartment should be cold about an hour after switching on while the food compartment takes several hours. For a compressor unit, leveling barely matters, though an extreme tilt is still bad for any RV's operation.

Clear the rear vents and the cooling fins

The back of the fridge rejects heat through the roof and wall vents. If either is blocked by debris, or the fins are packed with dust, the unit cannot shed heat and never gets cold. Vacuum the fins and clear the outside vents of leaves, insect nests, and anything else that found its way in. Some of that debris is also a fire hazard if it collects near the burner.

Check the door seal

A weak seal lets warm air in continuously and makes the unit run harder than it should. The dollar-bill test works: close the door on a bill, tug it, it should hold, all the way around. Clean the gasket, and replace it if a corner is crushed or torn.

Confirm the heat source is actually firing

On propane, you should hear a steady soft hiss at the burner and see a steady blue flame through the lower vent. A yellow or flickering flame means a dirty burner orifice, and clearing it is the first thing to try. On electric, the element at the back of the unit should be warm to the touch once it is running; if the boiler stays cold on a mode that should be heating it, the element is the suspect, and Dometic's own fault table points at a defective heating element for a unit that will not cool on 12-volt or mains power. On a compressor unit, check the battery voltage under load, because a battery that reads fine at rest but sags when the compressor starts is the first thing to rule out.

The expensive failure: The ammonia cooling unit

Run the fridge for several hours, then carefully feel the back of the unit, it is hot in places, so use care. The boiler at the top should be hot. The absorber lower down the back should be warm. The evaporator, inside behind the rear wall of the food compartment, should be cold. If the boiler runs hot but the compartments never cool, the ammonia charge is likely gone.

A cracked or leaking cooling unit loses its ammonia, and no amount of leveling or vent clearing fixes it. The signs are the yellow sodium chromate residue that appears where the solution has leaked, or the sharp smell of ammonia near the unit. Either one means the cooling unit has failed, and a cooling unit is a sealed assembly with no serviceable parts inside, so it is replaced as a unit rather than repaired. That is the biggest bill this fridge can hand you. Do not leave a leaking absorption unit running; a leak is what turns a cooling unit failure into a fire.

RV fridge power draw: Why the mode matters

If your fridge cools but you are fighting your battery bank, the mode it runs in is the whole story. The three modes of an absorption fridge are not equal, and neither is an absorption fridge against a compressor fridge.

Two of those columns look like they disagree, and the gap between them is the duty cycle. A compressor unit rated at 5.6 amps does not draw 134 amp-hours a day, because the compressor runs part of the time and rests the rest. The daily figure is the running draw multiplied by the hours it actually runs, and how much it runs is set by the ambient temperature and how often the door opens, which is why the makers publish the running current rather than a daily total. The absorption element on shore power works the same way: a 135-watt element consuming 2.4 kilowatt-hours a day at 25 °C is running about three quarters of the time.

Against the absorption unit it usually replaces, a 2.8 cubic foot Dometic compressor fridge is rated at 5.6 amps on 12 volts, about 67 watts while the compressor runs, against a 135-watt element in a mid-size absorption unit that runs most of the time on shore power. That is the honest version of the claim that a compressor fridge uses a fraction of the energy: it is a smaller load, and it rests.

If your fridge is an absorption unit and keeps draining the house battery, the fix is not a bigger battery, it is switching the fridge to propane or shore power, whichever fits where you are parked.

The other suspects, in order

The thermistor and thermostat

The thermistor is the sensor that tells the cooling unit how hard to run. A thermistor that has slipped off its fin is a classic and easy fix, just reposition it. A faulty thermistor can be tested with a multimeter and replaced cheaply. On some units, the thermostat simply needs to be turned up before anything else.

High ambient heat

Absorption fridges are rated for a temperature band, and a hot day can push past it. Dometic rates its units to hold their cooling performance in ambient temperatures from 10 to 32 °C, which is about 50 to 90 °F, and for a spell above that it recommends fitting an extra fan on the back of the unit. Direct sun makes it worse: a fridge built in behind a sunlit window, or a closed-up RV parked in the sun, is on Dometic's own list of conditions where the unit will not hold perishable temperatures. Shade the RV and clear the vents. Do not judge the fridge by the thermostat number either, because Dometic's manuals say the temperature levels do not relate to absolute temperature values, so a box that reads warmer than the dial on a hot afternoon is not proof of a fault.

Over-packed or door left open

A packed fridge blocks the internal airflow that carries the cold around the box. Airflow inside matters as much as outside, so leave the shelves breathing, and check that the door is actually latching, not just swinging shut.

The rule that saves most owners

Most "my RV fridge is not cooling" cases begin as a leveling, ventilation, seal, or burner problem, and those are afternoon jobs. The lost ammonia charge is the one failure that no amount of work brings back, which is why it is worth ruling out first. Keep the RV level, keep the back clear, and check the model against the recalls, and you have covered the ground between a cold fridge and a serious one.

Why does my RV refrigerator need to be level but my house fridge does not?

An absorption RV fridge works on gravity, the ammonia solution flows downhill around a closed loop that the burner heat drives. Tilt the RV and the flow stalls, so the unit stops cooling and the heat keeps building. A compressor fridge pumps refrigerant mechanically and does not care about level.

Is an RV absorption refrigerator a fire hazard?

Operating one off-level for long periods and running one with a leaking cooling unit are the two real risks. The NHTSA recalls on Dometic and Norcold units traced fires to boiler tube cracks releasing flammable cooling solution. This is why the safety steps matter: keep it level, check your model against the recalls, and shut the unit down if you smell ammonia or see yellow residue at the back.

My RV fridge cools on propane but not electric. What does that mean?

The heat source is the difference. On propane the burner heats the boiler; on electric a heating element warms the same boiler. If propane works but electric does not, the electric element or its 120-volt supply is the fault, not the cooling unit.

Why does my RV fridge drain the house battery so fast?

Check the mode. An absorption fridge on 12-volt draws about 11 amps, because its 12-volt element is rated around 130 watts, and it can take a 100 amp-hour battery down to half charge in about four and a half hours; it is meant only for driving. On propane it draws nothing beyond the control board. A compressor fridge draws 5 to 6 amps while the compressor runs, and its daily total is that draw times the hours it actually runs, which is why it is the boondocking choice.

Should I replace my RV absorption fridge with a compressor fridge?

Many owners do, especially full-timers and boondockers, because a 12-volt compressor unit is a smaller electrical load than an absorption element and does not care about level. Dometic rates a 2.8 cubic foot compressor unit at 5.6 amps on 12 volts, about 67 watts while it runs, against a 135-watt element in a mid-size absorption unit. The trade is cost, and the installation often needs a heavier 12-volt wire run from the battery. It is the right upgrade when the old absorption cooling unit is the failure and you live off-grid.

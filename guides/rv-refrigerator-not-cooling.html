RV refrigerator not cooling: Absorption vs compressor

The first fact that saves you money: an RV fridge is not a house fridge. Most RVs use an absorption unit that runs on heat, propane or electric, and it behaves completely differently when the rig is tilted, hot, or vented wrong. Here is how to tell which fridge you have and fix the common things before anything expensive.

Last reviewed: Sep 18, 2026, against current absorption and compressor unit guidance. We update these guides when real-world data changes.

The short version: level the rig, clear the rear vents, confirm the burner or element is actually firing, and check the door seal. Those four solve most cases. If the boiler at the back is hot but the inside never cools, suspect the ammonia cooling unit, the expensive failure.

First, which fridge do you have?

Absorption (3-way). Runs on propane, 120-volt, or 12-volt, and uses a heat-driven ammonia cycle. This is the classic RV fridge with the rear vent on the outside wall. It must be reasonably level and needs good rear ventilation. It is the type that fails as cooling unit.

Compressor (12-volt / DC). Mechanically like the fridge at home, a compressor and refrigerant, sized for 12-volt so it runs off the house battery. It does not care about level and does not need the same vents. It cares about battery voltage, a low or sagging battery is the most common reason it stops cooling.

The maintenance and the fixes below are different for each, so identify yours first. The door tag or the model number will tell you, or the outside rear vent gives the absorption unit away.

The free checks for any RV fridge

1. Level the rig (absorption units especially)

An absorption fridge uses gravity to move the ammonia solution through the loop. If the rig is noticeably tilted, the cycle stalls and the fridge warms up. Level side to side and front to back with the blocks or stabilizers, then give it time, absorption units are slow, expect hours to reach temperature, not minutes.

2. Clear the rear vents

The back of the fridge rejects heat through the roof and wall vents. If either is covered, blocked by debris, or packed with dust on the cooling fins, the unit cannot shed heat and never gets cold. Vacuum the fins and confirm the outside vents are clear before suspecting anything else.

3. Check the door seal

A weak seal lets warm air in continuously. The dollar-bill test works: close the door on a bill, tug it, it should hold. Do it all the way around. Clean the gasket, and if a corner is crushed or torn, gaskets are replaceable.

4. Is the heat source actually on?

  • Propane mode: you should hear a steady soft hiss at the burner. Look behind the lower outside vent, the flame should be a steady blue. A yellow or flickering flame means a dirty burner orifice, the number-one owner fix, clean it and cooling returns.
  • Electric mode: the heating element behind the unit should be noticeably warm within 15 to 20 minutes. Cold after that, the element has failed.
  • 12-volt / compressor mode: check the battery voltage under load. A battery that reads fine resting but sags when the compressor starts will stop a DC fridge cold.

The diagnosis that finds the expensive failure

  • Run the fridge for several hours, then carefully feel the back of the unit (it is hot in places, use care):
  • The boiler at the top should be hot.
  • The absorber lower down the back should be warm.
  • The evaporator inside behind the rear wall of the food compartment should be cold. If the boiler is hot but the compartments never cool, the ammonia charge is likely gone. A cracked or leaking cooling unit loses the ammonia, and no amount of leveling or vent clearing fixes it. Yellow residue (sodium chromate, a corrosion inhibitor) or a sharp ammonia smell behind the fridge confirms a leak. A leaking absorption cooling unit is also a fire hazard, treat it seriously. The repair often costs as much as replacing the fridge, which is why many owners replace.

The other suspects, in order

  • Thermistor / thermostat. The sensor that tells the cooling unit how hard to run. A mispositioned thermistor (slipped off the fin) is a classic, reposition it. A faulty thermistor can be tested with a multimeter.
  • High ambient heat. Absorption fridges struggle in direct sun above about 95°F with the vents working properly. Shade the rig and clear the vents, this is physics, not a fault.
  • Over-packed or door left open. A packed fridge blocks internal air circulation. Airflow inside matters as much as outside.

The rule that saves most people

Ninety percent of "my RV fridge is not cooling" begins as a leveling, ventilation, seal, or burner problem, all fixable in an afternoon. The ammonia loss is the rare and expensive one, and you can recognize it before paying anyone to tell you.

Why does my RV refrigerator need to be level but my house fridge does not?

An absorption RV fridge works on gravity, the ammonia solution flows downhill around a closed loop that the burner heat drives. Tilt the rig and the flow stalls, so the unit stops cooling properly. A compressor fridge pumps refrigerant mechanically and does not care about level.

My RV fridge cools on propane but not electric. What does that mean?

The heat source is the difference. On propane the burner heats the boiler; on electric a heating element warms the same boiler. If propane works but electric does not, the electric element or its 120-volt supply is the fault, not the cooling unit itself.

How do I know if the ammonia cooling unit is bad?

The classic test: after hours of running, the top boiler is hot but the inside stays warm, and you may see yellow residue or smell ammonia behind the unit. Those two together mean a lost ammonia charge, a failed cooling unit that is expensive and, when leaking, a fire hazard.

Can a 12-volt compressor refrigerator run off my house battery?

Yes, that is the design, but it draws real current, so battery voltage and bank size matter. A healthy 12-volt bank runs it through the night; a weak or undersized one lets it stall. Check the voltage under load before replacing the fridge.

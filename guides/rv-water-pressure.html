RV Water Pressure: The Regulator, the Inlet and the Park Supply

Campground water can arrive at over 100 psi. An RV fresh water system is built for roughly half that. The regulator is the part that stands between the two, and it does exactly one thing: it reduces.

By OriginRV. Last reviewed on Oct 23, 2026.

The short version: a regulator only ever reduces pressure. Jabsco states it in its own specification: pressures from 46 to 125 psi are regulated down to 45 psi, and "lower pressures will pass through unregulated." No regulator raises pressure, so low flow at the tap is never a regulator problem. Fit one before the hose, not after it.

What the makers publish

Four makers publish a target, and they agree closely. Winnebago's owner's manual recommends "regulators that control water pressure to 50 psi. max." Camco's fixed regulator is preset to 40 to 50 PSI according to its own product copy, and its adjustable unit ships at 45 psi with settings above 60 psi not recommended, a figure that appears in distributor listings rather than on Camco's own pages. Jabsco regulates 46 to 125 psi down to 45 psi, with a stated variance of plus or minus 5 psi. Blu Technology presets at 45 PSI and recommends 40 to 60 PSI.

Camco's own guidance describes the range differently from the way a preset might suggest. Its expert article says that "many standard, non-adjustable regulators are preset to 40-50 PSI to be safe. But, most RV plumbing systems can comfortably handle 55-60 PSI." That is the case for an adjustable unit: a fixed regulator protects the system at the low end of the range, and an adjustable one lets a reader sit higher in it.

Jabsco's regulator brochure gives the supply side of the same picture, describing unregulated city water pressures that "can run as high as 125 psi".

The tubing is not the weak point

Fresh water lines in an RV are usually PEX, and PEX is rated far above anything a campground delivers. Viega publishes a design rating of 160 psi at 73 degrees Fahrenheit and a minimum burst pressure per ASTM F876 and F877 of 480 psi for half-inch tubing at the same temperature. Rovanco publishes the same design rating, 160 psi at 73.4 degrees Fahrenheit, and does not publish a burst figure; Wavin publishes the design rating and the same burst table.

So the 50 psi recommendation is not about the tube. It is about everything attached to it: the fittings, the city water inlet, the check valve, the water heater connections and the appliance inlets. A system can hold 160 psi in the straight runs and still lose a fitting, because the tube rating says nothing about the parts screwed onto its ends.

What a regulator cannot do

Jabsco's specification settles the most common misunderstanding in one line. Its regulator acts on pressures from 46 to 125 psi, and pressures below 46 psi "will pass through unregulated." A regulator is a restriction with a set point. Below that set point it is not doing anything, and it has no mechanism to add pressure.

The practical consequence: if the water is weak at the tap, the regulator is not the cause and replacing it will not help. Look at the supply, the hose, the filter and the inlet screen instead.

The regulator has a ceiling of its own

Camco rates its regulators to a maximum incoming pressure of about 125 psi, and states that above it performance may be reduced or the body damaged. A regulator is protection against the range a campground normally delivers, not a guarantee against any supply. A park known for extreme pressure is a reason to check with a gauge rather than to trust the regulator alone.

The city water inlet, and the check valve inside it

The inlet is where the hose connects, and it carries a check valve. Jabsco describes the backflow preventer as "federal and state required on RV's with water service hookup," so the valve is not an accessory. It is the reason water does not run back out of the inlet when the pump is running and no hose is connected.

Four failures account for most inlet trouble. The check valve leaks, so the fresh tank slowly fills while you are on city water, or water runs out of the inlet when the pump runs. The valve sticks shut, so no water enters at all. The inlet screen tears, letting grit into the check valve and the regulator. The cap is lost, and the inlet fills with road spray.

Gravity fill and city fill are two different paths

A gravity fill is a large open port, usually on the side of the coach, that fills the fresh tank by pouring. A city fill connects to the pressurized supply and can either run the coach directly or, through a diverter, fill the tank.

The city fill bypasses the pump. That is why a pump fault and a city water fault can look identical at the tap and why the two are worth separating before any part is replaced. If the tap is weak on city water and strong on the pump, the pump is fine and the problem is upstream of the inlet. If it is weak on both, the restriction is downstream of both.

Checking the supply

A pressure gauge on the spigot before the hose answers the question the regulator is there to answer. Camco sells a regulator with a color-coded gauge for exactly this, and the reading tells you whether the park is delivering within the range your system was built for or well above it.

What this does not cover

The pump side has its own guide: RV water pump won't prime covers the tank, the suction line and the strainer. This page is the city water side, which is a different system with a different fault path.

RV toilet not flushing: The pedal, the seal, and the water

Three things go wrong with an RV toilet, they have three different answers, and only one of them is at the water supply. Separating them takes a watch and a bucket, and it is the difference between replacing a seal and replacing a pump.

Last reviewed: Sep 24, 2026.

The short version: a bowl that will not hold water is the blade seal, and the maker's first step is debris in the seal track. A flush that is weak or missing is usually a pedal that was not held fully open, because the manual expects a good flush within two to three seconds. A pedal that has gone stiff wants silicone lubricant and nothing else. Work them in that order and the water supply is the last suspect rather than the first.

Start here: Which of the three is it

The symptoms sound alike and are not. Before touching anything, decide which of these three you have, because the repair, the part and the cost are all different. The procedure and the figures below are Thetford's, from the owner's manual for its permanent RV toilets.

Rule out the tank and the vent first

A full black tank gives the same weak or missing flush as a starved toilet, so drain it and try again before any part is touched. A blocked roof vent is the other impostor: with the tank unable to breathe, the bowl drains slowly, gurgles, or pushes back. Neither one is a seal or a pedal fault, and both cost nothing to check.

It flushes but will not hold water

Water runs, the bowl fills, and then it drains away instead of standing. That is the blade seal, and it has nothing to do with the water supply. Thetford's own order for it is in the manual, and it runs cheapest first: debris in the seal track, then seal compression, then replacement.

It flushes weakly, or not at all

No water, or a trickle that never clears the bowl. The maker's first instruction is not about a part: Pedals or hand levers must be held fully open during flush. A pedal held half way gives a weak flush on a toilet that is working perfectly, which is why the manual puts that sentence before anything else. If the flush is still poor with the pedal held all the way, the next step is a measurement rather than a part.

The pedal has gone stiff

It moves, but it takes more effort than it used to. That is lubrication, and the manual names what to use and what not to.

The seal, and the maker's own order for it

Debris in the seal track comes first

A blade seal closes against a track, and anything caught in that track holds the blade off its seat. The manual's first instruction for a toilet that will not hold water is to check for and remove any debris from blade seal track. In practice that means opening the blade and looking at the channel it seats into, with the water supply off and a torch. It costs nothing, it takes a minute, and it is the cause the manual lists first.

Then seal compression, then replacement

If the track is clean and the bowl still drains, the second check is blade seal compression with mechanism: whether the blade is being pressed against the seal hard enough as it closes. Then the third, which is the one that costs money: if the blade seal is worn, replace it. A seal is a consumable, and its life is decided by how much the toilet is used and how much grit has gone through it.

One thing worth knowing before you start: anything you open at the toilet is a sewage path. Gloves, a container for the part, and somewhere to wash are part of the job rather than an afterthought, and the water supply goes off first.

The water side

Hold the pedal open, then time the flush

Two numbers come out of the maker's manual and both are useful. The first is the requirement: the pedal or lever must be held fully open during the flush. The second is the expectation: a good flush should be obtained within 2 to 3 seconds. Timing it turns a vague complaint into a measurement, and it separates a pedal problem from a supply problem in one flush.

The flow rate to test against

If the flush is still poor with the pedal held fully open, the manual's next step is a measurement at the supply line. Switch the water pump off at its switch and open a tap to bleed the pressure out of the system before the line is disconnected; a pressurised line sprays when it is opened, and the fittings at a toilet sit in the same space as 12-volt wiring. With the pressure off, remove the water supply line and check flow rate, and the manual gives the figure to test against: the flow rate should be at least ten quarts, or 9.5 litres, per minute. That is a bucket and a watch. Anything below it means the toilet is being starved rather than failing, and the fault is upstream: a kinked line, a partly closed valve, a clogged inlet screen, or a pump that is not delivering what it should. The battery page is not the place for the pump, but the pump's own supply is a 12-volt question, and the pressure side is in the manuals section under water and plumbing.

The water valve, the vacuum breaker and the water module

When the flow rate is fine and the toilet still misbehaves, the parts the same manual names are these, and knowing which one does what saves buying the wrong one. The water valve is what the pedal opens to let water in. The vacuum breaker is the anti-siphon device that stops bowl water being drawn back into the fresh supply, and it is the part that leaks when it fails. The water module is the assembly those parts sit in on some models, which is why the maker's instruction for a failed vacuum breaker is to replace it or the water module, depending on the model.

If it leaks instead of failing to flush

The same troubleshooting section lists three leaks, and each has its own first step. A leak at the back of the toilet is the supply line connection at the water valve: resecure or retighten it, and if the valve itself keeps leaking, replace the valve. A leak from the vacuum breaker while flushing means replacing the breaker, or the module with it. A leak between the floor flange and the toilet is the flange nuts first, then the flange height, which the maker's spacers set to 7/16 inch above the floor, and then the flange seal.

Lubrication, and the one rule that matters

A pedal or hand lever that is harder to operate than normal takes a light film of Thetford drain valve lubricant or silicone spray applied to the blade or the ball. The manual attaches a warning to that instruction, and it is the reason this is its own section rather than a line in a list: to avoid damage, do not use spray lubricants other than silicone. Petroleum-based sprays attack the seals and the plastic they are meant to protect, so the wrong can in the toolbox turns a stiff pedal into a replacement job.

Two notes on doing it properly. Apply it to the blade or ball with the toilet dry rather than into a bowl of water, and work the mechanism through a few cycles so the film reaches the whole track. If the pedal is stuck rather than stiff, that is a different fault with a different cause, and lubricant will not fix a mechanism that has jammed or a linkage that has come apart.

What it costs

The parts here are small and the labour is what moves the number, because a toilet is a compact assembly in a tight space, and its position means the job is done kneeling in a bathroom rather than standing at a bench.

At the bottom is the work that costs nothing but time: a pedal held fully open, a seal track cleaned out, a supply line retightened, a lubricant applied. Two of the three faults on this page are fixed at this end, which is why the page puts those first.

In the middle are the parts: a blade seal, a water valve, a vacuum breaker. These are inexpensive and they are replaced rather than repaired, so the deciding factor is access rather than the price of the part.

At the top is anything that means taking the toilet out: a flange problem, a water module that has to come out with the bowl, or a floor that has been getting wet. That is when the job changes, and it is also the argument for dealing with a slow leak at the back of the toilet rather than wiping it up each time.

Why will my RV toilet not flush?

Check the pedal before anything else. Thetford's manual says pedals and hand levers must be held fully open during the flush, and that a good flush should be obtained within 2 to 3 seconds. If the flush is still poor, the next step the manual gives is to turn the water pump off and open a tap to take the pressure off first, then remove the water supply line and check the flow rate, which should be at least ten quarts, or 9.5 litres, per minute.

Why will my RV toilet not hold water?

That is the blade seal rather than the water supply, and Thetford's manual gives the order to work in: first check for and remove any debris from the blade seal track, then check the blade seal compression with the mechanism, and replace the seal if it is worn. Debris in the track is the first thing to look for and the cheapest thing to fix.

Why is my RV toilet pedal hard to push?

Thetford's remedy is a light film of its drain valve lubricant or a silicone spray applied to the blade or the ball. Its manual attaches a warning to that instruction: do not use spray lubricants other than silicone, because the others damage the mechanism. A pedal that is stiff rather than stuck is a lubrication job, and it is the cheapest fix on the toilet.

How much water should an RV toilet use per flush?

Thetford measures this as a flow rate rather than a volume per flush: with the water supply line removed, the flow rate should be at least ten quarts, or 9.5 litres, per minute. A flush that takes longer than the two to three seconds the manual calls for, or one that never builds enough water to clear the bowl, is a flow problem rather than a seal problem.

When to replace RV tires: The age rule that saves your trip

RV tires age by the calendar, not the odometer. A trailer tire can look brand new and be structurally done, because the rubber degrades from sun, heat, and time whether you drive 500 or 5,000 miles a year. Here is how to read your tires' real age, when to replace them, and what a blowout at 65 mph costs.

Last reviewed: Sep 18, 2026, against NHTSA tire-aging guidance and Goodyear's service bulletin. We update these guides when real-world data changes.

The short version: replace any RV tire six years from its date in service, or six years from the manufacture date on the DOT code if you do not know when it was mounted. At five years, have a tire professional inspect them. Never buy used RV tires, the service history is unknowable.

Read the real age: The DOT date code

  • Look on the sidewall for the letters DOT, then a longer code. The last four digits are the date of manufacture: week, then year.
  • A code ending in 2619 means the 26th week of 2019, so the tire was made in the middle of 2019.
  • A code ending in 0924 means the 9th week of 2024, early 2024. Check every tire, including the spare and including both sides of the trailer. RV tires are often replaced one or two at a time, so the dates can differ wildly across the same set. A tire that "looks new" may have sat in a warehouse for two years before it ever went on the rig, that time counts.

The replacement rule

  • Six years in service: replace, even if the tread looks fine. This is the rule Goodyear states in its tire service bulletin and NHTSA references for aging risk.
  • Six years from manufacture: if you cannot prove when the tire was first mounted, the DOT date is the clock, replace at six from that date.
  • Five years: have a qualified tire technician inspect for sidewall cracking, belt separation, and rubber degradation.
  • Never buy used RV tires. The service, storage, and load history of used tires is unknown, and a tire that has been overheated once can fail regardless of appearance.

The visual checks before every trip

  • Sidewall cracking. Small radial cracks along the sidewall, check the inner face too, the sun reaches it differently and cracks on the inside are easy to miss.
  • Bulges or blisters. A bubble in the sidewall is internal belt damage, the tire can fail without warning.
  • Dry rot or "crazing." A spiderweb of fine cracks means the rubber compound is breaking down.
  • Belt separation. A ripple or waviness in the tread area can mean the belts are separating internally.
  • Pressure. Tires lose about 1 psi for every 10°F the temperature drops. Check cold pressure, and fill to the sidewall-cold spec or the trailer's inflation table, not the maximum number on the sidewall.

Why age beats tread for RVs

A passenger car wears its tread out in 40,000 to 60,000 miles long before rubber age becomes the risk. An RV often accumulates fewer miles than the rubber's useful life, so the tires age out first. UV light, heat cycling, and time harden the rubber compound and break down the internal structure, which is why the DOT date, not the tread depth, is the number that matters.

The one check that gets skipped

The spare. It sits mounted for years, ages exactly like the running tires, and is the first thing people trust in an emergency. Same DOT rule applies, date it and replace it on the same six-year clock.

How do I read a DOT tire date code?

The last four digits after DOT are the week and year of manufacture: WWYY. 2619 = the 26th week of 2019. If the code has only three digits it was made before the year 2000, and at that age you replace it, no question.

Can an RV tire be too old even with perfect tread?

Yes, and this is the core of RV tire safety. Age degrades the rubber regardless of tread. The industry guidance, per NHTSA-adjacent aging research and Goodyear's bulletin, is replacement at six years from service or manufacture.

Do trailer tires and motorhome tires age differently?

Same physics, same calendar degradation. Trailer tires can run hotter under heavy load, and steer tires on a motorhome carry the most risk if they fail at highway speed. The six-year clock applies to both.

Is over-inflating tires for long storage a good idea?

Some owners add about 10 psi for storage to reduce sidewall flat-spotting, then drop back to spec before driving. It is a storage-only practice, never drive on the over-pressured figure, and it is secondary to getting the weight off the tires with jacks or moving the rig periodically.

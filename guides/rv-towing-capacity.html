How much can I tow? The honest math

The number on the brochure and the number you are actually safe to pull are different, and the gap is where people end up overloaded. This guide explains the four ratings that decide the real answer, why each one matters as a matter of physics, and then hands you the tool that does the whole calculation on your own numbers. By the end you will know exactly which of your limits you are closest to, and what happens if you cross it.

Last reviewed: Sep 21, 2026, against the sources listed below.

The short version: your truck's towing rating is only one of four limits, and it is rarely the one you hit first. Payload, the trailer's GVWR, and gross combined weight matter just as much, and each one that you cross makes the RV measurably less safe. The 80% rule keeps you clear of all of them on real roads. Run our RV towing weight calculator with your own numbers and it shows you, in plain words, which limit you are near and what it means.

The four ratings that decide whether you can tow it

1. Towing capacity: The number everyone quotes

This is the maximum loaded trailer weight your truck is rated to pull, measured by the manufacturer under the SAE J2807 standard: a controlled test track, ideal conditions, no wind, no steep grades. It assumes a bare trailer and little load in the truck. Find your number in the owner's manual towing table for your exact cab, engine, axle ratio, and four-wheel-drive setup, never the television commercial. It is not usually printed on the door, which is why most people misquote it.

2. Payload capacity: The one that bites first

Payload is how much weight your truck itself may carry: people, gear, fuel, and the tongue weight pressing down on the hitch. It lives on the yellow and white sticker inside the driver's door, the one that says occupants and cargo should never exceed a specific number. Most trucks hit payload long before towing capacity, which is why a half-ton can promise 10,000 pounds of towing on paper yet the sticker allows nowhere near that once four people and a dog are in the cab. Tongue weight counts against payload, and too much of it unweights the front axle, which is the axle that steers.

3. Trailer GVWR: The heaviest your trailer may ever be

Gross Vehicle Weight Rating is the maximum a loaded trailer may weigh, printed on its own sticker, usually the front left or inside a cabinet. Dry weight, unloaded vehicle weight, is what it left the factory at, before water, propane, bikes, and gear. A dry weight of 5,500 pounds can be 6,500 loaded before you see a campground. You compare your loaded trailer weight to the trailer's GVWR, not to the dry weight, because an overloaded trailer is what overheats tires and bends axles.

4. Gross Combined Weight Rating: The binding ceiling for the whole RV

GCWR is the most the truck and trailer together may weigh, everything, truck, people, fuel, trailer, cargo, all of it. It lives in the owner's manual towing table. Your truck can be under its own GVWR, your trailer under its GVWR, and the combination still over GCWR, and that is, in every meaningful way, a no. GCWR is the number that protects the drivetrain, the transmission, and the brakes on a downhill grade, which is rarely where people think about it.

What actually happens when you cross a limit

These four numbers are not paperwork, they are engineering limits, and each one you cross takes the RV measurably closer to a specific failure.

Exceed payload or tongue weight and you lift the front axle. The rear suspension squats, the headlights point skyward, and the front tires, the ones that steer, lose their contact patch. Steering gets vague, and at 65 miles an hour with a crosswind, vague steering on a loaded RV is how a lane-change becomes a ditch. The trade association numbers say an overloaded truck can add 25 to 50 percent to its stopping distance, several car lengths you do not have.

Overload the tires and you build heat. Every tire has a load index, and exceed it and the sidewall flexes harder, generating internal heat that weakens the rubber from the inside out. NHTSA links tire-related crashes to more than 600 highway fatalities a year in the United States, and a trailer or rear-axle blowout at speed is one of the fastest ways to start the swaying that turns into a jackknife. This is the failure the tire guides on this site exist to prevent.

Exceed gross combined weight and you cook the brakes. On a long mountain descent, more mass means more kinetic energy the brakes must convert to heat. Past the design limit, the pads and rotors get so hot they lose friction, the brake fluid can boil, and the pedal goes soft. An RV with no effective brakes and tons of momentum on a 6 percent grade is not a breakdown. It is a rescue. The mechanical stresses compound: engine overheat, transmission wear, suspension strain, frame flex, all at once.

And the insurance note no one likes. If an overweight RV causes a crash, an insurer that investigates can deny the claim because the RV was outside its rated limits. The overload is documented on the scale ticket and in the owner's manual. Overriding your ratings is not a gray area, it is a coverage decision made before you leave the driveway.

The 80% rule: Margin is what real roads remove

Ratings are measured on a flat test track with no wind, sea level, ideal conditions. Real towing has mountain grades, gust fronts, crosswinds, panic braking, and the temperature swing that robs engine power at altitude. The 80% rule is the margin that turns a laboratory number into a safe road number: keep the loaded trailer at or under 80 percent of rated towing capacity, and apply the same conservative thinking to payload and combined weight. It is not law, it is the difference between an RV that feels planted and one that is at the edge of its design on the first long grade. The towing calculator flags anything over 80 percent as yellow for exactly this reason.

Can I really tow the towing capacity number on the brochure?

Not safely in real conditions, no. That figure assumes ideal road, no wind, no load in the truck, and a bare trailer. The 80% rule covers the difference between the test track and real roads. Work from the loaded weight, not the brochure, which is the whole point of the towing calculator.

Why does payload matter if my truck has a huge towing rating?

Because the tongue weight sits on the truck, and payload is your truck's ceiling for everything on and in it. A big towing rating does not add payload. Exceed payload and the rear squats, the front axle loses steering grip, and braking distance grows, which is a safety problem, not an inconvenience.

What is the difference between GVWR and towing capacity?

GVWR is the maximum a single vehicle, truck or trailer, may weigh when loaded and ready. Towing capacity is the maximum trailer weight the truck is rated to pull. They are different limits, and you must respect all of them at once, which is exactly what the calculator checks together.

Is the 80% rule the law?

No, it is a widely used safety guideline, not a regulation. The legally binding limits are the manufacturer ratings. But every experienced tower treats 80% as the practical maximum because it preserves the margin real roads remove, and because an overweight RV can void insurance coverage in a crash.

Why does the calculator refuse to answer until I fill in four fields?

Because a partial answer is a false answer. If only the towing rating is entered, showing a verdict would pretend the payload, trailer, and people figures do not matter, when they are often the limits that bite first. The calculator waits until the core numbers exist, then tells the truth.

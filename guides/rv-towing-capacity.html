How much can I tow? The honest math

The number on the brochure and the number you are actually safe to pull are different, and the gap is where people end up overloaded. Four ratings decide the real answer. Each one matters as a matter of physics, and the calculator does the whole calculation on your own numbers. By the end you will know exactly which of your limits you are closest to, and what happens if you cross it.

Last reviewed: Sep 23, 2026, against the sources listed below.

The short version: your truck's towing rating is only one of four limits, and it is rarely the one you hit first. Payload, the trailer's GVWR, and gross combined weight matter just as much, and each one that you cross makes the RV measurably less safe. The 80% rule keeps you clear of all of them on real roads. Run our RV towing weight calculator with your own numbers and it shows you, in plain words, which limit you are near and what it means.

The four ratings that decide whether you can tow it

1. Towing capacity: The number everyone quotes

This is the maximum loaded trailer weight your truck is rated to pull, and under the SAE J2807 standard it is not a brochure number: a manufacturer rating to that standard has to demonstrate it. The standard sets minimum performance for acceleration, gradeability, trailer sway, braking and parking-brake hold, and hitch structure. The gradeability test is not a flat track. The combination climbs Arizona SR 68 to Union Pass, 11.4 miles, at 100 degrees or hotter, with the air conditioning on maximum, and a single-rear-wheel truck has to hold at least 40 miles an hour the whole way up. Other tests launch it five times from a standstill up a 12 percent grade, and measure its stopping distance. The standard also assumes a 150 pound driver and a 150 pound passenger, up to 70 pounds of aftermarket hitch, and a test trailer of a defined frontal area. Your truck, loaded the way you load it, is not that truck. Find your number in the owner's manual towing table for your exact cab, engine, axle ratio, and four-wheel-drive setup, never the television commercial. It is not printed on the door, which is why it gets misquoted.

2. Payload capacity: The one that bites first

Payload is how much weight your truck itself may carry on top of its own curb weight: people, gear, hitch hardware, and the tongue weight pressing down on the hitch. Fuel is not on that list, and that catches people out, because a full tank is already inside the curb weight the rating was worked out from. Subtracting it again takes 150 to 250 pounds off a number that was never yours to spend. It lives on the yellow and white sticker inside the driver's door, the one that says occupants and cargo should never exceed a specific number. That placard is federal law, required by FMVSS 110 on any vehicle up to 10,000 pounds GVWR, which is why it sits in the same place in almost every truck a travel trailer gets hitched to. Above 10,000 pounds that standard stops applying, so on a one-ton truck the placard is the maker's own label rather than a regulated one, and it is still the number that governs. Payload is the limit that arrives first, which is why a half-ton can promise 10,000 pounds of towing on paper while the sticker allows nowhere near that once four people and a dog are in the cab. The weight-distributing hitch counts too, and it is not small: the head and shank on a properly set up RV run 75 to 100 pounds, bolted to the receiver, every ounce of it against payload. Tongue weight counts against payload, and too much of it unweights the front axle, which is the axle that steers.

3. Trailer GVWR: The heaviest your trailer may ever be

Gross Vehicle Weight Rating is the maximum a loaded trailer may weigh, printed on its own sticker, usually the front left or inside a cabinet. Dry weight, unloaded vehicle weight, is what it left the factory at, before water, propane, bikes, and gear. A dry weight of 5,500 pounds can be 6,500 loaded before you see a campground. You compare your loaded trailer weight to the trailer's GVWR, not to the dry weight, because an overloaded trailer is what overheats tires and bends axles.

4. Gross Combined Weight Rating: The binding ceiling for the whole RV

GCWR is the most the truck and trailer together may weigh, everything, truck, people, fuel, trailer, cargo, all of it. It lives in the owner's manual towing table. Your truck can be under its own GVWR, your trailer under its GVWR, and the combination still over GCWR, and that is, in every meaningful way, a no. GCWR is the number that protects the drivetrain, the transmission, and the brakes on a downhill grade, which is rarely where people think about it.

What actually happens when you cross a limit

These four numbers are not paperwork, they are engineering limits, and each one you cross takes the RV measurably closer to a specific failure.

Exceed payload or tongue weight and you lift the front axle. The rear suspension squats, the headlights point skyward, and the front tires, the ones that steer, lose their contact patch. Steering gets vague, and at 65 miles an hour with a crosswind, vague steering on a loaded RV is how a lane-change becomes a ditch. More weight also means more energy the brakes have to shed, which is the failure described two paragraphs down.

Overload the tires and you build heat. Every tire has a load index, and a tire carrying more than its rating runs hotter. Heat is what does the damage: NHTSA's own tire guidance is that sustained high temperature can cause a tire to deteriorate, which leads to blowouts and tread separation. NHTSA counts 511 people killed in tire-related crashes in the United States in 2024, and a trailer or rear-axle blowout at speed is one of the fastest ways to start the swaying that turns into a jackknife.

Exceed gross combined weight and you cook the brakes. On a long mountain descent, more mass means more kinetic energy the brakes must convert to heat. Past the design limit, the pads and rotors get so hot they lose friction, the brake fluid can boil, and the pedal goes soft. An RV with no effective brakes and tons of momentum on a 6 percent grade is not a breakdown. It is a rescue. The mechanical stresses compound: engine overheat, transmission wear, suspension strain, frame flex, all at once.

And the brakes that matter are not all on the truck. A trailer heavy enough to need brakes has to have its own, working through a controller in the cab. The federal rule that set this benchmark governs commercial vehicles, not your RV, but it is worth knowing what it says about the worst case: trailer brakes have to apply automatically and immediately if the trailer breaks away, and stay applied for at least 15 minutes. What actually binds a personal RV is state equipment law, and the thresholds differ by state rather than sitting at one number: California requires brakes on a trailer coach at 1,500 pounds, Washington draws its line at 3,000 pounds, and Oregon requires none at all. The 3,000 pound figure people repeat comes from a federal exemption written for commercial vehicles, not from a national rule. A combination can be inside every rating on this page and still be overloaded in the way that matters on a descent, because the controller is set too light or the trailer brakes have not been adjusted in years. The check takes ten seconds: at walking pace, squeeze the manual override and see whether the trailer pulls back. If nothing happens, the truck is doing all the work.

And the insurance note no one likes. There is no rule that an overweight RV is automatically an uninsured one, and there is no rule that it is covered either: exclusions vary by state and by policy, so read yours rather than arguing from a forum post. What overloading does is hand everyone else in the crash a story about how you loaded the RV: the scale ticket and the owner's manual are already in evidence, and you are the one who has to explain the number on the sticker. What is not in question is that overriding your ratings is a decision you make in the driveway, not one that gets made for you afterwards.

The 80% rule: Margin is what real roads remove

Now look at what that rating actually is. The test is demanding, but it is still one test: one trailer of a defined size, one grade, one day, a professional driver, no traffic, and a pass line the vehicle only has to meet. It tells you the point at which the combination is just good enough. It knows nothing about the gust front that meets you sideways on an open bridge, the panic stop when someone cuts in, the load that shifts because a strap stretched, or the second and a half it takes you to react. A rating is also a ceiling rather than a target: at exactly 100 percent you have nothing left for any of that.

The 80 percent rule is not in the standard and it is not law. It is a convention, and a reasonable one, because it puts margin back for the things a test cannot measure. Keep the loaded trailer at or under 80 percent of rated towing capacity, and apply the same thinking to payload and combined weight. The towing calculator flags anything over 80 percent as yellow for exactly this reason.

Use the calculator: The answer on your numbers

Reading about the four limits is how you understand towing. Entering your own numbers is how you know. The RV towing weight calculator takes the ratings off your stickers and the loads out of your RV, and runs five checks at once: towing capacity, truck payload, the trailer against its own GVWR, the loaded truck against its GVWR, and the two of them together against the GCWR. The one number no sticker can give you is your actual loaded weight, and that comes from a CAT scale at a truck stop. It answers in plain words, safe, careful, or not safe, and it refuses to give an answer until the four core numbers are in, because a partial answer is a false answer.

A worked example, run through the math this guide teaches: a truck rated to tow 9,200 pounds with a 1,500 pound payload, and a trailer with a 5,600 pound dry weight and a 7,000 pound GVWR. Add 30 gallons of water, which is 250 pounds, a 30 pound propane cylinder, which is about 43 pounds on a scale because the steel counts too, 500 pounds of cargo, and two people and their gear, counted here at 400 pounds. The calculator returns a loaded trailer of 6,393 pounds, 69 percent of towing capacity, payload at 1,167 of 1,500, and a hitch weight of 767 pounds, which is that trailer weight at 12 percent tongue weight. Every constant is on the page so you can check the arithmetic, and your numbers will be different, which is why you run it.

Where every number lives

  • Towing capacity and GCWR: the owner's manual towing table for your exact cab, engine, and axle, or the maker's towing guide.
  • Payload and truck GVWR: the yellow and white sticker inside the driver's door.
  • Trailer dry weight and GVWR: the sticker on the trailer, front left or inside a cabinet.
  • What it all adds up to loaded: a certified scale. Find one near you, weigh the RV as you actually travel, and the printout settles every estimate on this page and in the calculator.

Can I really tow the towing capacity number on the brochure?

Not as a target, no. The rating is a pass line a truck has to meet on one defined test, with a defined trailer and two 150 pound people in the cab. It is a ceiling, not a comfortable working weight, and it assumes a truck that is not carrying your family, your water and your gear. The 80% rule is the margin that covers the difference. Work from the loaded weight, not the brochure, which is the whole point of the towing calculator.

Why does payload matter if my truck has a huge towing rating?

Because the tongue weight sits on the truck, and payload is your truck's ceiling for everything on and in it. A big towing rating does not add payload. Exceed payload and the rear squats, the front axle loses steering grip, and braking distance grows, which is a safety problem, not an inconvenience.

What is the difference between GVWR and towing capacity?

GVWR is the maximum a single vehicle, truck or trailer, may weigh when loaded and ready. Towing capacity is the maximum trailer weight the truck is rated to pull. They are different limits, and you must respect all of them at once, which is exactly what the calculator checks together.

Is the 80% rule the law?

No. It is a widely used convention, and you will not find it in the standard the ratings are built on. The legally binding limits are the manufacturer ratings, and they are what you are required to respect. The 80 percent figure is a way of holding margin back for wind, panic braking, load shift and reaction time, none of which a test can measure. It is advice worth taking, but it is advice.

Why does the calculator refuse to answer until I fill in four fields?

Because a partial answer is a false answer. If only the towing rating is entered, showing a verdict would pretend the payload, trailer, and people figures do not matter, when they are often the limits that bite first. The calculator waits until the core numbers exist, then tells the truth.

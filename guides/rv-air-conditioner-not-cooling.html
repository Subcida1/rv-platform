RV air conditioner not cooling: The troubleshooting guide

A roof air conditioner that runs and blows air but never gets cold is usually losing its cooling to one of three things: air that is not moving through the coil, a divider that lets cold air back into the intake, or a coil that is frozen or blocked. One temperature reading, taken at the return and at the supply, tells you whether you are looking at airflow or at the sealed system, and it takes ten minutes.

By OriginRV. Last reviewed on Oct 29, 2026.

The short version: measure the air temperature at the return and at the supply with the fan on high, after the unit has run at least half an hour on a warm day. Airxcel publishes 16 to 22°F as the drop for its units. Below that, check the filter, the evaporator coil and the divider between the return and the supply before you suspect the sealed system.

Start here: Match your symptom to its fault

Runs, blows air, but the air is not cold

The largest group. The temperature drop is what separates them. A drop inside the maker's range while the RV still will not cool is a heat-gain or air-distribution problem: the unit is doing its job and the vehicle is gaining more heat than the unit removes. A drop below the range points at the coil or the sealed system.

Nothing happens at all

No fan, no hum. That is a supply or control problem, not a cooling one. Check the fuse or circuit breaker at the panel, then the selector switch or thermostat that is supposed to be calling for cool. Nothing in the refrigeration side can make a unit completely dead, and Dometic's own service guidance says the same: check the generator or shore power, the line sizing, and the fuse or breaker before calling anyone.

Cold for a while, then warms up

Classically a coil that has iced over. Once ice covers the evaporator, air cannot pass and the cooling stops even though the unit is still running. Restricted airflow is what causes it, so the filter, the return-air path and the divider are the places to look. Airxcel notes that restricted airflow over the evaporator can also produce a temperature drop greater than normal, which reads like a healthy unit and is not.

Ice visible on the coil

The same cause, further along. Airxcel states that a clogged filter will produce a loss of air volume and may eventually ice up the cooling coil. Airxcel also publishes a maintenance note for its ceiling assemblies: check the filters and either clean or replace them, and do it often in heavy use.

Weak on high fan and colder on low

This is the signature of air leaking from the supply side back into the return side, which is the divider fault described below. On low fan there is less air to leak, so the vent feels colder.

The compressor cannot be heard running

If the fan runs and the compressor never starts, the unit is not cooling because nothing is moving heat. Airxcel lists low voltage, a failed start or run capacitor, a start relay with open contacts, and the compressor as the ranked causes. All of them are technician work.

The cooling sequence: Where your unit is stopping

Every roof unit follows the same steps. Deciding which one has failed is most of the diagnosis, because the steps after a failure never happen.

1. Power and the control call

Line voltage arrives at the unit and the thermostat or selector switch asks for cooling. Dometic's installation guidance specifies a 120 VAC supply on 12 AWG copper, protected by a time-delay fuse or a circuit breaker, and the thermostat on a mechanical unit covers roughly 65°F to 90°F at the air box inlet.

2. The fan moves air

The blower pulls air through the return, across the evaporator coil and out of the supply. If the fan is not moving enough air, nothing downstream works properly and the coil eventually ices.

3. The compressor starts

The compressor is what actually moves heat. Airxcel notes it is a hermetic compressor, sealed and welded, and therefore not internally serviceable. Dometic's operating instructions add one behavior: after shutting a unit down by the switch or the thermostat, wait two to three minutes before turning it back on, so the refrigerant pressures equalize and the compressor can start.

4. Heat leaves through the condenser

The condenser on the roof rejects the heat the evaporator collected. It needs outside air moving across it and it needs to be clean. Airxcel says a dirty outdoor condenser coil is one of the things to examine when the temperature differential is off.

5. The air that arrives is cold

If steps one to four happen, this follows. Measuring the drop is how you confirm it, and it is the reading that tells you which half of the problem you are in.

The temperature drop across the coil

This is the one measurement that does the most work. It needs no tools beyond a thermometer.

What the maker publishes

DocumentPublished dropConditions it states
Airxcel / Coleman-Mach, Operation, Maintenance and Trouble Shooting Guide, 1976A45516 to 22°FFan on High Cool, unit run at least half an hour to saturate the coil, outdoor air 80°F or above, measured at the evaporator inlet and the air supply vent with a digital thermometer

Furrion's rooftop manual publishes no temperature-differential figure at all, and offers symptom-based troubleshooting instead. If your unit is a Furrion, work from the method below rather than from a maker's number, and treat the general range as guidance rather than a specification for your unit.

How to take the reading

Run the unit on high fan for at least half an hour so the coil reaches a steady state, on a day at or above about 80°F. Read the return air at the intake, ideally at the filter, and the supply air in the discharge right at the grille rather than across the room. Subtract. Write both numbers down: "the air feels cold" is not a measurement, and a shop cannot work from it.

What the results mean

Inside the range and the RV is still hot. The unit is working and the problem is heat gain or air distribution: sun on the glass, a slide-out with no shade, ducting leaking into the roof cavity, or a unit undersized for the vehicle. Airxcel's guidance for high outdoor temperatures is to shade the RV, keep the doors and windows shut, and minimize heat-producing appliances. Dometic adds window shades and starting the unit early in the morning so it gets a head start.

Below the range. The coil is not getting cold enough. Airflow comes first because it is the common cause and the one you can fix. If airflow is clean and the reading is still low, the sealed system is the next step and that is technician work.

Above 22°F. Counter-intuitive but documented: Airxcel states that a difference greater than 22°F is possible in warm dry weather, and that restricted airflow over the evaporator can also push the number up while the unit is actually losing capacity. A big number is not automatically a good one.

Airflow: Filters, coils and the divider

Almost everything an owner can fix lives here, and it is where the manuals put the emphasis.

The filter

Airxcel states plainly what a clogged filter does: it produces a loss of air volume and may eventually cause the cooling coil to ice up. Its maintenance guidance for the ceiling assembly is to check the filters and either clean or replace them, and to expect that to be frequent during heavy use. This is the cheapest item on the page and the most common cause.

The evaporator coil

The face of the coil collects lint and dirt, and a coated coil restricts the airflow through it. Airxcel's instruction for a soiled coil is that the unit comes off the roof and the coil is cleaned with a proper coil cleaner and water. A vacuum and a soft brush handles the surface; anything more thorough is off-unit work.

The divider between the return and the supply

This is the fault owners rarely check. The air box separates the cold supply air from the warm return air, and if the divider has moved, torn or was never sealed, cold air is pulled straight back into the intake. The unit then reheats its own output, the vent air feels barely cool, and the coil can sit below freezing and ice up.

Dometic's own AirCommand installation instructions show what the part is meant to do and how it is installed: a polystyrene baffle is cut to the height between the ceiling and the steel base of the unit, with a self-adhesive foam strip on three sides, and it is cut from the side without the foam so the foam seals against the surfaces. Any gap left at that baffle produces the same symptom as a failed divider. When you have the ceiling grille down, this is the thing to look at.

How much return air the unit needs

Furrion publishes a requirement that applies to any brand, because a return that has been blocked by a curtain, a cushion or a storage bin is a real cause of poor cooling: the return-air opening must have at least 80 square inches of free area including the filter.

The compressor, and where owner work stops

Running amps against the nameplate

The rating plate gives a compressor rated load amps figure, and Dometic's own specification tables publish the comparable numbers for their units, for example 12.5 amps full load on the 13,500 BTU AirCommand model and 13.0 amps on the 15,000 BTU model, both with locked rotor amps of 63. The measured current is not fixed, because compressor current rises with outdoor temperature, and Airxcel publishes the correction: the reading should equal the rated figure at 95°F outdoor, and rise by about 0.7 amps for every 5°F above that, falling the same way below it. Airxcel labels its own chart approximate, and so should anyone quoting it.

Why the manuals will not let you test the rest yourself

Airxcel's notice reads that these instructions are for qualified individuals specially trained and experienced in this equipment, and that persons not qualified shall not service it, adding that service personnel are required by some states to be licensed. Dometic prints that the unit must be serviced by qualified service personnel only, and that this product must be installed by a qualified service technician. Furrion requires that installation and repair be carried out only by qualified personnel familiar with the risks and the relevant regulations, and that any work in the refrigerant system, including recharging, be done by a certified service technician.

A capacitor is the specific reason that warning is not boilerplate. It holds a charge after the power is off, and the manuals treat that as a hazard rather than a curiosity. Disconnect the power and do not put your hands inside the control box until the charge has had time to dissipate; a start capacitor is read, not touched.

Running it on limited power: 30 amps, a small generator, or batteries

The running load is rarely what stops a roof air conditioner. Dometic lists 12.5 amps full load for its 13,500 BTU AirCommand and 13.0 for the 15,000, Furrion lists 11.5 cooling amps for the Chill HE 13.5K, and Coleman-Mach lists 13.9 for the Mach 15. Two of the 13,500 BTU units add up to about 25 amps, which is inside a 30 amp service. What a 30 amp service, a small generator or a battery bank cannot do is start them, and the start is where the numbers change.

What the start draws

Dometic publishes 63 locked rotor amps for both AirCommand sizes. Cummins describes the same event from the generator side: air conditioners need reserve power at startup and can draw three to four times the 1,400 to 2,400 watts they need to run, and too much baseload can prevent them from starting. The locked rotor figure is the current the compressor draws at the instant it starts, before it comes up to speed.

What a soft start does, and what it is not

A soft start fits between the supply and the compressor and ramps the motor up instead of switching full voltage into it. Micro-Air publishes the mechanism and the result in its EasyStart installation manual: the manual states that the device controls the power applied to the compressor during the start and slightly lengthens the start process, and gives the reduction as up to 65 to 75 percent of the start current. The same company publishes the distinction: a hard start kit is not a soft start. A hard start adds current to the start winding, and Micro-Air states those devices deliver at best a 10 to 20 percent reduction to the normal locked rotor amps, with no reduction at all in some cases.

The air conditioner makers sell their own. Coleman-Mach lists a compressor soft start kit as an accessory that fits all profiles, states in its catalog that the kit reduces the inrush amps up to 75 percent in the power-up phase, and lists a Mach 10 model with soft start. Dometic lists Advanced Soft Start on its FJZ series, described as quiet and gentle start-up even on campsites with low fuse protection.

Two units on a 30 amp service

The running load fits a 30 amp service. The two starts are what do not, and that is the case Micro-Air makes for its own product, claiming two roof air conditioners on a 30 amp cord with soft starts fitted. Cummins' RV generator handbook describes a related trap: some RVs with two air conditioners have a 20 amp breaker for the rear unit and a 30 amp breaker for everything else, and when the two circuits are reversed the breaker trips when the second unit is turned on. The handbook's answer is that the generator connections and wiring should be checked at an authorized service location.

A generator smaller than the coach's service

The generator's rating is the ceiling, not the coach's 50 amp service. Cummins publishes the pairing in its accessory catalog: a 4.0 kW generator runs one 15,000 BTU air conditioner, 5.5 kW runs two 13,500 BTU units, and 7 kW runs two 15,000 BTU units. The QG 4000i's operator manual lists its output as 33.3 amps at 120 volts, and its appliance table puts an air conditioner at 1,200 to 2,400 watts, or 10 to 20 amps. A small inverter generator fails on the arithmetic even when its rating looks close: 2,000 watts is about 16.7 amps at 120 volts, against a startup draw Cummins puts at three to four times the running figure. If the generator is the question, sizing a generator for an RV works through the load list.

Off-grid on solar and lithium

The DC side is arithmetic rather than a published figure. A unit drawing 12.5 amps at 115 volts is drawing about 1,440 watts, and at 12 volts that is about 120 amps, before any losses in the inverter. What Dometic and Micro-Air publish about battery operation is about control rather than runtime: Dometic's FJZ7 pairs with its TEMPRA battery and a SmartEco mode that reads the charge state and steps the unit down from an energy-saving mode to fan to off, restoring full power when the charge reaches 60 percent, and Micro-Air lists off-grid use with solar arrays and starting a unit on a battery bank among the uses of its soft starters. No document cited on this page publishes a runtime figure for a roof air conditioner running on a battery bank, and the running figure above is where that arithmetic starts.

Heat-pump mode: when the unit heats instead of cools

A heat pump is the same roof unit running its refrigerant circuit in reverse, and it produces one cooling complaint that is not a fault at all: the thermostat is asking for heat. Airxcel's heat pump documents describe the two heating selections a controller can offer, HEAT ELE for the electric heat pump and HEAT GAS for a gas furnace, and state that HEAT GAS should only be used if a gas furnace is to be operated with the controller. A unit set to a heating selection will heat, and no amount of refrigerant work changes that.

Where the heat pump stops working on its own

Airxcel states that the heat pump shuts down at conditions that would cause the outdoor coil to freeze, generally near freezing temperatures, and that it operates on reverse cycle above freezing. A thermostat guide published on Coleman-Mach's site puts the working range at an outdoor temperature above 40 degrees, with humidity moving that number a little either way. Airxcel also publishes the temperature change to expect in each mode: a 15 to 20 degree drop from inlet to supply in cooling, a 25 to 40 degree rise in heating, and only a 10 to 20 degree rise once the freeze switch has activated. That cooling figure is narrower than the 16 to 22 degrees Airxcel's troubleshooting guide publishes for the same measurement.

When the thermostat has locked the heat pump out

The same thermostat guide describes a lockout that owners meet as a heat pump that has stopped heating or will not return to electric heat. The thermostat counts a strike each time the system cannot hold the set point, either from a five degree split between the set point and the room or from twenty minutes of running without reaching it. After three strikes it locks the electric heat out for two hours and runs gas heat only. The guide states the lockout is hard, that no reset bypasses it, and that pulling the fuse will not clear it. It also states there is no outdoor ambient sensor that shuts the heat pump down, so a heat pump that has stopped heating has either reached its own low temperature limit or been locked out by the thermostat.

After a control board or thermostat swap

This failure follows a repair rather than causing one. Dometic's CCC 2 thermostat reads the system's configuration from DIP switches on the control board, and its instructions state that any time those settings are changed a system reset must be done before the thermostat will recognize the updated selection. A board or thermostat replaced without matching the switches to the equipment, or without the reset, can leave the furnace unrecognized while the air conditioner still works, which reads as a furnace fault and is a configuration one. Its error codes name two invalid combinations directly: the heat pump and heat strip switches set on the same zone, and the dehumidifier switch set with either of them.

The high-pressure lockout

Airxcel's R410A heat pumps carry a high-pressure switch that stops the compressor when a fan motor fails, the condenser coil is dirty or the filters are blocked. Once it has tripped, the circuit locks the compressor out until the supply power is turned off and back on, and Airxcel states that repeated trips mean the unit needs a qualified technician.

Refrigerant: What the documentation supports

You will read online that these units are sealed and cannot be recharged, so a unit that has lost its charge is scrap.

What is documented

The compressor is hermetic: Airxcel states it is completely sealed and welded together and therefore not internally field serviceable. Airxcel also prints, in capitals, that if you have not been trained in sealed-system refrigeration repair you should not attempt to break into the system. Dometic and Furrion both require qualified or certified personnel for work in the refrigerant circuit, and Furrion adds that the refrigerant must never be released into the air. Manufacturers do publish the charge a unit holds, which tells you the sealed system is a specified, measured thing rather than a black box: Dometic lists 18 oz for one AirCommand model and 24 oz for the larger one, in R410A.

What this page will not tell you

The field procedures for diagnosing a low charge, and the charging procedure itself, are in service documentation this page could not read from the manufacturer's copy when it was written. Procedures that appear only on third-party mirror sites are not published here, because a mirror can vanish and it is somebody else's copy. If you want the details, ask a technician who has the current service manual, or ask Dometic directly through their documents database. What you should not do is let anyone top the system up without finding out where the charge went, because refrigerant does not leave a sealed circuit on its own.

When the complaint is noise

Dometic's FreshJet 5 sheet states that the motor and blower changes reduce cabin noise by up to 9 dB and gives about 59 dB on low, and its FJZ page gives about 43 dB for the quietest unit in that series. Furrion's Chill HE 13.5K spec sheet lists an outdoor sound pressure level of 80 dB. Coleman-Mach sells a Quiet Series and states the series has some of the quietest units on the market, without publishing a figure. The published numbers are not comparable with each other: 80 dB outdoors is a different measurement from 59 dB inside a cabin, and neither sheet states a distance or a weighting. None of the service documents cited on this page treats noise as a fault with a cause and a fix.

The start is the loudest moment

Micro-Air describes the noise its soft starter removes: the loud noise when the compressor first starts, replaced by a smooth ramp. If the noise you are chasing happens at the moment the unit starts and then settles, that is the compressor start, and the soft start section above is about the same event.

What you can and cannot conclude from this page

That range is Airxcel's, and no rooftop air conditioner document publishes an outdoor temperature above which the unit is not expected to cool. If your unit is losing ground on a 105°F day, that is consistent with the documentation rather than proof of a fault, and the manufacturer's advice in that situation is about reducing heat gain, not about repairing anything.

Related guides

Moisture is the other thing an air conditioner gets blamed for, and condensation inside an RV separates the appliance from the humidity. If the unit that has stopped cooling is the fridge rather than the roof air conditioner, that is refrigerator not cooling. If the problem is the power it is plugged into rather than the unit, sizing a generator for an RV works through the loads. The maker's documents for the roof unit are under heating and cooling manuals.

RV smells like sewage: finding the actual source

A sewer smell in an RV comes from somewhere specific, and where you smell it narrows the cause faster than anything else you can do. Dometic's own manual splits it two ways at the toilet: odour with the flush ball open is tank pressure through a blocked vent, and odour with the bowl closed is a failed water seal. Those are different faults with different fixes, and the smell alone does not tell you which one you have.

By OriginRV. Last updated on Oct 2, 2026.

Start with where, not what. At the toilet with the lid up and the flush ball open, it is tank air getting in. At the toilet with the bowl closed and dry, it is a seal. Under a sink or in a cabinet, it is an air admittance valve. Only while driving, it is pressure. Those four lead to different checks, and guessing at tank chemicals first wastes a season.

Start here: match where you smell it to its source

At the toilet, with the flush ball open

This is the manufacturer's own first branch. Dometic's troubleshooting table lists "odor emits from the toilet when the flush ball is open" with the cause "the holding tank vent is plugged", and the remedy is to clear obstructions from the vent and refer to the tank owner's manual. The same cell allows for the simpler case, that the odour is from the holding tank contents, in which case their remedy is to add deodorant to the tank. So an open-ball smell means tank air is finding a way in, and the vent is the first place it is finding it.

At the toilet, with the bowl closed

The second branch, and it is a different fault entirely. Dometic's table gives "odor emits from the toilet when the flush ball is closed" three possible causes: there is no water in the bowl, the flush ball seal is damaged, or the floor flange seal is damaged. The remedies are, in that order, add water to the bowl, replace the ball seal, or replace the floor seal. Note what is not on that list: tank treatment. If the bowl holds water and you still smell it with the lid down, the seal is the suspect.

Under a sink, or inside a cabinet

That is the air admittance valve, and it has a signature. Oatey, who make them, state the failure symptom directly: "the clearest sign is a foul sewer odor near a fixture where a Sure-Vent is installed, which means the valve is not sealing properly and should be replaced." They also rule something out, which is as useful: "slow drains, on the other hand, are typically unrelated to the valve."

Only while driving

This is the case owners describe most and the one with the least manufacturer documentation. There is a maker statement about it, which is quoted in full further down, and it is narrower than the forums suggest. What matters first is that a smell which appears only in motion is a pressure problem, not a chemistry problem, so tank treatment will not touch it.

After dumping, or when a specific fixture runs

Odour that appears during or after a dump is usually procedure, and odour that appears when a sink or shower runs is usually a dry trap. Both are covered below.

The toilet bowl is a water seal, and it is measured

The single most useful piece of maintenance on this page is also the easiest. Dometic states that "a small amount of water will collect in the bowl after a flush to create an airtight seal", and puts a number on it: "so that holding tank odors do not enter the living space, make sure 1 in. (24 mm) of water remains in the toilet bowl." They add that depending on the plumbing and how you use the toilet, you may have to add water deliberately to reach that depth.

They also document how to add it without flushing: depress the flush pedal part way, so water flows into the bowl while the flush ball stays shut, and let up slightly if the ball starts to move.

A bowl that will not hold an inch of water is a bowl with a leaking seal, and no amount of tank treatment fixes that.

Reconditioning a seal before replacing it

Worth knowing before you buy a part. Dometic explains that each toilet is factory-tested for a watertight seal, and that this testing holds the flush ball open with a device which may distort the ball seal from its original shape, which can then leak into the holding tank in normal use. Their procedure is to hold the ball open, press down around the edge of the seal with a finger to conform it, close the ball, pour a quart of hot tap water into the bowl and let it cool completely, then check for a leak. Only if it still leaks does the seal get replaced.

Thetford puts a service life on theirs: they recommend replacing the lip seal every four to six years, depending on use.

The air admittance valve

An AAV is a one-way air valve fitted in place of a vent pipe you cannot run, and it is common under a kitchen island or a bathroom vanity. Oatey, who make them, describe its function in their own words: the valve "help[s] maintain proper system pressure while preventing sewer gases from entering a building", and it is used "where permitted by local plumbing codes as an alternative venting method". It opens under the negative pressure of a fixture draining and closes again when the flow stops, and because it closes, a valve that has stopped sealing is a valve that lets the smell back in.

So when it is working it is shut, and when it is not working it is a hole between your cabinet and your tank.

The documented failure, and the documented RV limit

Oatey's own symptom is odour at the fixture, and their answer is replacement rather than repair. What is more interesting is what they say about RVs, because a manufacturer rarely speaks to this at all. Asked whether the valve is approved for RV use, Oatey answer: "Yes, with exceptions. When the RV is stationary, the Sure-Vent will properly vent the drains for the kitchen and bathroom. If the RV is in motion and depending on how the DWV system was assembled from the factory, positive pressure could stop the Sure-Vent from opening."

Read that carefully, because it is the closest thing to a manufacturer explanation of the driving smell. In motion, the valve may not open when it should, which is a venting failure rather than a seal failure. It is also the reason a smell in motion is not fixed by anything you pour into the tank.

What is not documented: a widely repeated forum claim is that these valves fail in the open position. Neither Oatey nor Studor publishes that failure mode, and Studor describes the opposite, that positive pressure "causes the valve to seal even tighter". The makers' position is that a failed valve shows as odour at the fixture and gets replaced.

The roof vent and its cap

Every holding tank is vented, and the vent runs up through the roof, which is why your roof has one or two small pipes on it. The cap on top is there to keep rain out while letting air move, and it is governed by a standard: NFPA 1192 requires that "vent caps shall provide a free air exposure equal to at least the cross-sectional area of the vent pipe." A cap that has been replaced with something decorative, or clogged with debris or a wasp nest, is a real defect rather than a matter of taste, and it is the first thing to check after the open-ball symptom above.

Caps that claim to do more exist. Lippert make a cap, the 360 Siphon, that vents whenever the wind moves across it and, in their description, works by creating an updraft to expel odours. Treat that as the maker's description of their product rather than as a fix for a fault you have not found yet: a blocked vent is a blocked vent, and a new cap on a blocked pipe changes nothing.

Why driving is different

Three things change when the RV is moving: the toilet bowl sloshes, the roof vent sees a different pressure, and anything relying on gravity or a pressure difference behaves differently.

Traps, and the smell that comes from one

Every sink and shower has a trap holding a water plug against the tank side, and a trap that has dried out is a straight path between the grey tank and the room. This is the common cause of a smell in a room that has not been used, and of a smell that arrives the first time a fixture is used after a long park-up. Running water down the fixture refills it, and if the smell returns within days, the trap is being siphoned or the fixture has a venting problem rather than a dry trap.

The dump, in the order the manuals give

Procedure matters more than most owners expect. Forest River's owner manual gives the order and the reasons together: drain the black tank first, then drain the grey tank, because "draining the gray tank last uses the soapy water in the tank to rinse the drain and flexible hose." The same manual sets a rule that prevents the most common aftermath: "never dump black water tank until it is 3/4 full. This practice ensures that enough water is in the tank to flush all wastes into sewer line." Jayco and Winnebago give the same order.

So a smell that appears immediately after dumping is usually one of two things: dumping a tank that was too empty to move its contents properly, or leaving the hose and the outlet dirty because the grey tank was drained first.

What the manuals say not to put in the system

Both major toilet makers are blunt about chemicals, and their reason is the seals. Dometic: "do not use chlorine or caustic chemicals, such as laundry bleach or drain-opening products, in the system. These products damage the seals in toilets and other plumbing valves." Thetford: "bleach and acids found in most household cleaning products will potentially dry out and damage the seals", and separately, "do not use petroleum based products", and never use petroleum jelly or vegetable oil to lubricate a seal. If your tank treatment has to be chosen from a shelf, that is the constraint that decides it.

Thetford also adds a rule worth knowing before a trip: never travel with a waste holding tank more than three quarters full.

And Forest River's list of what must never go into either tank: "normal drain water, wastes and biodegradable products" is the whole list, with paper wrappers, gum and cigarettes named explicitly, and automotive antifreeze, household toilet cleaner and drain cleaners prohibited.

Owner-doable vs call a technician

Yours to do: keep an inch of water in the bowl, recondition a seal before replacing it, run water into fixtures that have sat dry, check the roof vent and its cap for obstruction, replace an air admittance valve under a sink, and follow the dump order. Those cover the large majority of smells that owners report.

Not yours: anything requiring the toilet to come off the floor, the floor flange seal, and any work on the tank itself. A damaged floor flange seal is a documented cause of odour with the bowl closed, and it is beneath the toilet.

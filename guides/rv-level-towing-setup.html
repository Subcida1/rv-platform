How to Level Your RV When Towing: Hitch and Weight Setup

The combination rides at three levels at once, and they are set by three different adjustments. Set the ball height, restore the truck's front fender to the target your own manual publishes, then set the trailer's pitch and re-prove the distribution, in that order. When a sag survives all three, it is a weight or a frame fault, and no hitch setting removes it.

By OriginRV. Last reviewed on Oct 29, 2026.

What level actually means

Three separate things ride at once behind a tow vehicle, and a driver who says "it is not level" is usually describing one and adjusting another.

  • The tow vehicle's front end. The tongue weight lands on a ball or a kingpin behind the rear axle, which pushes the rear axle down and, through the frame, lifts the front. Lifting the front takes weight off the steering axle, and a steering axle with less load has less grip to steer and less grip to stop.
  • The trailer's frame. Level means parallel to the road: the same distance from the ground to the frame at the front and at the back, measured on level pavement with the combination hitched and loaded.
  • The hitch itself. The ball height or the fifth wheel hitch's plate height sets where the trailer's nose sits before any spring bar or pin box is touched.

The order matters. A hitch head set too low, then cranked tight enough to lift the truck, gives a tow vehicle that looks level and a trailer that rides nose high, and the cranked bar is holding up tongue weight the tires are supposed to carry. That combination is the one that does not go away no matter where the hitch is set, and it has a cause further down than the hitch.

The ratings, and which one bites first

Four numbers decide the whole setup, and they are printed, not chosen. GVWR is the most a vehicle may weigh loaded. GAWR is the most one axle may carry, front or rear. Payload is GVWR minus the vehicle's own weight, and it is what tongue weight, pin weight, passengers and gear all come out of. GCWR is the most the pair may weigh together.

Tongue weight is the number novices skip, and it is the one that makes the truck squat. The floor is the same across most published guidance: 10 to 15 percent of gross trailer weight on the ball. Equal-i-zer's manual states that towing outside that range "increases the likelihood for loss of vehicle control and/or equipment failure." For a fifth wheel, Reese's 16K fifth wheel instruction manual gives "approximately 15%-25% of trailer weight should be on hitch (Pin Weight)."

The receiver hitch has two ratings of its own, and they are different numbers. CURT publishes Class 3 as up to 8,000 lb gross trailer weight and 800 lb tongue weight in weight-carrying form, and up to 12,000 lb and 1,200 lb when a weight-distributing hitch is used. The label on the receiver states both. If the label or the vehicle manual says a weight-distributing hitch is required above a certain trailer weight, a plain ball mount is not an option at that weight however well the combination looks.

The bottleneck is the lowest number, not the average. If the truck's rear GAWR says 300 lb of headroom and the trailer's tongue weighs 800 lb, the truck is overloaded by 500 lb before the first mile, and no hitch adjustment changes where that weight sits on the truck. It only moves part of it forward.

What a weight-distributing hitch does, in one picture

Picture the truck as a lever with its pivot at the rear axle. Tongue weight hanging behind the pivot pulls the back down and the front up. A weight-distributing hitch adds a second lever: spring bars clamped into the hitch head, loaded against brackets on the trailer's A-frame, push the trailer's nose up and the truck's rear down at the same time, and by doing so they send part of the tongue weight forward onto the truck's front axle and back onto the trailer's axles.

Three consequences follow, and every setup instruction on this page is one of them:

  • Bar tension sets how much goes forward. More tension, more load on the front axle.
  • Ball height sets the trailer's pitch. Raise the ball and the trailer's nose rises; lower it and the nose drops. This is independent of tension, which is why a combination can have perfect distribution and a nose-high trailer at the same time.
  • Too much tension is a fault, not a margin. Equal-i-zer's manual warns that over-distribution "can remove too much weight from the tow vehicle's rear axle. This can cause a loss of traction and control causing jack-knifing, especially in slick road conditions."

The measurement that settles it: front fender height

Every major maker reduces the adjustment to the same three tape measurements taken at the truck's front wheel opening. Equal-i-zer's manual spells the arithmetic out, and Ford publishes the same method with the name front axle load restoration.

  1. A: uncoupled. Park on level ground, loaded as you travel. Measure from the ground to the bottom of the front wheel well, through the center line of the axle. Measure both sides and use the average.
  2. B: coupled, bars off. Lower the coupler onto the ball, latch it, retract the jack fully. The whole tongue weight now rests on the truck. Measure the same point.
  3. C: coupled, bars on. Raise the combination with the tongue jack, swing the spring bars onto the brackets, pin them, then retract the jack until the tongue weight is back on the hitch. Measure again.

The percentage of weight restored is 100 x (B minus C) divided by (B minus A). Equal-i-zer prints a worked example: A 30 in, B 31 in, C 30 1/4 in, which is 75 percent, and states the target as between 50 and 100 percent. Ford's 2026 RV and Trailer Towing Guide publishes a correction factor per vehicle and a worked example: on an F-150, H1 at 37 in and H2 at 38 in with a 50 percent factor gives a target height of 37.5 in. A 25 percent factor appears only on the F-450 Regular Cab.

Ram's 2500 and 3500 owner's manual gives the same rule in one line: adjust the bars "so that the height of the front fender is approximately (H2-H1)/2+H1", which is half the sag, restored. That is 50 percent, and it applies to the air suspension trucks and the steel-sprung ones alike.

Read your own manual's target before you aim for a number you read somewhere. Ford's factor is 50 percent on most trucks and 25 percent on one cab; Equal-i-zer calls 50 to 100 percent good and warns above 100. The truck's manual wins, because the truck's front axle is what is being loaded.

The setup, in the order that works

The sequence below is the order Equal-i-zer and Blue Ox both use, with the reason each step comes where it does. Doing the trailer pitch before the distribution, or the distribution before the truck is loaded, is how a combination ends up with two faults that cancel each other on the tape and neither one fixed on the road.

  1. Load everything first. Water, propane, gear, the people who ride in the truck. Equal-i-zer's manual is explicit: "the tow vehicle and trailer should be loaded just as they will be while traveling." Distribution set on an empty trailer is set for a trailer you are not towing.
  2. Turn off the truck's auto-leveling. Equal-i-zer's manual says auto leveling and air suspension "will decrease the amount of weight distribution provided by the hitch after setup," and its appendix quantifies a four-corner system as reducing distribution by 20 to 25 percent. If the system cannot be left off while driving, the manual says to set the hitch for 75 to 100 percent so the loss comes off a larger number.
  3. Level the trailer on its own jack, parallel to the ground. Measure to the frame at the front and the back.
  4. Set the ball height. Equal-i-zer wants the top of the ball 0 to 1 inch above the top of the coupler. CURT's instruction is one to three inches. Blue Ox says the ball height "needs to be the same or 1" higher than the trailer coupler height," and adds a note that for Ram trucks it recommends 2 inches higher, pointing the reader to Ram's own manual. Ford's guide says to set the ball the same height as the coupler. These are different numbers from different makers, and the spread is the point: the ball height is a starting point, and the trailer's own pitch measurement in step 8 is what proves it.
  5. Couple and take measurement B.
  6. Load the bars and take measurement C, then adjust. Equal-i-zer's two levers are spacer washers at the hitch head, which change the head's angle, and the L-bracket height. Changing either moves the percentage; the manual's loop is to re-hitch, re-measure, and repeat.
  7. Set the trailer's pitch, then re-check the distribution. This is the step most owners skip. Equal-i-zer's manual says that if the difference between the front and rear frame measurements is more than 1 1/4 inches, adjust the ball height, then "after making any adjustments to the ball height, fully hitch and engage the spring arms and remeasure the front wheel well, for line C." Raising the ball to fix the pitch changes the distribution, so the distribution has to be re-proven. Repeat until both are right at the same time.
  8. Torque everything and re-check after the first tow.

Nose high, nose low, and what each one costs

A trailer that rides perfectly parallel is the goal, and a small tolerance is normal. Beyond it, the faults are not symmetrical.

Blue Ox's manual also carries the plain version for a truck that squats with a nose dip: "raise the chains of the spring bars up by one link." Its chain-latch design counts distribution in links, and the manual says to adjust "one (1) link at a time."

The sag that will not go away

A combination that sags with a good bar setting, at a tongue weight inside the trailer's rating and the truck's payload, has a fault the hitch cannot reach. The hitch moves weight between axles that can carry it. It does not create capacity, and it cannot fix a bent frame or a spring that has already given up. Work down this list, in this order, and each item has a check that tells it apart from the one above.

The trailer is heavier than it reads

Water is the usual culprit. A 50 gallon fresh tank is about 415 lb, and a full black and gray pair can be another 600 lb or more, sitting in tanks whose position relative to the axles decides whether that weight lands on the tongue or behind the axles. A trailer loaded with water it does not need, proven by the scale and not by the brochure, is the first thing to rule out.

The truck's bed is loaded behind the rear axle

Tools, a generator, firewood and a full cooler behind the rear axle are tongue weight the hitch bar cannot see and the rear axle feels fully. Each 100 lb there lifts the front axle roughly in proportion to the distance from the rear axle, which is the same arithmetic that makes 800 lb of tongue weight lift the front.

The receiver, the shank, or the hitch mount is damaged

A hitch that sags further than it used to, without the load changing, can have a bent or cracked receiver, a wallowed pin hole, or a shank that is no longer seated. This is a visual and physical inspection, not a tape measurement, and it comes after the weight questions because a bent hitch on an overloaded truck is a symptom and a bent hitch on a correct truck is the fault.

The rear springs have given up

Leaf springs that sit lower than they used to when empty, or that go flat under a load they used to carry, are worn rather than overloaded. The measurement is the needle: measure the rear wheel arch height empty on level ground, and compare it with the same measurement at the same fuel level a month or a season earlier. A truck that has lost an inch empty will look overloaded with half the weight it used to carry.

The trailer's frame or A-frame is bent

This is the fault that produces a sag no hitch setting removes, because the hitch is mounted to the bent part. A frame that has taken a hard hit, a battery bank hung on a tongue that was not built for it, or a coupler that has been flipped by an owner working outside the coupler's rating all belong here. The check is a straightedge along the top of both A-frame rails from the coupler back to the first cross member, and eye height at frame level on level ground. A bend shows as a curve where the rail should be straight, and a crack shows as a line in the paint at a weld or a bolt hole. Both are a repair, not an adjustment.

The trailer's axles are not carrying their share

Weight distribution sends part of the tongue weight back to the trailer's axles, and it only works if those axles are where the design says they are. A tandem axle trailer with one axle out of alignment scrubs sideways, and the tires wear in a diagonal pattern that a single axle never shows. The check is tread depth across each tire on both axles, compared left to right and front to back: a trailer that is tracking straight wears all four evenly.

Worn or uneven tires hold one side down

A tire that has lost pressure, or a pair on one side that sit at a different pressure from the other, changes the loaded geometry enough to read as a lean rather than a sag. Set all four trailer tires and both truck tires to the pressures on their own labels, cold, and measure the frame again on level ground before blaming the hitch.

Fifth wheels: level is set by the hitch and the pin box

A fifth wheel does not have spring bars. Its leveling tools are the hitch's height and the pin box's height, and its weight lands over or near the rear axle instead of behind it, which is why a fifth wheel of the same gross weight usually squats a truck less than a bumper pull.

Reese's 16K fifth wheel instruction manual gives the rule: "The height of the hitch and the pin box should be adjusted so the trailer is approximately level as it is towed. Allow approximately 6 inches clearance between the top of the pickup walls and the underside of the front of the trailer for pitch and roll of the trailer." It says to allow more clearance for off-road use.

Both halves are adjustable on most setups, and moving one to solve a problem can create the other. B&W's Companion manual shows the vertical range it builds in: 17, 18 and 19 inches at the kingpin on the Ram base, and 16 3/4, 17 3/4 and 18 3/4 inches on the Ford and GM bases, with the height choice made by which set of pivot-arm holes the arms are mounted in. Its instruction for bed clearance says to "allow several inches so that uneven ground doesn't cause contact," and to raise the hitch or lower an adjustable kingpin to change that distance. A trailer that is level with the hitch at its lowest and still close to the rails is a trailer that needs the pin box moved, not the hitch.

Pin weight for a fifth wheel is heavier than tongue weight: Reese's 16K manual gives 15 to 25 percent of gross trailer weight on the hitch. That percentage is why the truck's rear axle rating and the tires are the first check on any fifth wheel setup.

Coupling is its own procedure. Fontaine's fifth wheel instructions require a tug test with the trailer brakes applied, then a stop and a visual inspection of the jaws and the gap, and state plainly that "some improper couplings can pass a tug test and sound is not reliable to verify proper coupling."

Tow-behinds behind a motorhome: flat tow and dolly

A motorhome towing a car faces the same level question in a simpler form: the tow bar has to sit level, and the coach's hitch has to be within its rating with the car's weight on it. There is no weight distribution at this end of the trade, because a tow bar is not a weight-distributing hitch and the car's own suspension carries its weight.

Flat tow

The measurement is base plate to hitch. CURT's instruction measures from the ground to the center of the RV's receiver pin hole and from the ground to the attachment tabs on the car, and says the difference should be no more than 3 inches, with a drop or rise adapter for more. Demco gives the same 3 inch tolerance and adds that its own tow bar carries a built-in 1 1/2 inch rise or drop, so a difference over 4 1/2 inches needs an additional adapter.

The rest of flat towing is the car's own manual. Ford's 2026 RV and Trailer Towing Guide lists, model by model, which of its vehicles can be towed four wheels down and under what conditions: the Escape Hybrid and Plug-in Hybrid, the Bronco, the Expedition 4x4, the Maverick Hybrid, the Ranger 4x4 and the F-150 4x4 carry a yes with footnotes, while the Explorer, the Escape, the Bronco Sport and every 4x2 truck carry a no. The footnotes name the procedures: neutral tow mode, a two-speed transfer case in neutral, a maximum speed of 70 mph on the hybrid transmission, and an engine start every six hours or fewer.

The equipment list is base plates, a tow bar, safety cables crossed once under the bar, wiring, and a supplemental brake system. Demco's own guidance on brake systems is that they take the load off the coach and shorten stopping distance, and Ford's guide recommends a separate functional brake system on any towed vehicle, dolly-towed or towbar-towed.

Dolly

A dolly lifts the car's front wheels and leaves the back two rolling, and it is a trailer in its own right: it has its own weight, its own brakes and its own straps. Ford's tow-dolly guidance is to attach the dolly to the drawbar, keep it on level ground before a car goes on or comes off, drive the car on front wheels first, secure it to the dolly maker's instructions, and wire the lights. Its dolly availability chart excludes every AWD and 4WD vehicle with a footnote: "AWD/4WD vehicles cannot be towed with 2 wheels lifted off the ground."

The dolly choice is mostly an arithmetic question against the coach's hitch rating. A car at 3,500 lb on a dolly at 600 lb is 4,100 lb of trailer behind a hitch rated for 5,000, and it is 4,100 lb that the coach's brakes have to stop in addition to its own weight. A vehicle that needs a dolly and cannot take a flat tow belongs on a flatbed trailer, where the whole car is carried and the trailer's axles carry it.

The car's manual decides, not the dolly seller. Demco's own advice is to look in the car's owner's manual under Recreational Vehicle Towing before buying anything, because the maker may permit one method and not the other. Ford's chart is the worked example: the same trim level is a yes or a no depending on the drivetrain.

Terrain: why it looks right in the driveway and wrong at the campsite

Everything above is measured on level pavement, and most campsites are not level. Three things change on a grade, and each has a different answer.

  • A ramp or a dip under the hitch loads the bars hard. Equal-i-zer's manual says to disengage weight distribution before backing or towing "where there is a significant transition in grade, for example, backing from a level street onto a driveway with a steep uphill slope," because "failure to disengage the hitch will put excessive strain on the trailer and receiver hitch."
  • A side slope tilts the whole combination, and a trailer parked across a slope with one side low puts its weight on two tires and one spring pack. That is a parking problem, answered with blocks under the low side's tires and the wheels chocked.
  • The combination that was level on flat ground will pitch as it goes up a grade, because a long trailer sees the change in grade before the truck's rear axle does. A trailer that looks nose high only on a climb is showing geometry, not a setup fault.

At a campsite, level is about the trailer's frame and the appliances, not the hitch. A level set on the frame rail at the door, and the fridge's own tolerance, are what the trailer is being measured against once the truck is unhooked.

A checklist for the first tow, and every tow after

  1. Trailer loaded as it will travel. Water in the tanks you intend to use.
  2. Auto-leveling off, or the hitch set to the higher target its manual gives.
  3. Ball height set from your hitch's manual, coupler latched, safety pin in, jack fully retracted.
  4. Bars loaded, distribution measured at the front fender, target from the truck's own manual.
  5. Trailer pitch measured front and back on the frame. If it is out, fix it and re-measure the distribution.
  6. Both tire pressures set cold, on truck and trailer.
  7. Lights, breakaway cable, safety chains crossed, brake controller set.
  8. After the first ten miles: tires, hitch bolts, the coupler latch, and the bars.

Frequently asked

My hitch is set up right and the trailer still rides high. What else is left?

Ball height and bar tension are two different adjustments. If the front fender measures inside the target range and the trailer still looks nose high, the ball is too high or the coupler sits too high on the trailer's frame, and those belong to the shank and the coupler rather than the bars. Set the pitch first on level ground, then re-prove the distribution, because changing the ball height changes the measurement that proves distribution. If neither adjustment holds, the next suspects are a bent A-frame or a coupler that was flipped or replaced outside its rating.

Can I set my hitch in a campsite?

Not accurately. Every method here assumes level pavement and a trailer loaded for the trip, and a dirt site with a slope makes both of those false. A rough setting made on a site is fine for limping to a scale or a lot; the real setup wants a flat lot or a level pad.

How much sag is normal?

The makers do not publish a "normal sag" figure, because the number that matters is the front fender height restored, not the rear height lost. A truck that drops an inch and a half at the rear with the front restored to its target is doing exactly what the hitch is for. A truck that drops four inches with the front still high is over its rear axle, and the tape on the front fender is how you tell the two apart.

Does a weight-distributing hitch raise my tongue weight limit?

No. It changes where the tongue weight sits on the vehicle; the tongue weight itself is the trailer's and does not move. What changes is the receiver's rating, which is often published as two numbers, one for weight-carrying and a higher one for weight-distributing. The truck's rear axle rating and payload do not change at all.

Will airbags fix the squat?

Airbags change the truck's ride height and, in Ram's own instruction, change how much the bars distribute: the height the front fender ends up at is measured after the truck levels itself. Ram's manual includes the air suspension trucks in the same (H2-H1)/2+H1 procedure. Equal-i-zer's manual says a four-corner auto-leveling system can reduce distribution by 20 to 25 percent, so a setup made with the system on and driven with it on is under-distributed.

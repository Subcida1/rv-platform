Guide

Keep your RV tires alive through winter

Tires are the most ignored safety system on an RV, and the one you bet your family's life on at 65 mph. Winter storage kills them three ways: pressure loss, flat spotting, and standing water. All three are preventable.

Last reviewed: Sep 16, 2026, for the 2026-27 winter. We update these guides when real-world data changes.

The short version: set cold pressure before storage (not after a drive), get the weight off the tires if you can, use a breathable cover, and check the DOT date code, rubber over six or seven years is done regardless of tread.

Do this before you park it

1. Pressure: set it cold, and check it cold

Tire pressure drops roughly 1 psi per 10°F of temperature. Parked in September and checked in February, a properly-filled tire reads "low", and an underinflated tire overheats and fails in spring when it matters most.

  • Fill to the sidewall-cold pressure (or the inflation table in the owner's manual) before parking, when the tire is cold.
  • Re-check monthly through winter; top off as temps fall.
  • Don't over-inflate to "compensate" for cold, the spec IS the cold spec.

2. Flat spots: get the weight off if you can

A trailer sitting for months develops flat spots where the sidewall rests, then thumps down the highway until it warms out (or doesn't). Prevention, cheapest first:

  • Ideally: chassis jacks / jack stands at the frame, weight off all tires.
  • In-between: block the suspension and over-inflate ~10 psi for storage only, dropping back to spec before driving.
  • Minimum: move the rig a few inches forward on the blocks every few weeks so the contact patch shifts.
  • Never just "lower pressure and hope."

3. Covers: breathable only

Trapped moisture rots sidewalls and corrodes wheels. If you cover:

  • Use a purpose-made breathable tire cover (they're ventilated on purpose).
  • Never use plastic sheeting or a tarp wrapped tight, that's a greenhouse at the sidewall.
  • Covers also block UV, which is the sneaky third killer (check the sidewalls from underneath . UV cracks on the inner face mean the sun got them too).

4. Age: read the DOT code before you trust the tread

Find "DOT" molded on the sidewall, the last four digits are week/year of manufacture (e.g. 2619 = the 26th week of 2019). Industry practice: over six years old, replace even with tread to spare . RV tires age by calendar, not miles. Two different-looking tires from the same set will tell two different truths; check all of them. And because tire pressure spec assumes a correctly loaded RV weight, run the numbers with your real cargo, not the brochure weight.

The spring check

  □ Inspect sidewalls for UV cracks, bulges, and dry rot before airing up.
  □ Set cold pressure to spec (not the storage over-pressure).
  □ Re-torque lug nuts in the first 50 miles after re-parking cycles.
  □ If the DOT code says 6+ years: budget for tires before the trip.

Don't guess the load

Tire pressure spec assumes you're not grossly overloaded. Run the weight calculator with your real load, the tires are rated for weight plus speed plus margin, and all three matter.

Can I just leave it on leveling blocks and skip the jack stands?

Blocks reduce but don't remove the load, the tire still carries the rig. Jack stands at the frame are the only true weight-off. Blocks are acceptable for one season; stands are better for a long one.

What pressure is "spec" for my trailer?

The number molded on the sidewall is the maximum, not the recommendation. Use the inflation table in your owner's manual or the trailer's placard. When in doubt, a tire shop with the load/inflation chart is the right authority.

Is it worth moving the trailer monthly even if it's covered?

If you can't get weight off, yes, a few inches of movement shifts the contact patch enough to prevent a hard flat spot. It also gives you a reason to check pressure while you're out there.

RV roof leak repair: Finding it, and sealing it properly

Water on the ceiling is the last stop on a journey that started somewhere else on the roof. This is how to work back to the entry point, the four places it usually is, and how to seal it with the prep and the cure times the sealant maker publishes rather than what the tube suggests.

Last reviewed: Sep 24, 2026.

The short version: the stain is where the water came out, not where it got in, so start by working outward from the mark rather than at it. Then check the four places a roof leaks: every penetration and screw head, the front and rear cap seams, the hatches and skylights, and the join along each sidewall where the awning rail is bedded. Seal what you find with the same type of sealant that came out, on a surface cleaned of dirt, loose paint, rust, oil and grease, and leave it alone for the four hours the maker says it takes to become waterproof.

Start here: The stain is not the leak

Water runs before it drips

Water that gets past the roof does not fall straight down to the first thing under it. It follows the framing, the inside face of the ceiling panel and the wiring runs, and it stops where gravity, a low point or a joist finally lets it through. The mark on the ceiling is therefore the exit, and the hole is often feet away in a direction nobody guesses: forward under a vent, sideways along a seam, or down a wall cavity to a stain that looks like a window leak.

Two things follow from that. Start at the mark and work outward, checking the nearest roof feature in every direction before you open a single tube of sealant. And when you find a leak you did not expect, do not stop at the first one you can fix: water that has been travelling for months usually means more than one entry point has opened in the same weather.

The four places a roof actually leaks

Almost every roof leak is at a discontinuity: a place where something passes through the membrane, or where two panels or two materials meet. There are four groups of them, and the order below is the order worth checking.

  • Penetrations. Roof vents, the black tank vent pipe, the air conditioner, antenna mounts, and every screw head in the membrane. Sealant fails at the flange first, because that is where the membrane flexes and where water stands.
  • The front and rear cap seams. Where the fibreglass front cap or rear wall meets the roof, there is a long seam with a trim strip bedded onto it. It takes the full force of the wind at highway speed, which is why this is the seam that opens first on a coach that lives outdoors.
  • Hatches and skylights. A hatches hinges and a skylight does not, and both depend on a bead of sealant around a flange that expands and contracts with the sun.
  • The sidewall transition. Along each side of the roof the membrane turns down into a trim strip, with the awning rail usually bedded into the same run. Water gets in at the top edge of the trim, runs behind it, and shows up as a wall stain or a soft spot along the floor.

Finding it

The hose test, from the bottom up

Once the roof is wet, the evidence is gone, so work deliberately and one area at a time:

  • Start low. Test the sidewall transition along one side first, then the rear, then the front, and only then the penetrations on top. Water runs downhill, so the lowest suspect is the one most likely to be responsible for a stain near the floor.
  • One area, then stop and look inside. Run a gentle hose, no pressure nozzle, on a single two or three foot section for five minutes. A spray nozzle drives water sideways and creates leaks that do not exist.
  • Have somebody inside with a torch. The water usually shows at the framing before it shows on the ceiling, and a wet run along a joist is the pointer to where it came from.
  • Work uphill from the place you find it, until you reach the point where the water stops appearing.

Reading the inside of the coach

A stain is the obvious one. The three that matter as much and get missed are a soft patch in the ceiling or floor, a trim strip that has come loose or feels spongy behind the wallpaper, and a damp smell in a cupboard that has no visible water. All three mean water has been arriving for a while, which changes what the job is: see the section below on what a sealant cannot fix.

When the leak only appears when you drive

A leak that never shows in the rain at a standstill but appears after a wet drive is being driven in by airflow. That points at the front cap seam, the sidewall transition, and anything on the leading edge of a roof feature rather than at a penetration in the open field of the roof. Test it the same way, but with the leak in mind that the water is being pushed rather than falling.

Sealing it

Use the sealant the roof was built with

Jayco's owner manual gives the rule plainly: Always use the same type of sealant that was removed. The roof's membrane decides which type that is, and the sealant makers publish the compatibility: Dicor's self-levelling lap sealant is made for EPDM, TPO and PVC membranes, and it adheres to aluminium, wood, vinyl, galvanized metal, fibreglass and concrete.

It is worth knowing what lap sealant is for, because it is not a general-purpose caulk. Dicor describes it as creating a secure, secondary seal along the roof's edges, air vents, vent pipes and screw heads: secondary, in the sense that the mechanical fastener or the gasket underneath is the primary seal and the sealant is there to cover its edges. A penetration whose only seal is a bead of sealant is a penetration waiting to leak.

Self-levelling on flat, non-sag on vertical

The maker's own product line separates the two, and the label on the product says which is which: self-levelling is for horizontal surfaces. It flows out to a flat bead and fills the shape of a flange, which is exactly right on a roof and exactly wrong on a vertical seam or a sidewall trim, where it runs. A vertical edge wants a non-sag sealant, usually applied with a smoothing tool rather than left to find its own level.

The prep the maker insists on

This is Dicor's own direction, and skipping any of it is why reseals fail in a season: Clean surface of all dirt, loose paint, rust, oil and grease. Allow surface to dry before applying sealant. Use standard caulking equipment to apply sealant. In cold weather, warm container at room temperature before use.

The practical version: take off anything already loose with a scraper and then a plastic brush, wipe the area with the cleaner the sealant maker or the membrane maker recommends, let it dry completely, and only then lay a new bead that overlaps the old sound sealant on both sides. There is no adhesion between sealant and dirt, and no adhesion between sealant and damp membrane.

Cure times, and what waterproof in four hours means

The maker publishes a cure table for its self-levelling lap sealant, at 50 to 70 degrees Fahrenheit:

Time after applicationWhat it means
About 5 minutesSkins over
About 4 hoursWaterproof
About 48 hours80 percent cured
About 30 daysFully cured

Rain after a few hours is survivable. Traffic is not. A sealant that is waterproof at four hours is still soft for days, so walking on the new bead, dragging a hose across it, or letting a branch scrape it inside that month can lift it. The other half of the table matters the same way: in cold weather it cures slower, which is why the maker says to warm the container to room temperature before use.

And the caution is the maker's, not a formality. Dicor's own warning says to keep the sealant away from heat, sparks and flame, to keep fresh air moving while it is applied and while it dries, and that it irritates the nose, throat, eyes and skin. Roof sealants are solvent-based products, and a roof with no airflow over it is the worst place to use one without thinking about it.

The inspection that prevents the next one

Every six months, in the maker's own words

Jayco's owner manual is specific about the cadence and the reason: Sealants perform a very important function and should be inspected closely and regularly maintained. Its instruction to owners is to inspect all sealants a minimum of every six months, with the note that a quick walk around before a trip helps, and it says why there is no fixed life to rely on: sealants do not have "set" lifetimes. Varying environmental factors affect the pliability and adhesiveness of sealants. The same section names those factors: ultraviolet exposure, air pollution, freezing temperatures and exposure to the elements.

It also gives a first-year instruction that is easy to miss: cap seal all trim and openings at least once after the first year, and thereafter as cracks, peeling, lifting and shrinkage appear.

What to look for: Cracks, voids, lifting, shrinkage

The manual's list is the inspection checklist, and it is short enough to memorise: Have the sealant replaced if you notice any cracks, peeling, voids, gaps, breaks, looseness or any sign of physical deterioration. In practice, on the roof, that means looking for four things at every fitting:

  • A void or a gap, where the bead has pulled away from the flange or the membrane. This is the one that leaks today.
  • Lifting at the edge, where the bead has curled up and the surface under it is visible.
  • Cracking or crazing across the surface of the bead, which usually means it is dried out and near the end of being useful.
  • Shrinkage, a bead that has pulled back from where it was laid and is now narrower than the joint it covers.

The ladder matters, and the maker says so. Use a stepladder placed alongside the vehicle and do not prop a ladder against the body, because it damages the finish. The roof itself has decking under the membrane and can be walked on with caution, which is the maker's own description of it.

When it is not a sealant job

Sealant repairs the way water gets in. It does not repair what water has already done, and three signs say the job is now bigger than a bead of lap sealant:

  • A soft spot in the roof or the floor. That is decking that has been wet long enough to delaminate or rot. Sealant over it stops nothing, because the water is already inside the structure, and the repair means opening the roof or the floor and replacing the material.
  • A wall that has gone soft or wavy behind the wallpaper. This is delamination, and it is the most expensive outcome of a small leak that ran for a season.
  • A stain that keeps growing after a reseal. Either the reseal missed the entry point, or there is a second one. Either way, the next step is the hose test again rather than another tube of sealant.

None of that is a reason to avoid a reseal, because the reseal is what stops the process getting worse. It is the reason to look at the inside of the coach before deciding the job is finished.

What it costs

The parts here are the cheapest on the site: a tube of lap sealant and a tube of non-sag sealant cost about as little as anything you can buy for a coach, and a roof's worth of sealant is still a small number. The variation is all in the two things you cannot put in a tube: access and what the water has already reached.

The cheapest version is a handful of fittings resealed from a ladder in an afternoon, by an owner who has already worked out where the water is getting in. The middle is a full roof inspection and reseal, which is labour rather than material: every penetration, both cap seams and both sidewall transitions, with the old sealant scraped and cleaned before anything new goes on.

The expensive shape is decking. Once the roof surface has to come up, or a wall has to be opened to replace delaminated panel, the number is decided by access and by how long the leak ran, not by the sealant. That is the practical argument for the six-month inspection: it is the same job every time, and it is the one that keeps the roof on the cheap side of that line.

Why is my RV ceiling wet when I cannot find a hole in the roof?

Because roof water runs before it drips. Water that gets in at a vent flange or a cap seam travels along the framing and the inside of the ceiling panel until something stops it, and the stain appears where it finally came through rather than where it entered. That is why the leak is often several feet from the mark, and why finding it means working outward from the stain rather than standing on it.

How often should an RV roof be resealed?

Jayco's owner manual instructs owners to inspect all sealants a minimum of every six months, and to replace the sealant when there are cracks, peeling, voids, gaps, breaks or looseness. Its manual also states that sealants do not have set lifetimes, because ultraviolet exposure, air pollution and freezing temperatures change how pliable and how adhesive they stay. The same manual says to cap seal all trim and openings at least once after the first year.

What sealant should I use on an RV roof?

The same type that was removed, which is the rule Jayco's manual gives, and the roof's membrane decides which type that is. Dicor's self-levelling lap sealant is compatible with EPDM, TPO and PVC membranes, and its own product line separates self-levelling sealant, for horizontal surfaces, from non-sag sealant, for vertical seams. A vertical seam sealed with a self-levelling product runs.

Can I seal over the old sealant?

The maker's directions for use require a surface cleaned of all dirt, loose paint, rust, oil and grease, and allowed to dry before the sealant goes on. Sealant that has already lifted, cracked or gone loose is in the way of that, so it comes off first. Sealing over a failed seal traps the water that is already getting in and hides the failure from the next inspection.

How long before RV roof sealant is waterproof?

Dicor publishes the cure times for its self-levelling lap sealant at 50 to 70 degrees Fahrenheit: it skins over in about five minutes, becomes waterproof in about four hours, reaches 80 percent cure in about 48 hours, and is fully cured in about 30 days. So rain a few hours after the job is survivable and traffic on the roof during the first weeks is not ideal. In cold weather the maker says to warm the container to room temperature before use.

RV pin weight and payload: Which number actually limits you

The definitions that decide whether a truck can tow a given trailer are federal, and they are quoted below. The number that surprises owners is pin weight, and the reason it surprises them is that four manufacturers publish four different figures. The one that binds you is not a percentage from the internet. It is printed on your hitch.

By OriginRV. Last updated on Oct 2, 2026.

The short version: GVWR, GAWR and curb weight are definitions from federal regulation, not conventions. Payload is a legal figure and the trailer's tongue or pin weight counts against it. For a fifth wheel, makers publish 15 percent, 20 percent, 22 percent and 15 to 25 percent depending on who you ask, so the number that actually limits you is your hitch's and pin box's own vertical weight rating, which is stamped on the hardware.

The ratings are federal, not marketing

Manufacturers do not invent these numbers. The regulation that governs the certification label is 49 CFR 567.4, and it requires the label to state, among other things: "'Gross Vehicle Weight Rating' or 'GVWR' followed by the appropriate value in pounds, which shall not be less than the sum of the unloaded vehicle weight, rated cargo load, and 150 pounds times the number of the vehicle's designated seating positions." And "'Gross Axle Weight Rating' or 'GAWR,' followed by the appropriate value in pounds, for each axle."

The definitions themselves come from 49 CFR 571.3, and reading them settles most arguments about what a rating means.

TermFederal definition
GVWR"the value specified by the manufacturer as the loaded weight of a single vehicle"
GAWR"the value specified by the vehicle manufacturer as the load-carrying capacity of a single axle system, as measured at the tire-ground interfaces"
Curb weight"the weight of a motor vehicle with standard equipment; maximum capacity of engine fuel, oil, and coolant; and, if so equipped, air conditioning and additional weight optional engine"
Unloaded vehicle weight"the weight of a vehicle with maximum capacity of all fluids necessary for operation of the vehicle, but without cargo, occupants, or accessories that are ordinarily removed from the vehicle when they are not in use"

Note what GVWR is and is not. It is the loaded weight of the vehicle, and it is set by the manufacturer. Nothing you bolt to the truck changes it, which is why heavier springs, airbags and bigger tyres do not raise the figure you have to stay under. They may change how the truck carries a load. They do not change a federal rating.

Payload is a legal figure, and the trailer counts against it

The door-jamb sticker is a federal requirement too. 49 CFR 571.110 requires the vehicle capacity weight to be stated as "The combined weight of occupants and cargo should never exceed XXX kilograms or XXX pounds."

That single sentence is why the advertised "max tow rating" is not the number that limits a half-ton truck. The occupants, the fuel, the accessories, the hitch, and the downward load of the trailer all consume that figure. A trailer's tongue weight or pin weight is load on the truck, and it spends payload before it spends anything else.

There is a companion rule for trailers, and it explains why a fifth wheel's pin weight matters to the trailer maker as well: "On RV trailers, the sum of the GAWRs of all axles on the vehicle plus the vehicle manufacturer's recommended tongue weight must not be less than the GVWR. If tongue weight is specified as a range, the minimum value must be used."

The same section is also where the trade acronyms get their legal names. The label titles are "MOTOR HOME OCCUPANT AND CARGO CARRYING CAPACITY" and "RECREATION VEHICLE TRAILER CARGO CARRYING CAPACITY", which is where "OCCC" and "CCC" come from.

The RV industry's own definitions of UVW, CCC and OCCC are inside standards that must be purchased. RVIA's public pages list the acronyms without defining them, and the binding text sits in NFPA 1192 and ANSI/RVIA A119.5 behind a paywall. The free and binding definition of unloaded vehicle weight is the federal one above, so that is the one to use.

Pin weight: four makers, four answers

This is the part owners find most confusing, and the confusion is not their fault. Manufacturers rate their own hardware and publish their own expected range, and the figures do not agree.

SourceWhat they state
Ford, towing guide"Trailer tongue (trailer king pin for 5th-wheel towing) load weight should be 10% (15% for 5th-wheel and gooseneck towing) of total loaded trailer weight."
Reese, 16K fifth wheel hitch manual"Approximately 15%-25% of trailer weight should be on hitch (Pin Weight)."
Lippert, Turning Point pin box"Pin weight of 2,000 lbs (20 percent maximum of Step 1.C.)"
Curt, Helux pin box"Pin weight can also be approximated by multiplying the GVWR of the 5th wheel by 22% (0.22)."

Fifteen, twenty, twenty-two, and fifteen to twenty-five. These are all manufacturers of the parts involved, and they are all describing the same physical quantity. The reason they differ is that each is rating its own product rather than defining a universal rule.

So the binding number is not the percentage. It is the vertical load rating of your specific hitch and pin box, which Reese and Lippert and Curt each publish as a maximum for each part number. Reese's 16,000 pound hitch states "King pin weight must not exceed 4,000 pounds". Lippert's pin box is rated to a specific pin weight. Curt publish a minimum and maximum pin weight per part number, with an optimal range between them. Find that rating on your own hardware and use it, because it is the number your setup was built to take.

How it is expressed varies, which is worth knowing when you go looking: pin weight, king pin weight, kingpin vertical weight, vertical pin weight, or simply hitch weight. They all mean the downward force the trailer's pin box applies to the fifth wheel head.

A weight-distribution hitch does not change your tongue weight

This is a widely believed correction that turns out to be a manufacturer's explicit statement. Ford's towing guide says plainly: "Trailer towing values are the same for weight-carrying and weight-distributing hitches."

What a weight-distributing hitch does is move load onto the front axle rather than reduce it. Ford's own target for that is specific: "If using load bars for weight distribution, Ford recommends 50% front axle load restoration (FALR)."

So the hitch changes how the load is carried across the axles, not how much of it there is. It does not increase the trailer's tongue weight, it does not reduce the truck's payload consumption, and it does not raise the GVWR.

How to weigh it, the maker's own procedure

You do not have to estimate, and a manufacturer publishes the method. Curt's pin box manual: "The best way to attain the pin weight is to take your 5th wheel to a certifed scale, i.e. CAT Scale." Their procedure then reads:

  1. Connect the fifth wheel and drive onto the scale, front axle on Scale 1, rear axle on Scale 2, trailer axles on Scale 3.
  2. Note those weights and total the first two.
  3. Disconnect the fifth wheel and weigh the tow vehicle alone on scales 1 and 2.
  4. Total those two.
  5. Subtract step 4 from step 2. That difference is your actual hitch weight.

Ford offer the same advice for the simpler case: "we recommend visiting a weight station or public scale at a truck stop." On vehicles with onboard scales, Ford note that the trailer tongue load can be measured directly.

What the weighing gives you that a brochure cannot is the load as built and loaded, with your gear, your water and your accessories aboard. Two identical trailers leave the factory with the same rating and arrive at the scale with different numbers.

The order to work in

Start with the yellow door-jamb sticker, not the brochure and not the advertisement, because the sticker is the federal statement and the advertisement is configuration-specific. Subtract the occupants, the fuel, the accessories and the hitch from that payload figure. What is left is your real remaining capacity.

Then look at the trailer's GVWR rather than its dry weight, since dry weight excludes the battery, the propane and the water you will actually carry. Work out the tongue or pin load from the loaded trailer.

Then read your own hitch's rating, because that is the number that binds. And finally weigh the combination, because every estimate above is a starting figure and the scale is the only measurement.

Winterize your RV plumbing before the freeze

Frozen pipes crack. Then they leak where you can't see them for months. This is the one winter chore that's better done a week early than an hour before the temperature drops.

Last reviewed: Sep 16, 2026 — for the 2026–27 winter. We update these guides when real-world data changes.

The short version: drain the tanks, bypass the water heater, run pink RV antifreeze through every line and both faucets, and walk away confident it'll survive.

Do this, in order

1. Drain the fresh tank & water heater

Open the fresh tank drain and the low-point drains (red = hot line, blue = cold). Open both faucets to let air in, and pull the water heater anode rod / drain plug so the tank empties — then leave the plug out until spring. A partially full water heater is the classic first thing to crack. (Need to check your towing weight before you move the rig this spring — do both chores in the same afternoon.)

2. Bypass the water heater

Find the bypass valves (three of them, usually behind the water heater access panel). Shut the inlet and outlet valves, open the bypass loop. This keeps pink antifreeze out of the tank — which both wastes gallons and settles as sediment. If you skipped step 1, do it now.

3. Dump both waste tanks

Black and gray. Water left in either can freeze and warp the tank or the valve. Dump, flush, and leave the valves open or cracked so nothing collects.

4. Pull the inline filter (if you have one)

A water filter full of water will freeze and split its housing. Remove the cartridge, store it inside, and put a bypass cap on the housing or leave it open.

5. Run pink RV antifreeze through everything

Standard RV antifreeze is propylene glycol — designed for drinking-water systems, non-toxic. Use the water pump to pull it from a jug (a 3-gallon pour into the fresh tank works if your pump won't self-prime). Method:

  • Open the farthest faucet until pink flows, then close.
  • Repeat hot and cold, every faucet, plus the outdoor shower if you have one.
  • Run the toilet flush, an outside spray port, and any ice-maker line.
  • Finish at the nearest faucet — the pump and inlet line are the last to protect.

Never use automotive antifreeze — ethylene glycol is poison and will ruin the system.

6. The alternative some techs use: blow it out

Compressed air (about 25–30 psi through the city-water inlet, with every valve open) drys the lines instead of filling them. It's cleaner than pink stuff and works — but it has to be done well, and a single trapped pocket can still freeze. Antifreeze is the forgiving option for most owners; blow-out is for the fastidious.

7. Protect the wet bay

If your rig has a heated wet bay, clean the strainers and confirm the heater works; if not, insulate the compartment and leave a 60W-ish incandescent bulb or a small heater on colder nights — but only if the setup is safe to leave unattended. Many owners simply winterize the whole bay.

The payoff

One cold afternoon now beats $1,500 of PEX and cabinet work in March. Check each item off as you go — the order above is the order that saves you.

Can I leave the water heater full if it never gets below 32°F?

If you're certain the bay can't freeze, sure. But draining is free and forgetting costs a tank. Most techs drain year-round.

How much antifreeze do I need?

About 2–3 gallons for a typical travel trailer, more for larger rigs with extra loops (outdoor showers, washers). Buy the checklists' worst case: 3 gallons.

The pink stuff smells for weeks in spring. Why?

It sits in the lines until you flush. Flush by filling the tank with fresh water, running every faucet until pink is gone (taste-test the kitchen cold), then drain once more. Don't use bleach in the same pass as propylene glycol.

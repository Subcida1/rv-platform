Propane safety guide

RV Propane Detector Beeping: What Each Signal Means

A propane detector that will not stop beeping has four possible causes and only one of them is propane. The pattern of the light and the beep tells you which one you have, and two of the four are not faults at all.

By OriginRV. Last reviewed on Oct 16, 2026.

The short version: read the light before you do anything else, because the pattern tells you which of four things is happening. A constant tone with a flashing red light is propane: ventilate, shut the gas off at the tank, and do not re-enter until it clears. An alternating red and green light with one short beep is a fault or low voltage, and low voltage is the common one, because the detector runs normally down to 8 volts and a tired house battery reads exactly like a broken alarm. Red red green green with a beep every 25 to 30 seconds is the end of the sensor's life, which every propane alarm reaches five years after it was made. And if you have pressed the test button to reassure yourself, know what it tested: the button checks the alarm circuit, not the sensor.

First: read the light, then decide

A propane detector is not a smoke alarm with a different label. It is a sensor that measures the concentration of propane in the air near the floor, and it reports what it finds through one light and one sounder. The makers publish the patterns, and they are distinct enough that the pattern alone tells you what to do.

Two of the four are not faults. That is the part most owners never learn, and it is why a detector that beeps at two in the morning is often doing its job rather than failing at it.

Constant tone and a flashing red light: this is propane

The alarm sounds when the concentration crosses the threshold the maker sets. MTI's figure is "< 25% of the LEL of Propane", which is a quarter of the lowest concentration that can burn. That is not a trace. It is a real leak, and the instruction is to act on it.

MTI's procedures during an alarm, in its own order: shut off the gas supply at the tank, extinguish every flame and every cigarette, do not operate electrical switches or appliances, open the doors and windows, and leave. Do not re-enter until the alarm has cleared and the coach has been ventilated. Then have the system checked by a qualified technician before you use it again.

The alarm will keep sounding until you press the mute button or the gas clears. The mute is not a fix and it is not permanent: MTI's cycle is "about 90 seconds", and "If dangerous gas levels remain after the end of the Mute cycle (about 90 seconds) the Alarm will reactivate."

Alternating red and green with one beep: a fault, or the battery

This is the signal that sends most people shopping for a detector they do not need. MTI describes it in one sentence that names both causes together: "A fault in the sensors operation or low battery voltage will activate the malfunction Alarm. The LED will alternate Red / Green and sound one short beep."

The voltage cause is the common one, and the maker publishes the threshold: "This Detector will operate normally down to 8vDC. Low voltage will activate the malfunction alarm." A house battery that has been sitting, a converter that is not charging, or a corroded ground stud all produce the same pattern as a failed sensor.

MTI's own instruction for this signal is to check the voltage first: "IN THE EVENT OF A MALFUNCTION ALARM check the batteries voltage. If the voltage is above 8v DC, call 1-800-383-0269." The maker also adds a safety instruction rather than a warranty one: "Do not Use your RV without a working Gas Detector installed."

Red red green green: the sensor has reached the end of its life

This one has no fix. The sensor has a finite life and the detector announces the end of it: "The signal is the LED flashing RED RED GREEN GREEN with a beep every 25-30 seconds."

The signal can be silenced, but only temporarily, and the maker gives the exact terms: "The EOL Signal may be reset by pushing TEST / RESET button on the alarm. This will reset the EOL Signal for a period of 72 hours (3 days) for a total of up to 30 days. After 30 days the signal cannot be reset and the alarm must be replaced."

One instruction goes with it, and it is the practical one: "DO NOT DISCONNECT THE ALARM UNTIL YOU HAVE A REPLACEMENT ALARM AVAILABLE TO INSTALL." A coach with the detector unplugged has no gas detection at all, which is a worse position than a detector that beeps.

Flashing green for two minutes after power-up: nothing is wrong

Every time the detector is powered, it runs a warm-up. The light flashes green and the unit cannot detect anything until the warm-up finishes. MTI states the limit plainly: "This Detector cannot detect propane gas or sound the Alarm for two minutes after it has been powered up."

On a combination carbon monoxide and propane alarm the warm-up is longer and the two sensors differ. Safe-T-Alert's 35 Series needs "a ten (10) minute initial warm-up period" for the CO sensor and about "1 minute" for the gas sensor, and the unit cannot alarm during either.

So a detector that flashes green for a couple of minutes after you reconnect the battery is starting up, not failing. The one thing to check is that it stops.

The signal that is almost always the battery

The alternating red and green pattern has two causes and the maker lists the voltage first in its own troubleshooting instruction. That ordering is worth following, because the battery is the cheap answer and the sensor is the expensive one.

What the detector needs, and what a tired battery does

The detector runs on the coach's 12 volt system and it is not fussy about much, but it has a floor. For reference on the unit itself, MTI's 30 Series is UL 1484 listed for gas alarms for residential and RV use, and its audible output is 85 dB at 10 feet. MTI's figure is 8 volts DC, and below it the unit reports a malfunction rather than going quiet. That is deliberate: a detector that simply stopped working at low voltage would be silent in exactly the conditions where a coach is most likely to have a problem.

Trojan Battery publishes the state-of-charge table this reads against: a flooded battery at rest sits at 12.10 volts at 50 percent and 12.73 at full. So a reading that low at the alarm means something is wrong upstream rather than at the detector. The usual causes are a battery that has been sitting without a charge, a converter that is not putting out, or a ground connection that has corroded.

Where to measure it

Measure at the alarm's own terminals rather than at the battery. The detector is at the end of a run that includes a fuse and a ground path, and a voltage drop along that run is one of the things that produces this signal. A reading above 8 volts at the alarm with the malfunction still showing is the point at which MTI says to call the maker, and a reading below it is the point at which to charge the battery and look at the connections.

The detector's own wiring is part of the same check. MTI's 20 Series specifies a 1 amp in-line fuse on a circuit fused at no more than 15 amps, and the caution is not to connect the alarm to a wall-switch-controlled outlet, because a detector that can be switched off is a detector that will be off.

The test button, and what it does not test

Every propane detector has a test button and almost every owner presses it and feels reassured. The makers are unusually direct about what that button does, and MTI prints it in capitals: "THE TEST BUTTON ONLY TESTS THE ALARM CIRCUIT, NOT THE SENSOR."

What the button verifies is that the unit has power, that the wiring is intact, and that the horn and the light work. What it does not verify is whether the sensor can still detect propane. A detector that passes the test every week for five years can still be a detector that stopped sensing two years ago, which is the reason the five-year replacement rule exists and the reason the end-of-life signal is not optional.

The testing instruction is the same across the range: test after storage, before each trip, and at least once a week while the coach is in use. MTI's wording is "TEST THE OPERATION OF THIS ALARM AFTER THE RECREATIONAL VEHICLE HAS BEEN IN STORAGE, BEFORE EACH TRIP AND AT LEAST ONCE PER WEEK DURING USE."

There is a way to test the sensor itself, and it is not the button. MTI's instruction is to "USE PROPANE OR BUTANE GAS TO TEST THE SENSOR", and it notes that "it may take up to 10 seconds for the alarm to sound." An unlit propane torch held near the detector, with the gas flowing and no flame, is the field version of that test. It is the only check that answers the question the button cannot.

When the detector is finished, and how to date it

The five-year figure is not a suggestion and it is not a warranty period. It is the sensor's service life, published by the maker, and the detector is designed to announce the end of it.

The five-year service life

MTI's 20 Series manual carries it in the section heading: "End of Life Signal - The Sensor Has a 5 Year Service Life" The 70 Series says the same thing in its own words: "This unit has an End of Life (EOL) signal that will sound after 5 years of use." And the consumer page states it as a product fact rather than a caveat: "Every Safe-T-Alert alarm has a 5-year service life and should be replaced proactively to ensure reliable protection."

The reason is the sensor rather than the electronics. An electrochemical gas sensor consumes itself as it works, and its output drifts as it ages, which is why a detector can pass every electrical test and still be unable to smell propane.

The production date on the back of the case

The clock starts when the unit was made, not when you bought the coach. MTI prints a production date on the back of the case, and its instruction for a unit whose installation date is unknown is direct: "If you don't know the installation date, replace the alarm no later than 5 years after the production date on the back of the alarm."

That is the rule to use on a used coach. Take the detector off the wall, read the date on the back, and add five years.

The fallback rule for a coach you did not buy new

For a new coach the maker asks for the month and year the RV was manufactured. For anything else it gives a fallback that resolves to a date rather than to a shrug: "If you are not sure, enter the month and year you purchased the RV or enter May and the model year of the RV (example May 2020), whichever is the earlier date."

Whichever rule you land on, the record-keeping instruction is the useful habit: two self-adhesive labels ship with every alarm, one for beside the unit and one for near a fresh air source, and the replace-by date goes on both.

False alarms, and the gases that cause them

A propane detector is not a propane-specific instrument. It responds to a class of flammable gases, and the makers say so rather than leaving it to be discovered.

Safe-T-Alert's wording is the clearest: "Other explosive gases such as Acetone, Alcohol, Butane, and Gasoline may cause false alarms." That list matters in a coach, because every one of those things is commonly aboard. Aerosol sprays, solvents, adhesive fumes, paint, and fuel vapours from a generator or a gas can in a compartment can all produce a reading.

The makers also build in a delay for exactly this reason. Safe-T-Alert describes it as "a recheck cycle before alarming", added "To prevent false alarms from brief "puffs" of gas", and the alarm sounds only if the level is still high when the recheck finishes. A detector that alarms the moment a cabinet is opened would be useless, so it does not.

The practical test is the same one the alarm response uses. Ventilate, let it clear, and watch whether it comes back. A reading that clears and stays cleared was a puff of something. A reading that returns after ventilation is a leak, and the instruction for that is the same as for any alarm: shut the gas off and have the system inspected.

Where the detector is mounted, and why it matters

Propane is heavier than air. It settles, which is why the detector is mounted low and why a detector mounted high will not do its job however well it works.

MTI's instruction is specific: "Propane is heavier than air. For propane gas detection, install this Detector from 4 to 20 inches off the floor." The maker also lists where not to put it, and the list is about airflow rather than aesthetics: not behind furniture, drapes, or in closets, and not in areas that block air reaching the sensor.

Two more placement notes apply here. The detector should not be on a wall-switch-controlled outlet or circuit breaker, because a detector someone can switch off is one that will be. And it is not a substitute for the other alarms: MTI states that "This Detector is not a smoke or fire Alarm. It is also not a carbon monoxide alarm." A coach needs each of the three, and a combination unit covers two of them rather than all three.

What it costs

The cost of this fault is decided by which signal you are reading, and the two cheapest outcomes are the two most common.

At the bottom is the diagnosis and the fix it leads to: a battery that needed charging, a converter that needed looking at, a ground stud that needed cleaning, or a puff of aerosol that needed ventilating. None of that is a part, and the alternating red and green pattern is the one that most often lands here.

In the middle is the replacement detector, which is the answer for the end-of-life signal and for a genuine sensor fault. The mounting bracket on most current units is designed to fit the hole the old one leaves, so it is a swap rather than a rebuild. None of the makers publishes a price, and none of them sells the sensor separately from the alarm, so the replacement is the whole unit.

At the top is a real leak, and the cost of that is a propane system inspection rather than a part. It is also the outcome that the other three signals can mask, which is the argument for replacing a detector that has reached its date rather than resetting it until the beeping stops.

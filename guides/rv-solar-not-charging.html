Troubleshooting guide

RV solar not charging: Panel or controller

Solar is three things in a row, and you can measure each one separately. Panel, controller, battery. Find which link is not passing power along and you are done. Two of the answers say nothing is broken at all.

By OriginRV. Last reviewed on Oct 16, 2026.

The short version: solar runs in parallel with your converter rather than replacing it, so it never touches the 120 volt side. The path is panel, then charge controller, then battery, and you can measure each one. Before hunting a fault, check two things that look like failures and are not: the battery may simply be full, and the controller may be set for the wrong battery chemistry.

Check these two things before you test anything

Both of these produce the complaint "solar has stopped charging", and neither is a fault.

One: The battery is full

Victron lists this first in its own troubleshooting order, and Go Power documents it too: once the battery is full, the controller will stop or significantly reduce its charge current, and that is normal rather than a fault. If your controller shows little or nothing in the afternoon after working all morning, that is the system doing its job. Check the battery's actual state rather than the controller display.

Two: The controller is set for the wrong battery chemistry

Zamp's own manual states plainly that its controllers ship with the default setting on AGM, and carries the warning that an incorrect battery type setting may damage your battery. If you've installed lithium and left the controller on the lead-acid profile, the controller will run equalization and temperature compensation that a lithium bank must not see, and the battery will shut the charge down rather than accept it. That looks exactly like a failing panel. This is a setting rather than a fault, so check it before you test anything electrical.

Related, and easy to miss: Victron warns that lithium batteries are prone to damage when charged below about 5 degrees Celsius, which is 41 Fahrenheit. The physical limit underneath that is freezing point: below 32 Fahrenheit, charging does damage the cells. So a battery that stops accepting charge on a cold morning is protecting itself rather than failing. Controllers handle it differently, and some will show an error rather than simply going quiet.

The causes, in Victron's order

Victron publishes its own ranked list of why solar stops charging. The causes are: the battery is fully charged; reversed panel polarity; panel voltage too high; reversed battery polarity; the controller being disconnected from the battery through a cable, fuse or breaker problem; incorrect charger configuration such as a low charge voltage or current setting; the charger being externally controlled by a system such as an energy management feature; and the temperature compensation feature misbehaving or being misconfigured with a battery that is too hot.

On your own RV, the order to check is:

The battery is already full

First, because it is free, and because a full battery is the first thing Victron's list names.

The controller setting is wrong

Second, because it is also free and it will quietly undercharge your bank for months if you leave it.

A fuse or breaker in the solar circuit

Victron's fifth cause is the charger disconnected from the battery through a cable, a fuse or a breaker. Controllers are often fitted with a fuse between the controller and the battery, and it is easy not to know it is there. Check it before concluding the controller is dead.

Shading

Even a small shadow on part of a panel can greatly reduce its output. Go Power's own wording: an object as small as a broomstick held across the solar module may cause the power output to be reduced. A new antenna, a roof vent, a tree that grew, or even a cable draped across a panel can do it. If output dropped suddenly without any work being done, look up before you look at the wiring.

Wiring faults come next: loose wires, loose connections and badly crimped connectors. Solar connectors that were crimped by hand rather than with the right tool are a weak point worth checking on any system that has been worked on.

Reversed polarity, panel side or battery side. Worth checking specifically if someone has recently worked on the system, because reconnecting things is how it happens.

Testing it in three measurements

Solar is easy to test because there are only three points, and each one has an expected reading.

Measurement one: What the controller is receiving

Start at the controller, with everything connected and the sun on the array. Measure the voltage at the controller's solar input terminals. If the array's operating voltage is there, the panel and its wiring are delivering and you can leave them alone. If it is missing, or far below the battery voltage, the fault is upstream of the controller: isolate the panel to test it. Victron's 5 volt rule is the figure on its own MPPT chargers: charging begins when the panel voltage is 5 volts above the battery voltage, and continues while it stays 1 volt above. A PWM controller needs far less than that and starts as soon as the panel is above the battery at all, which is why the two types behave differently on an overcast day. Either condition may simply not be met in poor light, and that is a reason for no charging rather than a fault.

Measurement two: What the controller is sending

Measure at the controller's battery terminals, on the terminals rather than on the lugs. That is what the controller is sending, and while the sun is out it should be tracking the battery up through bulk and absorption. A reading there at or below the resting battery voltage, with the array delivering into the controller, means the controller is not passing power along.

Measurement three: The panel, isolated

Only if Measurement one showed nothing at the controller's input is this worth doing, because it means taking the array apart. Cover the panels first, with a blanket or a sheet of cardboard, because a panel in sunlight is a live source that cannot be switched off. Then disconnect them and measure open circuit voltage against the figures on the panel's label. A reading much lower than the value on the label means the panel is the suspect rather than the wiring. Short circuit current is the other figure on the label, and it is the one to be careful with: shorting a panel through a multimeter draws an arc when the connection is broken, and most meters are fused at 10 amps, which a larger array will exceed. A DC clamp meter on a working circuit measures current without any of that.

Can injure you A panel in sunlight is a live source you cannot switch off. Cover the array with a blanket or a sheet of cardboard before you disconnect anything, keep your hands off the terminals, and never short a panel through a meter: breaking that connection draws an arc, and a larger array exceeds the 10 amp fuse most meters carry.

Putting it back together is where controllers get damaged. Renogy, Go Power and Zamp's current install guides all state the same order: the battery goes onto the controller first and the panel second, which means the panel comes off first when the system is taken apart. A panel connected to a controller with no battery on it exposes the controller to the panel's full open-circuit voltage instead of a battery-clamped input, which is the cause Renogy names for the damage, and on a unit that senses the system voltage automatically it can also leave the controller set for the wrong one. The battery is what powers the controller up, gives it the reference it regulates against, and tells it which system voltage it is working on.

What the combination tells you

Nothing at the controller's input means the fault is upstream: the panel, its wiring, or a connection. Input present but nothing coming out of the controller means the controller is the fault. Output present at the controller but a lower voltage at the bank means the wire between them, and the fix is often a thicker or shorter cable rather than a new part.

Victron's wiring guidance puts a number on it: an excessive voltage drop, more than 2.5 percent, is unacceptable anywhere in the system. That is a power-systems figure rather than a solar-specific one, but it applies directly. On long solar runs voltage drop is what to measure, and it shows up as a system that works but never performs to its rating.

PWM and MPPT, and why it matters to a fault

There are two types of charge controller and they behave differently enough that it affects what you should expect to see.

MPPT actively hunts for the array's maximum power point, which means it can harvest meaningfully more energy, particularly when the panel voltage is well above the battery voltage or in cooler conditions. It effectively decouples the array voltage from the battery voltage, which is why it keeps harvesting where a PWM controller cannot.

PWM is simpler and cheaper and effectively connects the panel closer to the battery, so some of the array's capability is simply not used. That makes it a good low cost choice for a small system and a poor one for a large array, which is where the two types stop being interchangeable.

How this matters when something is wrong

If you have upgraded your panels but kept a PWM controller, you may have a system that is working correctly and still underperforming, which looks like a fault. Equally, if you have added panels in series to raise voltage without checking the controller's maximum input, you can exceed its rating. Panel voltage too high appears in Victron's own list of reasons charging stops, so check the rating against what your array actually produces.

Two chemistry points that matter here

Victron states that sealed batteries and lithium batteries do not require equalization and should not undergo the process, and that temperature compensation applies to lead-acid, so it should be disabled for lithium. If those two are set wrongly on a lithium bank, you are running a charge profile the battery was never meant to see.

The charging stages, so you know what normal looks like

A lot of "solar has stopped working" is really a controller behaving correctly, so it helps to know the stages. A solar controller runs the same three stage pattern as a converter charger.

Bulk runs from empty up to 80 percent state of charge in Victron's own table, pushing maximum available current. Absorption covers the last 20 percent at a held voltage until the current tapers off, and this is the slow part. Float then holds a lower maintenance voltage at full charge.

The numbers differ by chemistry, which is why the setting matters. Go Power's GP-PWM-10 manual gives bulk and absorption at 14.4 volts and float at 13.7 for flooded and AGM batteries, and it has no lithium setting at all. That absence is the setting problem in one line: 14.4 volts is a sane absorption figure for a lithium battery, but a lead-acid profile also applies equalization and temperature compensation, and a lithium bank protects itself against both by refusing the charge. More panel capacity does not fix that.

So the test of "is my solar working" is not whether the controller is showing charge current at any given moment. It is whether the battery reaches full charge over a sunny day, and whether the controller goes through bulk, absorption and finally float. A system that sits in float all afternoon on a charged battery is working perfectly.

The settings that decide whether it charges

A controller ships with a lead-acid profile. Zamp's manual says its factory default is AGM, and an AGM profile applies equalization and temperature compensation that a lithium bank must not see. What follows is what the makers of lithium batteries actually ask a solar controller to be set to, and the one question readers ask most that has no setting behind it.

The voltages

The makers agree closely, and the small differences matter because a controller preset may not match your battery. Victron gives absorption at 14.2 volts for a 12.8 volt lithium battery and float at 13.5, with a permitted absorption range of 14.0 to 14.4. Battle Born gives absorption 14.2 to 14.6 and float 13.4 to 13.8. Epoch gives bulk and absorption 14.2 to 14.4 and states that float should be disabled, or set between 13.3 and 13.6. Renogy's Rover Li preset is a single boost figure of 14.4 volts with no float stage at all, which is the same idea: lithium does not need a maintenance voltage the way lead-acid does.

Absorption time is where the makers differ most. Victron's solar controller default is a fixed two hours, and it sets the tail current to zero amps so the full time is used for cell balancing. Battle Born asks for 30 minutes per 100 amp hours of bank. Epoch ends absorption on a current threshold instead of a clock: when the charge current falls to C/20, or after 15 minutes, whichever comes first.

What to switch off

Equalization. Victron's controller manual: do not equalise gel, AGM, VRLA or lithium batteries. Epoch calls equalization a lead-acid maintenance mode that must be disabled for LiFePO4. Renogy allows it only if the battery maker permits it, and Battle Born lists it as disabled.

Temperature compensation. Victron asks for it to be disabled or set to zero millivolts per degree on a lithium bank. Renogy goes further and says not to connect the temperature sensor at all to a LiFePO4 battery that has its own battery management system.

Temperature itself is a hard limit rather than a setting. Victron allows charging only between 5 and 50 degrees Celsius. Battle Born's management system will not accept charge below 32 degrees Fahrenheit, though it will still discharge down to minus 4. Epoch's battery cuts charging at zero Celsius and diverts the incoming current to internal heating pads, resuming at 5 degrees.

Tail current, charged voltage, and what they mean

These appear in controller and monitor settings and read like jargon. Victron defines the tail current as the low current threshold below which the charger ends absorption and switches to float, and its solar controllers default to 1 amp. On its battery monitor the same idea decides when the monitor resets itself to full: the voltage must exceed the charged voltage setting while the current stays below the tail current for the charged detection time, which defaults to three minutes, with the tail current expressed as a percentage of capacity and defaulting to 4 percent.

The 80 percent question

Readers ask how to set a controller to stop at 80 percent state of charge. No controller or battery maker in the sources behind this page publishes a setting that lands there. What they publish is voltage, time and current, and an 80 percent endpoint is not one of those. So the honest answer is that 80 percent is a practice rather than a setting.

Victron states that a lithium iron phosphate battery does not need to be fully charged, and calls that a major advantage over lead-acid. Battle Born says LiFePO4 batteries do not need to be fully charged every cycle and can operate safely without reaching 100 percent, while recommending a full absorption charge about once a week. Relion states that the chemistry is not damaged by being left partly charged, and that a single-stage profile will reach about 95 percent, which it calls sufficient. LiTime publishes a 20 to 80 rule, keeping state of charge between 20 and 80 percent, and says that holding a lithium battery at 100 percent accelerates wear.

One maker reads the same symptom the other way. Epoch states that stopping short of 100 percent usually means the charger's absorption voltage is set below what the battery needs, or that the reading is the app's estimate drifting from the true state of charge. So a bank that will not reach full is either being deliberately held short, or it is being undercharged, and the voltage setting is what tells the two apart.

What you can actually set is the absorption voltage, the absorption time or its ending current, and float. If you want to hold a bank below full, the lever is the absorption voltage and the time, not a state-of-charge number, and the battery's own management system is the other place a limit can live.

What a battery monitor adds, and where it goes

A voltmeter cannot tell you how full a battery is. Bogart Engineering states why: every chart relating voltage to state of charge uses the resting voltage, measured when the battery has not been charged or discharged for a few hours, and at a cell temperature of 25 degrees Celsius. Under load, it calls the voltage a very unreliable indicator of remaining capacity. Victron puts the same point shorter: how much energy is stored in the battery is something no one can tell by just looking at it.

What a shunt is, and where it goes

A shunt is a very low resistance resistor wired into the negative cable, and the monitor reads the small voltage drop across it. Bogart describes it as an accurate, very low resistance power resistor wired in series with the wire carrying the current to be measured, almost always between the negative battery terminal and all the loads and charging sources. Xantrex gives the arithmetic on its own part: a 500 amp, 50 millivolt shunt generates 50 millivolts at 500 amps across its small screw terminals, and that is what the monitor measures.

The installation rule is the one that decides whether the reading is worth anything. Victron's instruction is that there should be no other connections on the battery side of the shunt or on the negative battery terminal, because any load or charger connected there is excluded from the state of charge calculation. Renogy states the same for its own monitor and adds the wiring warning: the shunt goes in series on the negative circuit, not the positive one.

And the failure mode has a signature you can recognize. Victron's troubleshooting section says that when a load or charge source is connected to the battery side, its current never passes through the monitor, and the monitor will display a state of charge higher than the battery actually has. So a bank that reads fuller than it behaves is a wiring fault rather than a dying battery, and the thing to look for is a negative cable that reaches the battery post without going through the shunt.

What it needs in order to be right

A monitor does not measure state of charge, it calculates it, and the calculation needs numbers that are set rather than measured. Victron's monitor defaults to a 200 amp hour battery and asks for the real capacity, recommending the 20 hour rate for lead-acid. It needs a Peukert exponent, defaulting to 1.25 for lead-acid and 1.05 for lithium, and a charge efficiency factor, defaulting to 95 percent.

It also has to be told when the battery is full. Victron calls this synchronization: the monitor resets itself to 100 percent when the voltage is above the charged voltage setting while the current stays below the tail current for the charged detection time. Without that reset the reading drifts. Balmar states the problem bluntly for amp-hour counting generally: unless the monitor is regularly reset, by fully charging the battery or by the user, the error compounds, which it calls synchronization error. Maretron's guidance for its own monitor is to synchronize about once a month by fully charging.

Shunt against Hall effect

A Hall effect sensor measures the current in a cable without being wired into it. Maretron's sensor slips over the wire, which it describes as not having to break connections or install connectors the way an inline shunt requires. Its manual also gives the two things that arrangement needs: a calibration performed with no current flowing, and an awareness that the reading can drift slightly at zero current depending on temperature, which over a long period can make the monitor calculate that a battery is slowly discharging when it is not.

The makers do not agree on which is better, and both of the ones with a position are selling something. Bogart describes its shunt-based monitor as more accurate, stable and reliable than Hall effect based monitors. Balmar's no-shunt product argues the opposite way, that counting amp hours in and out is inherently inaccurate because state of charge is not necessarily linked to the energy a battery can deliver, and that the error compounds without a regular reset. What Balmar's shunt-based product says about its own method is the useful middle: it claims to be unaffected by the accuracy drift over multiple partial charge cycles that it says traditional shunt-based products suffer, because those need to reach 100 percent each cycle.

Published accuracy figures, with the conditions attached where the maker states them: Victron's monitor datasheet gives current to within 0.4 percent, voltage to within 0.3 percent and state of charge to within 0.1 percent, in a table headed as being with a 500 amp shunt. Bogart claims better than half a percent accuracy in amps. Renogy's monitor states 1.0 percent for voltage, current and capacity. Maretron states current to within 1 percent with its included sensor, and battery sense voltage to within 100 millivolts. Balmar's no-shunt monitor publishes three different figures in two documents, five percent in its manual, three percent after six months on its product page, and plus or minus ten percent charging and five percent discharging in that page's specification table. Where a maker publishes conflicting numbers, the honest thing is to say so rather than pick the flattering one.

Adding a panel, and mounting one

Matching a new panel to the ones you have

Renogy's installation manual is direct: it strongly recommends panels of the same specification and same batch, and states that mixing different brands, power ratings or ages will significantly reduce generation efficiency and increase safety risks. Victron's answer to the same question is shorter. Asked whether different panels can be connected to the same charge controller, its FAQ replies: no, under no circumstances.

Where a mismatch is unavoidable, the arrangement decides how much it costs you. Victron explains the arithmetic: placing different modules in series is possible as long as the open circuit voltage does not reach the controller's maximum at cold temperatures, and in series the voltages add while the lowest current of the two modules sets the maximum for the system. In parallel, the currents add and the lowest voltage sets the array voltage, which in its worked example produces less power. Its own advice is to keep the array to the same size and wattage.

Two limits decide the wiring before preference does. Renogy states that however the panels are connected, the result must not exceed the controller's maximum input power, input voltage range or maximum input current. Victron warns that open circuit voltage rises at temperatures below 25 degrees Celsius, so the cold-weather figure is the one to check against the controller's ceiling, and it states that panels with optimizers must not be used at all because in the worst case they cause irreparable damage to the charger. On cable, Renogy notes that the MC4 cables sold for this are typically 12 or 10 AWG with a 20 amp rating, which is the point at which a larger array needs a combiner rather than another Y branch.

A portable panel

A folding suitcase is the simplest addition because it arrives with its own controller. Renogy's manual shows the connection: the included cable ends in alligator clips, which attach to the battery terminals directly. Its expansion rules are worth reading before buying a second one, because a 100 watt suitcase can be paralleled with another of the same model on the controller it ships with, while a 200 watt unit cannot be paralleled directly at all without replacing that controller.

If your RV has a sidewall solar port, the question to answer first is whether there is already a controller behind it. A portable kit carrying its own controller and plugged into a port that also feeds a controller puts two regulators on one battery. The check is whether the port is a direct battery feed or the output of a controller, and if it is the latter, the kit connects at the battery instead.

Mounting a rigid panel on the roof

The roof is the part that needs the care, not the panel. Renogy's bracket manual has a section for RV roofs specifically, and it opens by saying that installation on RV roofs typically requires more specialized instruction because of how those roofs are built, and that the section uses a fastener type not included in the kit. That fastener is a well nut, which expands behind a thin panel rather than needing framing to bite into, and Renogy recommends a minimum roof thickness of three eighths of an inch for the method. Go Power's kit manual asks for a mounting surface at least half an inch thick and strong enough to carry the hardware, and specifies the screws it supplies.

Sealant is specified by every maker that publishes a mounting procedure, and the roof maker has its own rule about which one. Renogy asks for sealant around all edges of the bracket and the screws, and notes that with a well nut it is optional but adds assurance. Go Power asks for sealant under the bracket before the panel is set down, and then a generous amount on top and around each foot, with the feet and all screws completely covered. Dicor states that its lap sealant is the self-leveling caulk used to seal screw-type fasteners, and warns in capitals not to use silicone because it will not adhere to its TPO membrane. Alpha Systems, another membrane maker, goes further and states that butyl tape is required under every roof-top component screwed through the membrane, that the butyl tape is the primary seal and the lap sealant only the secondary one, and that only its own sealants may touch the membrane.

Leave a gap under the panel. Renogy asks for at least 10 centimeters, or 3.94 inches, of ventilation between the brackets and the roof to prevent heat buildup.

On a fiberglass roof specifically, no maker publishes a separate procedure. Renogy's RV section is written for RV roofs generally and treats the roof as a panel thin enough to need a well nut rather than a screw into framing. Go Power asks for a mounting surface at least half an inch thick without distinguishing what it is made of. What actually differs on a fiberglass roof is the two things those instructions already turn on: what the fastener has to bite into, which is why the well nut matters on a thin skin, and what the roof is covered with, which is what decides the sealant. So the fiberglass question resolves into a thickness question and a membrane question rather than a fiberglass rule.

And read the warranty before you drill. Renogy's manual states that installation must be performed by a licensed professional and that non-professional installation voids the warranty. Dicor's membrane warranty excludes alterations made on or through the completed roof, including fixtures and equipment attached to it, without the maker's written authorisation first. Alpha Systems states that its membrane warranty is void if any component in the membrane system is not an Alpha product. None of that stops an owner from mounting a panel, but it is the reason to check what your roof is made of and what its warranty says before the first hole.

What it costs

Parts and labor

A 100 watt panel kit with a 20 amp MPPT controller is a modest purchase, and a standalone MPPT controller such as a Victron 100/30 is cheaper than the kit it sits in. Individual panels cost less still. That matters because it means replacing a controller is often a better use of an afternoon than chasing a marginal fault in one.

Professional diagnosis is charged as its own fee, with labor by the hour. On a system whose main components cost about what a couple of hours of labor does, doing the three measurements yourself first is worth the afternoon.

Counterfeit controllers

Solar controllers are widely copied, so buy from a seller you can go back to rather than the cheapest listing.

Why has my RV solar stopped charging the battery?

Work the path in order rather than guessing. Check the panel's own output first, then whether the controller is receiving it, then whether the controller is passing it to the battery. Victron lists the documented causes, and one of them is simply that the battery is full, which is normal behavior rather than a fault. Shading is another, and even a small shadow on part of a panel can greatly reduce its output.

How do I know if my solar panel is faulty?

Measure the voltage at the controller's solar input terminals first, with everything connected, because that tells you whether the panel is delivering at all without taking anything apart. If it is missing, cover the panels with a blanket or cardboard, disconnect them, and measure open circuit voltage against the figures on the label. If your readings are much lower than the values on the label, the panel is the suspect rather than the wiring. Short circuit current is the other figure on the label and the one to be careful with, since most meters are fused at 10 amps and a larger array will exceed that; a clamp meter on a working circuit measures it without shorting anything. When you reconnect, the battery goes on first and the panel second.

What is the difference between a PWM and an MPPT charge controller?

An MPPT controller actively hunts for the maximum power point of the array, which means it can harvest meaningfully more power, particularly in cooler conditions or when the panel voltage is well above the battery voltage. A PWM controller is simpler and cheaper and works by effectively connecting the panel closer to the battery. That makes PWM a good low cost choice for a small system and a poor one for a large array.

Does adding solar mean I can remove my converter?

No. They are separate systems that happen to charge the same battery. The solar system runs from the panel through its own charge controller to the battery. The converter runs from shore power and does its own charging. They operate in parallel, and removing the converter would leave you with no charging at all whenever you were plugged in and the sun was down.

Why does my solar controller show charging in the morning but nothing by afternoon?

Almost certainly because the battery has reached full and the controller has correctly moved into float, reducing or stopping the charge current. Victron says this explicitly: once the battery is full, the controller will stop or significantly reduce its current, and that behavior is normal rather than a fault. Check the battery's actual state rather than the controller display.

Can the wrong battery setting on a solar controller damage my battery?

Yes. Zamp's manual warns that an incorrect battery type setting may damage your battery, and states that the factory default is an AGM profile. If you have lithium and the controller is still set for lead acid, it will apply equalization and temperature compensation that lithium must not see, and the battery will shut the charge down to protect itself. That is worth checking before you look for a hardware fault, because it is a setting rather than a failure.

Can I set my solar controller to stop charging at 80 percent?

Not as a state-of-charge number. No controller or battery maker in the sources behind this page publishes a setting that lands at 80 percent; what they publish is voltage, time and current. The practice is real: Victron states that a lithium iron phosphate battery does not need to be fully charged, Battle Born says the chemistry can operate safely without reaching 100 percent each cycle while recommending a full absorption charge about once a week, and LiTime publishes a 20 to 80 rule. If you want to hold a bank below full, the lever is the absorption voltage and how long it is held, not a percentage.

Do I need a shunt to know my battery's state of charge?

A voltmeter cannot do it on its own. Bogart Engineering states that every voltage-to-state-of-charge chart uses the resting voltage, measured hours after any charge or discharge, at a cell temperature of 25 degrees Celsius, which makes voltage under load a very unreliable indicator of remaining capacity. A shunt is a very low resistance resistor wired into the negative cable, and the monitor reads the small voltage drop across it. The installation rule that decides whether the reading is worth anything is that nothing may connect on the battery side of the shunt: Victron states that a load or charger connected there is excluded from the calculation, and that the monitor will then display a state of charge higher than the battery actually has.

Can I add a third panel that is a different size or brand from the ones I have?

Renogy strongly recommends panels of the same specification and same batch, and states that mixing different brands, power ratings or ages will significantly reduce generation efficiency and increase safety risks. Victron's answer to whether different panels can share one charge controller is no, under no circumstances. Where a mismatch is unavoidable, the arrangement decides the cost: in series the voltages add and the lowest current of the two panels sets the system maximum, while in parallel the currents add and the lowest voltage sets the array voltage. Either way the result must stay inside the controller's maximum input power, input voltage range and maximum input current, and the open circuit voltage figure to check is the cold-weather one, because it rises below 25 degrees Celsius.

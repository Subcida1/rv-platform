Sources

The ones that cost money

Never travel with the waste tanks full

Jayco's manual is blunt about it: "Never travel with full black or grey water holding tanks". A full tank is weight moving at the worst possible place in the coach, and the structure and the tank mounts were not built for the load shifting. It is also the reason the dump station is the last stop before the road rather than the first stop after it.

Close the dump valves when the tanks are empty, and never leave the black valve open

The dump procedure in the manual is a sequence, and the order matters: "Always drain the black water holding tank first so the grey tank wastewater can help rinse any solids or debris from the dump outlet and sewer hose." Open the black valve, close it when the tank is empty, then open the grey, then close that. Leaving the black valve open at a full-hookup site is the mistake: the liquid drains away and the solids stay behind, which is how a tank becomes a pile. And the tank wants water in it from the start, not just what the flushes add: the same manual says to "add enough water to prevent solid waste buildup", and gives the recipe as one to two quarts in the bowl, the chemical your toilet maker specifies, then a flush that puts at least two gallons into the tank.

Never plug into a pedestal you have not tested

This is the instruction that protects the most expensive thing in the coach, and the manual gives it in three parts. First: "Always test the external power source with a ground monitor before connecting your power cord to it. If the ground monitor indicates reverse polarity or an open ground, DO NOT connect the power cord." Then the specific cases: "DO NOT plug the shore power cord into a campsite receptacle that has reverse polarity, with non-functioning ground circuits, or that shows outward signs of heat damage." And the consequence, in the manual's own words: "Doing so may result in property damage or serious injury." A ground monitor, or a surge protector with one built in, is the cheapest thing you will ever buy for a coach, and it is the only thing standing between a bad pedestal and your converter. The outlets and GFCI guide covers what to do when the damage is already done.

Fill the water heater before you switch it on

Every year, somebody fires an empty water heater and buys a tank. Suburban states the rule as an imperative: "It is imperative that the water heater tank be filled with water before operating the water heater. Operation of the water heater without water in the tank may result in damage to the tank and/or controls. This type of damage is not covered by the limited warranty." The practical version: fill the system, open a hot tap until water runs steadily with no spitting, and only then switch the heater on. On a heater that has been drained for storage, that hot tap is the confirmation, and it costs a minute.

Never move the coach with the slide motors disconnected

This is the sentence a new owner has never read and the one that matters most when a slide will not retract and somebody has pushed the room in by hand. In capitals, in the manual: "DO NOT MOVE THE RV UNLESS THE MOTORS ARE PLUGGED IN TO THE CONTROLLER AND THERE IS BATTERY POWER TO THE RV. THIS SETS THE BRAKES ON THE SLIDEOUTS TO PREVENT THEM FROM MOVING DURING TRANSIT." A room pushed in with its motors out is held by nothing, and the slide-out guide has the overrides and the re-engagement step in full.

Never test for a propane leak with a flame

Also capitals, and worth reading twice: "Never use an open flame to test for a propane leak. Do not check for leaks using products that contain ammonia or chlorine; these products can cause cracks to form on the metal tubing and brass fittings." Soapy water is the test, and the ammonia and chlorine warning rules out a surprising number of household cleaners. The same section gives the operating habit worth copying: close every burner valve first, then "open the main valve in the propane tank slowly to avoid a rush of propane vapor".

Never fit a bigger fuse

"Never use a higher rated replacement fuse; doing so may cause a fire by overheating the RV wiring." The fuse is sized to protect the wire, not the gadget on the end of the circuit, so a bigger fuse moves the failure into the wall where nobody can see it. The fuse guide is about finding the fault instead of replacing the fuse.

Do not reverse the battery cables

"Do not reverse the positive and negative battery cables. Doing so will blow the reverse polarity fuses that protect the power converter." If the coach is dead on 12 volts after a battery change, those fuses are the first thing to check, and they are why the converter is often blamed for a mistake made at the terminals.

Never leave the coach while filling the fresh water tank

"Never leave the motor home unattended while filling the fresh water system", and do not overfill it: the manual warns that overfilling can pressurise the tank, cause leakage and water damage, and void the warranty. It also says not to cap, block or modify the tank's overflow tubes, because the pressure has to have somewhere to go.

Do not remove or plug the water heater's relief valve

Two instructions in one line, both absolute: "Do not place a valve between the pressure and temperature (P&T) valve and the tank. Do not remove or plug the relief valve under any circumstances." The valve is what opens if the tank reaches 210 degrees F or 150 pounds of pressure, and it is the reason a heater that is misbehaving vents water rather than becoming a projectile. Suburban publish both figures for their own valves, and 210 is the one that matters: 120 F is a thermostat setting on some models, not a relief temperature.

Never blow the water lines out with a valve closed

When the plumbing gets winterised with air rather than antifreeze: "Never apply air pressure to the water system with any valves in the closed position." Pressure against a closed faucet, valve or low-point drain damages the seals and produces leaks you will find in spring. Jayco's own manual caps its system at 30 PSI maximum, and warns that anything higher can rupture line couplings and void the warranty. Other makers publish a higher ceiling, so the figure is the plumbing's rather than one number for every RV. The winterising guide works that job in order.

Do not let the leveling system hold the coach while you work under it

A leveling system is a leveling system. Lippert's own manual prohibits using one to "provide service for any reason under the trailer such as changing tires", and states the consequence as "damage to the trailer and/or cause death or serious injury". A jack that holds a coach level is not a jack stand, and stabilisers are not jacks at all: they steady, they do not lift. The leveling guide has the overrides and the fluid checks.

Your first night plugged in

  1. Level and chock before anything else. The slides, the water system and an absorption fridge all want the coach level, and the fridge has a published limit: Norcold builds its absorption units to run within 3 degrees off level side to side and 6 front to back, and past that the cooling system can be damaged.
  2. Look at the pedestal, then test it. Breaker off, look for scorching or a loose fit, and use a ground monitor before the cord goes in.
  3. Connect, then switch the pedestal breaker on, so nothing is arcing while you hold the plug.
  4. Check the coach's own breaker panel for anything tripped, and switch the converter on if it has its own switch.
  5. Confirm the battery is charging by watching its voltage rise over a few minutes. The battery guide gives the numbers to expect.
  6. Water next, on city water with a pressure regulator, or off the tank and the pump. Open a tap and let it run, and do not walk away from a filling tank.
  7. Water heater last, and only once a hot tap runs without spitting.
  8. Propane after that, opening the main valve slowly, and if you smell it, shut it off and find out why before anything is lit.

The order to learn them, and why

The order below is not alphabetical and it is not the order a maker's manual lists them in. It runs from the system that can injure you, through the one that imitates all the others, to the ones you can safely leave until you have slept in the coach a few nights.

First: shore power, because it is the one that hurts

The 120-volt side is the only part of a coach that can kill you, and it is also the first thing you connect, so it gets learned first. What to know before your first hookup: the pedestal is somebody else's wiring, a breaker on the pedestal is what you switch rather than the plug, and a coach with a wiring fault can put voltage on its own skin. The outlets and GFCI guide covers the chain from the pedestal inward, including why a GFCI will not reset and what hot skin is.

Second: 12 volts, because it imitates everything else

Half the coach runs on 12 volts, and a battery that is low makes the pump weak, the furnace fan slow and the fridge's board unhappy, so a voltage problem gets misread as three different faults. Learning this system second means you stop chasing the wrong part before you start. The 12-volt guide has the layout and the voltage drop test, and the battery guide works out which of the four charging sources has failed when the battery will not hold charge.

Third: water in, then water out

Two systems that share a set of pipes and a set of habits. Water in is city water or the tank and its pump, and water out is two tanks and a valve. Learn them in that order because the fresh side is where you will first notice a leak, and because every fault on the waste side is a smell or a reading rather than a flood. The winterising guide doubles as the map of where the water actually sits, and the toilet guide covers the three faults that sound alike at the other end of it.

Fourth: propane, heat and the fridge

Propane is the one fuel you can learn by smell, and the furnace and the fridge are its two biggest consumers. Two things are worth knowing before the first cold night: how the furnace proves itself safe to light, and that an absorption fridge needs the coach close to level to work at all. The furnace guide carries the carbon monoxide hard stop, and the fridge guide explains why level matters.

Fifth: the extras, and what can wait

Solar, a generator and an inverter are all ways of making or moving power, and none of them is needed to spend a first weekend at a hookup. When one of them stops working, solar and the generator both have their own guide, and both begin by checking two things before anything is tested.

Where the two electrical halves meet

Two systems share one battery and one ground, and almost every confusing electrical symptom comes from that arrangement. 120 volts arrives at the pedestal and goes to the outlets, the air conditioner and any heating element. The converter turns some of that into 12 volts to charge the battery. Everything else runs off the battery at 12 volts.

Several components touch both halves, and that is where a fault crosses from one to the other: the converter, which turns 120 into 12; an inverter, if one is fitted, which turns 12 back into 120; and every appliance with a 120-volt element and a 12-volt board, which is the fridge in its electric mode, a dual-fuel water heater, and the air conditioner's controls.

The manual you got, and the manual you need

The manual in the drawer covers the coach. It rarely covers the appliances inside it well, because those are other makers' equipment, and it often covers several model years at once. Every appliance maker publishes its own document, and that is the one with the wiring diagram, the fault codes and the part numbers: find the label inside or behind each appliance for its model number, then open it in the manuals section, which links each document at the maker rather than rehosting it. If you do not know who made something, the brand list covers 44 coach makers and how far back each one's archive reaches.

What is safe to ignore for now

  • The inverter, if you mostly camp with hookups. It matters when you start running 120-volt equipment off the battery.
  • The solar controller's settings, until you have watched a full sunny day of charging and know what normal looks like.
  • Tank sensor calibration. The sensors read conductivity rather than depth, so a reading that disagrees with the tank is normal rather than urgent.
  • Seals, slide wipers and roof caulking. Servicing them is an annual job rather than a first-week one, and looking at them is not: a wiper seal that has folded inward on a slide room runs water into the room instead of off it, and it takes a glance while the room goes out.
  • The weight math, once your combination is matched. It comes back into play the day you change a truck, a trailer or how you load it.

Part 1: Your first days

The first night plugged in, the order to hook up water, power and sewer and to undo them, the walkaround before a trip, what to do on arrival, and the mistakes new owners make in the first week.

The first night, in order

The coach has to be level before the slide-outs move and before an absorption fridge runs, the water heater has to be full before it is switched on, and the electrical load should be off while the cord goes in. Work in this order and each step makes the next one safe.

  • Park, then level. Level side to side first, then front to back. RVtime states the order plainly: "Level side-to-side first, then front-to-back. Do it in that order and the job is quick and safe." The order exists because the two corrections are made with different tools: side to side is set with blocks or ramps under the wheels, and front to back is a small adjustment made last with the tongue jack or the landing gear.
  • Chock the wheels. Lippert answers the obvious question directly. Asked whether chocks are needed on level ground, its answer is "Yes, it's recommended to use wheel chocks regardless of the terrain." Chocks go in before the stabilisers, not after.
  • Look at the pedestal, then test it. Go RVing's instruction is to "Use a multimeter or surge protector with diagnostics to check the campground pedestal before plugging in." A pedestal that is scorched, cracked or loose is one to report rather than use.
  • Make the power connection with the pedestal breaker off. Go RVing's short version is "Turn off the power at the pedestal before connecting anything. Plug in your surge protector first." The full sequence, with the coach end of the cord first, is in the hookup section below.
  • Water next. Connect the drinking-water hose through a pressure regulator, then open a cold tap inside and let it run to push the air out of the lines. Do not walk away from a tank that is filling.
  • Fill the water heater before it is switched on. Mark Polk gives the check that confirms it: "open a hot water faucet at the sink and when you get a steady flow of water (no spitting or sputtering) the water heater tank is full." Only then does the electric element or the burner get switched on.
  • Propane last. Open the main valve slowly, and if you smell gas, stop and find out why before anything is lit.
  • Check the detectors. The propane and carbon-monoxide alarms run on the 12-volt system, so they should be working off the house battery from the moment you arrive, not only once the coach is plugged in.
On the order of power and water, nothing in the sources makes one strictly first. What they do agree on is the two rules that make the order safe: the pedestal breaker is off while the cord goes in, and the water heater stays off until a hot tap runs without sputtering.
Hookups: water, power, sewer, and the reverse

There are three hookups, and one habit covers the order of all of them: make and break each connection at the point that can arc or spill, and keep the power off while you do it. Water and power can be done in either order. The sewer is always last to connect, because it is the one connection you do not want to disturb once it is made.

Water. The order is spigot, regulator, hose, filter, coach. nuCamp's manual is written for its own trailers, but the logic is general: "Simply connect the regulator to the city water inlet on your RV, then hook up your hose to the regulator." A forum version puts the regulator ahead of the filter: "[U]se a pressure regulator between the hose bib at the site and your water filter (which connects to your hose, which connects to your trailer)". Either way the regulator sits between the park's supply and everything of yours, so it takes the pressure before the pressure reaches your plumbing.

  • Use a dedicated drinking-water hose. A garden hose is not made to carry water you drink.
  • Fit the pressure regulator at the spigot end.
  • Add an inline filter if the coach does not already have one.
  • Connect to the city-water inlet. The fresh-tank fill and the city-water inlet are often two separate fittings, and on some coaches a single fitting and a diverter valve do both jobs, so find out which arrangement yours has.
  • Turn the tap on, then open a cold tap inside and let it run to clear the air out of the lines.

Power. The two sources that publish a step order agree on it. Dicor's surge-protector manual says: "1. Turn off power to the RV before connecting or disconnecting power cable. 2. When connecting power, connect the power cable at the RV first before plugging into the AC power source. 3. When disconnecting power, disconnect power cable at the AC power source first" A fuller hookup sequence adds the pedestal breaker and the waiting step: "1. Turn off high-draw appliances and the RV's main AC breaker. 2. Make sure the campground pedestal breaker is OFF. 3. Inspect and test the pedestal. 4. If your cord is detachable, connect it to the RV first. 5. Connect the other end to the surge protector or power pedestal. 6. Turn the pedestal breaker ON. 7. Wait for your electrical management system to approve the power. 8. Turn on the RV's main breaker and appliances gradually."

  • Switch off the air conditioner, the water heater and other heavy loads, and switch off the coach's main breaker.
  • Turn the pedestal breaker off.
  • Inspect and test the pedestal.
  • If the shore cord is detachable, connect the coach end first.
  • Connect the pedestal end.
  • Turn the pedestal breaker on.
  • Let the surge protector or EMS run its check and approve the power.
  • Switch the coach's main breaker on, then bring appliances on one at a time.

Sewer. Connect the hose at both ends before you open anything, and keep the black valve closed. Go RVing states the rule as "Never open the black tank valve and leave it open throughout your stay. Always dump black tanks when they're at least 2/3 full, followed by the gray tank" The order when you do dump is black first, then gray, for the reason one source gives in a parenthesis: "Dump black tank first, followed by gray tank (gray water rinses the hose)."

  • Wear gloves. Fit a clear elbow at the outlet so you can see what is moving: "The clear sewer elbow is your only window into the black tank"
  • At a dump station with a smooth inlet, a rubber donut makes the hose fit snugly: "A rubber donut seal (sometimes called a sewer doughnut or hose adapter) creates a snug, secure fit between your hose and the station inlet. Some campgrounds require one."
  • Open the gray valve for a short burst and close it again, so that any leak shows up as gray water rather than black: "Carefully open the gray tank valve first for a short burst, then close it. This tests the connections for leaks. Better to find a leak with gray water than nasty black tank fluids!"
  • Weight the elbow with a rock or a brick so the hose cannot kick loose: "Find a rock or a brick and wedge it against the hose elbow to hold it in place. Most dump stations have a rock sitting nearby for exactly this purpose."
  • Dump black, then gray, then close both valves.
  • Precharge the empty tank so it does not start dry: "After dumping, always precharge your tank before using it again. Add your 5-10 gallons of water and treatment chemical immediately."

Disconnect, in reverse. The same source gives the order: "When disconnecting, reverse the order: switch off the loads, turn off the breakers, unplug from the pedestal, and then disconnect the RV end." In shorthand: "Power off first. RV end first. Pedestal end second. Breaker on last." A recognised electrical authority adds that this holds for every connector: "The same advice holds for ALL shore power connectors. Always turn off the pedestal circuit breaker(s) while plugging or unplugging any connectors from a pedestal."

The single most expensive habit in the first week is leaving the black tank valve open at a full-hookup site. The liquid runs away and the solids stay behind, and the tank slowly builds a pile that no amount of flushing removes. Keep the valve closed until the tank is at least two-thirds full.
The pre-trip walkaround

A walkaround is a fixed loop around the coach before every departure, done in the same direction each time so that nothing is skipped. The point is not a detailed inspection. It is to catch the handful of things that are dangerous or expensive when they are left loose or left behind.

  • Tires. Check pressure against the figure on the coach's placard, not the number on the sidewall, and look for cuts and bulges. Two age facts matter. The build date is the last four digits of the DOT code, where "the first 2 digits are the week (01-52) and the last 2 are the year." And a tire can be too old while still looking new. Goodyear's guidance, published through NHTSA, is that a tire in service for six years or more should be replaced even if it looks serviceable and has not reached the treadwear limit. Tread depth is checked with a coin: "a tire is unsafe and needs to be replaced when the minimum tread depth reaches 2/32-inch, which is the distance between the top of Lincoln's head and the edge on a penny"
  • Height. Know the tallest point of the coach before you drive anywhere, because an awning, an antenna and an air conditioner all add to it. "Measure from the ground all the way to the top of the highest object on your RV" and then "post this information on your dashboard, so you don't forget it."
  • The hitch or the coupling. For a trailer, the tongue weight wants to be roughly a tenth to a sixth of the loaded trailer weight: "For a conventional travel trailer, tongue weight should be 10 to 15 percent of the total loaded trailer weight." Load the heavy items low and forward, and keep them out of the back: "You should never load heavy items in the back of your trailer, or on the back bumper."
  • Everything that folds, slides or latches. Awning in, antenna down, steps up, slide-outs in, roof vents closed, compartment doors latched. Go RVing's reason for a written list is worth repeating: "Use a checklist every time you arrive at or leave a campsite. It's easy to forget simple things like unplugging power cords or lowering your TV antenna."
  • Propane while moving. One source states the driving habit plainly: "it is recommended that the propane supply be turned off at the tank while driving."
A checklist is not a sign of inexperience. The failures it prevents are the familiar ones: an awning left out, a television antenna left up, or a cord still plugged in as the coach pulls away.
Arriving at a campsite

Arrival is a sequence too, and the first step happens before you drive onto the site.

  • Check in, then look at the site on foot. Walk the pad before you commit the coach to it, and note which side the pedestal and the sewer connection are on, so you park with the connections within reach.
  • Do not cut across your neighbour. "Do not cut through another occupied site to reach the bathroom, the lake, or a trail."
  • Back or pull in with a spotter. Use someone outside who can see what you cannot, and agree on the hand signals before you start.
  • Level, then chock. Side to side first, then front to back, as set out above.
  • Hook up water, power and sewer in the order given earlier, keeping the black valve closed.
  • Keep the noise reasonable. "If you can hear it two sites away, it's too loud." On timing, "Even though 'official' quiet hours might end at 8 AM, generators and slide motors before 8 AM is rude even if technically permitted."
Some rules apply before you leave home. "Rules on how long an RV can be parked in a residential driveway vary by municipality and HOA, so it's worth a quick check of your local ordinance."
The mistakes people make in the first week

The first-week mistakes are remarkably consistent, and most of them are planning mistakes rather than operating mistakes. The machines can be learned in an afternoon. A rushed plan cannot be fixed at the campsite.

  • Going too far on the first day. "Most experienced RVers prefer traveling between 200 and 330 miles per day." A new owner who plans a long first day arrives tired, in the dark, at a site they have never seen.
  • Skipping the shakedown trip. "Take a short, close to home RV shakedown trip prior to venturing off on a longer RV trip." A first trip that is also the first use of everything is a first trip with no margin.
  • Making the first trip hard. The advice is to book somewhere forgiving, a full-hookup park with pull-through sites rather than a tight loop. One source frames the first trip as short by design: "Pick a destination within one to three hours from home. Stay two or three nights."
  • Not capturing the walkthrough. The dealer handover is the one time someone who knows the coach shows you how it works. "One of our very smart GoLifers recommends recording this orientation so that you can refer to it later."
  • Overpacking. "RV campers all have weight limits that are vital to ensuring a smooth trip."
  • Taking delivery with known faults. Before you sign, the problems belong to the dealer: "If your inspection finds significant issues, refuse to take delivery until they're resolved."
  • Missing the warranty window. "After 90 days, these items are no longer covered." The 90-day provision usually covers the small adjustment items a first season shakes loose, so the list wants to be written down as you find things, not remembered at the end.
  • Leaving the black valve open at a full-hookup site, which is covered above and belongs on this list anyway.

Part 2: The systems

How the 12-volt and 120-volt systems divide the work, how shore power is tested and sized, how water and propane behave, what each appliance needs, and what is settled and what is not on slide-outs, leveling and electronics.

The two electrical systems, and the rule for each

An RV carries two electrical systems at once, and almost every beginner confusion comes from that one fact. "Every RV has two separate electrical systems: a 12-volt DC system and a 120-volt AC system. The 12-volt system is powered by your RV's battery bank. It runs things like lights, water pumps, exhaust fans, and control boards for appliances. The 120-volt system powers heavier appliances and outlets, similar to your home. This is what runs air conditioners, microwaves, and standard plug-in devices."

SystemPowered byTypical loads
12 V DCThe house battery, kept charged by the converterLights, water pump, furnace blower and controls, vent fans, slide and jack motors, fridge control boards, propane and carbon-monoxide detectors, thermostats
120 V ACShore power, a generator, or an inverterAir conditioning, microwave, the electric water-heater element, household outlets, a household-style television

The distinction to hold onto is about when each one works. "The key difference: Your 12V system works all the time (as long as your batteries have charge). Your 120V system only works when you're plugged into shore power, running a generator, or using an inverter." A furnace is the clearest example of an appliance that uses both: the burner runs on propane, but the fan that moves the heat runs on 12 volts, so a furnace will not work off a flat battery even with a full propane tank.

Converter, inverter, battery. The four words that confuse new owners are easier once the direction of travel is clear. A converter goes from AC to DC; an inverter goes from DC to AC; an inverter/charger does both; the battery stores DC. "A converter serves several functions in an RV. It converts 120-volt AC to 12-volt DC to power low-voltage DC loads; provides battery charging; and distributes both 12-volt and 120-volt current to the various branch circuits in the RV." Not every coach has an inverter. "Almost every RV has a converter. Inverters are optional and are most often factory fitted on coaches with residential refrigerators or off-grid packages." A quick way to tell the two faults apart: "Flat batteries while plugged in is converter territory, dead outlets while boondocking is inverter territory." And an inverter only feeds the outlets wired to it: "Circuits that generally are not included in inverter loads are air conditioners, refrigerators, washer-dryers, and other high-current-draw loads."

The rule: if a load plugs into a wall outlet, or is a heavy appliance such as an air conditioner, a microwave or a water-heater element, it is 120 volts AC and works only with shore power, a generator or an inverter. If a load is a light, a pump, a fan or a control board, it is 12 volts DC and works whenever the house battery has charge.

When something stops working, the order to check it in is fixed: "1. Name the failed load. 2. Identify whether it needs AC, DC or both. 3. Confirm the source. 4. Check protection. 5. Inspect wiring and device last."

Battery sizing is the one electrical question with no published universal answer. The amp-hour capacity a coach needs depends on its loads and how it is used, and no manufacturer manual retrieved for this guide gives a formula. The sizing calculators that circulate are practitioner tools rather than specifications, so they can guide a choice but cannot settle one.

An RV is not wired like a house, and the difference is a safety rule rather than a curiosity. "RV wiring is different than house wiring. In an RV, neutral and ground conductors are isolated whereas in a house they are bonded at the service panel. Therefore; never connect neutral and ground as this can result in a ground fault condition, electric shock, and/or a fire hazard." More than one source can be live at once: "An RV may be energized by shore power, generator, inverter and batteries at the same time. Verify all sources before touching wiring." Before touching the 120-volt side at all: "NEVER attempt to open the 120-volt breaker box or access any of the 120-volt wiring before completely de-energizing all high-voltage sources, including disconnecting the shore cord and turning off the generator and inverter."
Shore power: the pedestal, 30 amps and 50 amps

The pedestal is the post at the campsite that carries the receptacles and, usually, the breakers. The electrical code treats an RV site as a small supply of its own: "Every recreational vehicle site with electrical supply shall be equipped with recreational vehicle site supply equipment with at least one 20-ampere, 125-volt weather-resistant receptacle." In practice a pedestal carries some combination of a household-style outlet, a 30-amp RV outlet and a 50-amp RV outlet, and which are present varies by park and by the age of the site.

Testing the pedestal. Before the cord goes in, the pedestal breaker is off and the outlet is tested. Go RVing's instruction is to "Use a multimeter or surge protector with diagnostics to check the campground pedestal before plugging in." Dicor's manual describes what a pass looks like: "Verify that all top three LED indicator lights are ON and Green." and what to do when it does not: "Consult a licensed electrician to correct the fault condition or move your RV and connect to a different pedestal."

  • Turn off high-draw appliances and the coach's main breaker.
  • Turn the pedestal breaker off.
  • Inspect and test the pedestal.
  • Connect the coach end of the cord first, then the pedestal end.
  • Turn the pedestal breaker on.
  • Let the EMS approve the power, then switch the coach's main breaker on and bring appliances on gradually.

Adapters: stepping down is normal, combining is not. A dogbone adapter changes the shape of a plug; it does not create power. Stepping a 30-amp coach onto a 50-amp pedestal, or a 50-amp coach onto a 30-amp pedestal, is routine. "You can use a 'dogbone' adapter to plug a 30-amp RV into a 50-amp pedestal safely (the RV will only draw what it needs). However, if you plug a 50-amp RV into a 30-amp pedestal, you are limited to 3,600 watts and will likely trip breakers if you try to run multiple heavy appliances." The 50-to-30 direction is common and normally fine, because the coach's own 30-amp breaker protects the cord, though it is not fully clean on the pedestal side: "[W]hile it is technically a code violation to use a 50/30-amp adapter without a built-in 30-amp breaker to protect the cord, there's a bunch of manufacturers who make and sell them to the public, and I've never heard of one melting down."

What is not safe is a cheater box or Y-adapter that combines two smaller outlets to feed a 50-amp inlet. Lippert is direct: "I do not recommend cheater boxes for a couple of reasons. First and foremost, they are not 'approved' for RV use." The 20-amp outlet on a pedestal is required to have GFCI protection, so "Cheater boxes will automatically trip that GFCI breaker and you'll only be provided power from the 30-amp side." And the failure mode is worse than a tripped breaker: "But, if one of the 30-amp receptacles is accidentally wired with Reverse Hot-Neutral Polarity, then plugging in a 30/30/50-Amp Y-Adapter will create a hot-to neutral short circuit. At the very least, you'll trip circuit breakers. But you might create an arc-flash which can melt the contacts and possibly blind you if you're not wearing eye protection." The same authority rules out the 30-plus-20 version: "These so-called 45-amp Y-adapters that are designed to plug into one 30-amp and one 20-amp pedestal outlet just won't work. That's because all 20-amp outlets on pedestals are required to have GFCI protection." The manufacturer's own bottom line: "The bottom line is that I consider it risky to use a cheater box. It's much better to connect a 50-amp coach to a 50-amp receptacle."

Chaining a step down and back up is its own hazard. Going from a 50-amp outlet down to a 30-amp extension cord and back to a 50-amp inlet removes the protection: "There's nothing to stop you from exceeding 30-amps of total current draw in your RV, and the pedestal will happily supply more than 30 amps without tripping the 50-amp circuit breaker." A 15-amp cord in that chain is worse still: "That's WAY more dangerous since a 15-amp extension cord with just 30-amps of current can easily reach the boiling point of water in 5 minutes." The practical rule: match the adapter to the mismatch in plug shape, and size the protection device to the coach's own shore cord.

Surge protector or EMS. These are not the same device. A basic surge protector does one thing: "Basic surge protector: Absorbs power spikes. That's it. Doesn't check for wiring faults, voltage problems, or ground issues." An EMS does more: "Electrical Management System (EMS): The full package. Monitors voltage continuously, protects against surges and spikes, and detects open ground, open neutral, and reverse polarity. If it finds a problem, it disconnects power to your RV automatically before damage occurs." The voltage thresholds are published: "Surge Guard provides protection to your RV by turning off power when the line voltages L1 or L2 fall below 102 Volts AC or go above 132 Volts AC." and the device restores power on its own: "Once voltage stabilizes and returns to normal, the circuit will restore power."
The fault most likely to cost money is not lightning. "The greater risk at a campground is not a surge but sustained low voltage, which makes motors draw more current and destroys air conditioner compressors, so an electrical management system that disconnects on low voltage is worth more than a plain surge protector." One device is enough, sized to the coach: "You only need a single surge protector that matches your RV shore power type. So if you have a 50-amp RV, get a 50-amp surge protector. If you have a 30-amp RV, get a 30-amp surge protector. Then add appropriate dogbone adapters to connect your RV to whatever pedestal outlet is available."

The hazards behind the device. On a 50-amp supply a broken neutral is the worst case, because the two 120-volt legs stop sharing the load evenly: "One leg may see 80V while the other sees 160V. Appliances connected to the high-voltage leg can be destroyed almost instantly. Open neutral is among the most dangerous conditions a 50-amp RV can encounter at a pedestal." Reverse polarity is more nuanced than it is often described. A recognised authority writes that "a reversed polarity outlet probably isn't immediately dangerous because modern appliances have isolation transformers and double insulation, plus all RVs should have their neutral and ground wires separated in their power panel." but still treats it as a warning sign: "a reversed polarity outlet in a pedestal is a sure sign that it was never tested after installation and wouldn't pass inspection, so who knows what else is wrong? And just two wrong things in an electrical circuit can create a deadly hot-skin condition." A hot skin is a shock from the coach's own metalwork, and it needs a broken ground path: "[I]f there's a broken EGC Bonding path, then any line-to-chassis current leakage will turn into a hot-skin up to 120 volts." If you ever feel a shock from the coach: "If you do feel a shock, unplug from shore power immediately until you can find the cause and repair it."

One hazard is not detectable by any consumer device. The rare miswiring called a reverse polarity bootleg ground is, per the same authority, "[A]n RPBG is not discoverable by any standard meter test, 3-light outlet tester, or even the most expensive EMS surge protectors on the market." That was written in 2023 and no later source re-tests it, so whether current protection units have closed the gap is not established here.
Where sources conflict: the 80 percent rule. Several practitioner sources say to keep a continuous draw under 80 percent of the breaker, for example "For long-term seasonal use, it is best practice not to exceed 80% of your breaker's capacity for extended periods. On a 30-amp circuit, try to keep your continuous draw under 24 amps." But no manufacturer or code document retrieved here applies that rule specifically to an RV pedestal, so treat the 24-amp figure as practitioner guidance rather than a published specification.
The water system

Water enters an RV in two different ways, and they do different jobs. "An RV freshwater connection is a way to add potable (safe to drink) water to fill the RV's freshwater tank. Most freshwater connections are 'gravity fill,' meaning there's an opening in the side of the RV for filling the tank with fresh water." The other way is the city-water connection, which pressurises the coach's plumbing directly from a tap. "In many (even most) RVs, the fresh water and city water connections are separate. So, there are two connections." Some coaches use a single inlet and a diverter valve, so a new owner has to find out which arrangement the coach has before the first hookup.

  • Tank and pump. Off the tank, a demand pump pressurises the system: "When you're not hooked up to city water, your 12-volt fresh-water pump is what pressurizes the system from your onboard tank." and "These pumps typically output 1 to 4 gallons per minute at 35 to 55 PSI"
  • City water. On a city-water connection the park's pressure does the work, and the pump should be off. nuCamp's manual describes the two modes of a combined system: "City Water Mode: Directly supplies water from a city water connection to your fixtures, bypassing the freshwater tank. No need to use the pump in this mode." and "Allows you to pump water from the freshwater tank to your fixtures."

The pressure regulator is not optional. "When connected to any pressurized water source, such as a city water connection at a campground, using a water pressure regulator is essential." "Many RV manufacturers recommend that the water pressure supplied to an RV not exceed 60 psi, with a comfortable recommended pressure of around 40-50 psi." The nuCamp manual states its own policy: "Every nuCamp RV is shipped with a pressure regulator designed to maintain water pressure between 40 and 50 psi." and "Always use the water pressure regulator when connecting to city water, especially when traveling or camping. It's essential for protecting your plumbing, as the pressure at some campgrounds or in some locations can be significantly higher than recommended." One practitioner source puts it bluntly: "Standard RV plumbing is designed to operate between 40 and 60 PSI. A pressure regulator is not optional equipment; it's an absolute must-carry."

  • Fit the regulator at the spigot.
  • Then the drinking-water hose.
  • Then the inline filter, if the coach does not already have one: "Many RVers (wisely) choose to add an inline water filter to this connection if their RV isn't already equipped with a built-in whole-house filter."
  • Then the city-water inlet on the coach.
Do not run the pump on city water. The two systems are meant to be used one at a time. A documented failure shows why: a coach hooked to city water with the pump accidentally left on can, if a check valve has failed and park pressure is low, push its entire fresh tank back into the park's supply. The pump's job and the city connection's job overlap, and running both is how a tank empties itself overnight. "Connect to city water and make sure your water pump is turned off." The corollary matters off-grid too: "You'll burn out your pump if you run it dry."
Propane and its safety drill

Propane runs the absorption fridge, the water heater, the furnace and the range. The system itself is simple: a tank, a regulator, a main valve, and appliances with their own burner valves. The rule that matters most is the one for a leak, and it is worth knowing before the first tank is opened.

  • Put out any flame, and do not operate a switch, a light, an appliance or a phone that could spark.
  • Get everyone out of the RV: "Get everybody out of the RV immediately."
  • Shut the gas off at the tank only if you can do it safely.
  • Leave the area and call for help.

The reason to smell low is a property of the gas. "Propane vapor is heavier than air, and it may collect initially at floor levels" so a check of the coach means checking the low spots first. When the supply goes back on, open the main valve slowly, which lets the tank's excess-flow device settle rather than trip. While the coach is moving, one source states the habit plainly: "it is recommended that the propane supply be turned off at the tank while driving."

A propane flame should burn blue. A yellow flame means the burner is not getting the air it needs, and that is a service item rather than something to live with.
The appliances: fridge, water heater, furnace, air conditioning

The absorption fridge, and why level matters. Most RV fridges of this kind cool by a gravity-fed process with no compressor, which is why the coach has to be level for one to work well. "Since the absorption system utilizes no mechanical pumps or compressors to circulate the refrigerant within the system, proper leveling is required to provide correct refrigerant flow in the gravity-feed system. Without proper leveling, refrigerant within the cooling coils will collect and stagnate at certain areas. When this happens the cooling process will stop." The manufacturer's own requirement is stated in plain terms: "To ensure proper leveling the vehicle needs to be leveled so it is comfortable to live in (no noticeable sloping of floor or walls). Any time the vehicle is parked for several hours with the refrigerator operating, the vehicle should be leveled to allow proper cooling." It can run on two energy sources: "The refrigerator is equipped with an automatic energy selector control system." choosing between 120 volts and propane, and at altitude it wants the electric source: "Always operate refrigerator on electric power at altitudes higher than 5500 feet." A compressor fridge is different: it does not care about level.

Where sources conflict: the fridge's tilt limit. The manufacturer manuals retrieved give no number, only 'no noticeable sloping of floor or walls.' The widely repeated figures of three degrees side to side and six front to back appear only in secondary sources and are attributed inconsistently, so they are not a manufacturer specification. Treat 'no noticeable slope' as the requirement.

The water heater: fill it before you switch it on. This is the most expensive mistake in the appliance section, because the electric element burns out in seconds when it is dry. "It is imperative that the water heater tank be filled with water before operating the water heater. Operation of the water heater without water in the tank may result in damage to the tank and/or controls. This type of damage is not covered by the limited warranty." The way to fill and confirm it: "Fill tank with water. Open both hot and cold water faucets to expel air from tank. When tank is filled and water flows from faucets, close both faucets and check all connections for leaks." The electric side is the fragile one: "Electric elements can burn out quickly, especially if they're switched on when the tank is empty." On gas there is no pilot light to light by hand: "This appliance does not have a pilot. It is equipped with an ignition device which automatically lights the burner. Do not try to light the burner by hand." and on a first start after the lines have been open, "The first start-up of the water heater may require several ignition cycles before all the air is purged from the gas lines."

Check the brand before you buy or fit an anode rod. Suburban steel tanks use one, and it wants replacing about yearly. Atwood and Dometic aluminium tanks do not use one at all, and fitting one can damage the tank. In Dometic's own words: "Dometic & Atwood Brand Water Heaters DO NOT use Anode Rods!" This brand split is the most useful single thing to know about RV water heaters, and most general guides do not mention it.

The furnace. A furnace is a good example of an appliance that uses two systems at once. "The heater runs on propane, but the fan that blows hot air through the RV runs on your coach batteries. So the furnace uses both the propane and battery systems to work." To start it, the manual's sequence begins: "1. Open the LP gas tank valve by turning fully counter-clockwise." then set the thermostat, and "Furnace fan should start to blow immediately after setting the thermostat." The burner lights about 30 seconds later. A furnace with a low battery will often not light at all, because the blower has to spin before the burner will.

Air conditioning. Air conditioning is a 120-volt load, so it needs shore power, a generator or a large inverter. Its one peculiarity is the restart delay. "Trying to restart the air conditioner compressor before pressure equalizes is known as 'short-cycling.'" and "Some, but not all, RV air conditioner thermostats have built-in time delays to prevent short-cycling." A generator has to be sized for the surge of a compressor starting, not just its running draw.

Slide-outs, and when to extend them

A slide-out runs on 12-volt motors, so it wants a healthy battery or another source of 12-volt power while it moves. On a motorhome that usually means the engine running: "The ignition key must be placed in the on or run position to operate the slideout room(s). The park brake must be applied for the room(s) to run." and "We recommend that you KEEP THE ENGINE RUNNING WHILE EXTENDING OR RETRACTING SLIDEOUT ROOMS so the engine alternator can provide maximum power for proper operation of the slideout mechanisms." A generic manual lists the acceptable sources: "To ensure ample voltage is being supplied to the slideout system motor, power should be supplied from one of the following sources: Attach the RV to shore power. Have the motorhome engine running. Turn on the generator." On a trailer there is no engine, so the slide runs off the house battery, or off the tow vehicle through the 7-way cord if the battery is low.

Extend and retract the room all the way, never part way: "NEVER extend or retract slide-outs partially. They must be fully open or closed to maintain their seal and keep the interior of your RV waterproof." Keep people clear while it moves: "Keep people and objects clear of the inside and outside of the slideout room when operating. Failure to comply could result in death or serious injury." And never drive with a room out: "CAUTION Never drive the vehicle with a slideout room extended!"

Where sources conflict: level first or slide first. Winnebago's manual for a motorhome says to level the coach and then extend the slide. Lippert's hydraulic fifth-wheel guidance says the unit must be leveled before the slideouts move: "Unit must be leveled prior to extending the slideouts to ensure coach will not move during operation of Slideout System and to provide the unit with a firm foundation." Other makers have reportedly reversed their own published order between model years. There is no universal rule, so the only defensible instruction is to follow the manual for your own make and model.

Two smaller habits are worth knowing. A Schwintek-style slide wants the switch held after the room stops: "Schwintek systems, hold the switch for 5 seconds once the room is fully opened (or closed) for the control board to complete its calculations. Failure to do so will cause the room to go out of synchronization over time." And the outside corner of a slide sits at head height on many coaches, which is why owners fit a slit foam noodle over it: "The primary reason RVers install pool noodles on their slideouts is to improve safety. The corners of a slideout can be sharp and pointy. Unfortunately, some slides sit right at head height. As a result, a lack of attention could result in someone running into the corner." The noodle is temporary: "Make sure you remove before you close the slides."

Leveling and stabilising are not the same job

Leveling corrects the slope the coach is parked on. Stabilising reduces the rocking that people and movement cause. The two are done by different hardware, and the most repeated warning in the manufacturer material is not to confuse them. "Confusion often arises from the interchangeable use of terms like 'leveling' and 'stabilization.' While levelers can technically stabilize, stabilizers cannot level." The distinction matters because "Using stabilizers for leveling can result in ineffective performance and potential damage to your RV."

The parts are described plainly by the maker: "Leveling jacks are specifically designed to support the weight of your RV and adjust its position as needed." and "Stabilizers, also known as stabilizer jacks, are distinct from leveling jacks in that they're not intended for leveling purposes." So the answer to the obvious question is no: "No, stabilizers are designed solely for enhancing stability within the RV and should not be used for leveling."

  • Position the trailer, then level side to side with blocks or ramps under the wheels.
  • Level front to back with the tongue jack or the landing gear: "Place trailer in designated position. Use leveling blocks, ramps, etc. to achieve the desired level before stabilizing. Deploy stabilizers or scissor jacks once RV is leveled."
  • Chock the wheels.
  • Lower the stabilisers last, to contact only: "If the RV begins lifting when the jack is applied, too much pressure is being used."

A stabiliser that lifts the coach is doing a job it was not made for. On a manual that says contact only: "If your stabilizer manual says 'contact only,' stop when the foot is planted and the slack is removed." Chocks are separate again and are used whatever the ground is like: "Install wheel chocks first, wheel stabilizers second." And a leveler is never a parking brake: "Never use the leveler as an emergency brake. They are not designed for any type of vehicle braking purpose."

The reasons to level: the absorption fridge is the well-supported one, and comfort is the other. The claims that a tilted coach drains its tanks badly or twists its frame are repeated by practitioner sources but were not found in a manufacturer manual, so they are asserted rather than established.
Entertainment and electronics: the reality check

The television is the easy part. Most RV televisions are a 120-volt load, so they work on shore power or a generator, and over-the-air reception comes from an amplified antenna on the roof. The harder part is connectivity, and this is the area with the most marketing and the least honesty, so the useful material is the material that argues against buying something.

A cellular booster does one thing: it amplifies a signal that already exists. It cannot create one. "Cellular boosters are often promoted online as being a game changer, that every RV and boat should absolutely have onboard." But "in many other situations cellular boosters might not make a difference at all in your internet performance" and can sometimes make data slower. The technical reason is that a booster uses a single exterior antenna: "Boosters, however, cannot take advantage of MIMO because they use just a single exterior antenna and can only work with a single signal at a time." "It's the equivalent of plugging one of your ears. You can still hear, but not as well." The same authority's own testing found "In our extensive testing over the years, MIMO antennas are the optimal signal enhancing choice for data more than 80-90% of the time."

  • A booster helps with distance to the tower, with obstacles, and with a metal-bodied coach. It does not help when there is no signal, when the tower is congested, or when you are being throttled.
  • It boosts cellular frequencies only: "Cellular boosters are designed to only work with cellular frequencies, and can not help with getting a better Wi-Fi signal."
  • The bands a legal consumer booster may use are limited: "The bands legally approved for mobile consumer boosters include Bands 2, 4, 5, 12, 13, and 25." but "But many cellular carriers use and depend on several cellular bands that aren't on this list." Some boosters sold online boost bands that are not permitted: "Using these boosters can result in a knock on your door by the FCC."
  • Placement does as much work as the device: "Placement matters: exterior pickup, interior router location, and cable runs can make or break real-world Wi-Fi performance."
The three tools people call a booster are not the same tool. "An RV WiFi booster is a broad, often imprecise term. Depending on the product, it may mean a cellular signal booster, an RV WiFi extender, or a router that creates WiFi inside your RV from a cellular connection. These are different tools, and choosing the wrong one will not solve the connectivity problem you actually have." Start by deciding whether the problem is weak cellular or weak campground Wi-Fi, because the two need different gear. And distrust the headline numbers: "Marketing materials for boosters often cite 'up to 50 dB gain' or 'covers 5,000 sq ft' or 'works with all carriers.' These are laboratory or ideal-case numbers." The band list and penalty figure behind these claims come from a consumer organisation rather than an FCC document retrieved here, so they are well sourced but not regulator-verified.

Water and waste

How the fresh, grey and black tanks work, how to read the panel, how to dump without clogging, where a sewer smell really comes from, and how to keep clean water and dry floors.

The three tanks and what each holds

Every RV has three tanks. One holds the clean water you use. Two hold the water you have used. The two waste tanks are kept separate on purpose, and confusing which is which causes most of the trouble that follows.

TankWhat it holdsWhat it is for

The split between the two waste systems is stated plainly by manufacturers. The Tiffin 2027 Allegro Bay owner manual says: "There are two, separate wastewater systems. The gray-water system is for wastewater from the sinks and shower. The black-water system is for sewage waste from the toilet. Each wastewater tank has its own control valve, and both drain through a common sewer-drain hose."

The fresh tank is separate from both. In Tiffin's wording it feeds "the freshwater system including the hot water heater, faucets, and toilet" when the coach runs on city water. The toilet is a fixture that uses fresh water. It is not an inlet to either waste tank; it drops its contents into the black tank.

The distinction matters for two reasons. First, only black waste carries the clogging, odour, and septic hazards, so only the black tank needs the careful habits in the sections below. Second, most parks let you leave the grey valve open while you are parked, but almost none let you leave the black valve open.

Leaving the grey valve open is permitted by some manufacturers and parks and forbidden by others. Tiffin says: "The gray tank can be kept open while hooked to a sewer connection." The University of Arizona Extension goes further and says: "Leave graywater valves open whenever you are connected to an RV park sewer service line." Some parks require both valves closed at all times. Check the rule where you are parked.
Reading the monitor panel, and why it lies

The wall panel is not a gauge. It is a set of lights that step up in thirds. Tiffin describes it this way: "The fresh, gray, and black-water vertical columns on the display screen are sequentially arranged to indicate fluid levels in approximately one-third increments." So the panel reads empty, one-third, two-thirds, or full. Treat it as a rough warning light, not a measurement.

Most panels use conductive probes set through the tank wall. A probe lights when liquid bridges the gap between that probe and a ground sensor lower down. The problem is that anything conductive coating the probe does the same job. Waste film, toilet paper, soap scum, and mineral scale all bridge the circuit, so a tank that is empty can read as full and a tank that is full can read as empty.

A holding-tank cleaning company, Kleen Tank, puts the cause this way: "Simply put, tank sensors mainly fail because of a buildup of waste inside an RV tank. Yes, failed electronics, broken or unattached connector wires, or failed reading boards can also cause misreads of tank capacities, but it's almost always because thick, sticky waste has built up in the tank." It adds: "In black tanks, this is, predictably, human waste and dissolved or broken down toilet paper. In gray tanks, it's molds and mildews that have grown in the hot, moist environment".

There is a simple physical check on a direct-drop toilet, which is one whose bowl empties straight into the black tank. The University of Arizona Extension fact sheet says: "Holding tank gauges/monitors are seldom accurate. Therefore, use a flashlight and look down from above the stool to judge the fullness of your tank."

That trick does not work on a macerator or vacuum toilet. Those designs do not open a straight path into the tank, so there is nothing to look at. On those, you rely on the panel and on your own sense of how long the tank lasts.

One explanation of sensor fouling is repeated by a vendor but was not opened and checked: the product maker Walex calls low water "the number one contributor to sensor fouling." Treat that as a claim, not a finding. The point the sources agree on is that water level and waste buildup drive the readings more than the electronics do.
The black tank: why water goes in, and how much

A black tank is meant to hold a mix of solids and liquid, not solids alone. Water is what lets the tank empty. When the tank is mostly liquid, the rush of draining water swirls the solids out through the valve and the hose. When the tank is dry, solids sit on the plastic, harden, and stay.

The rule that keeps water in the tank is to leave the black valve closed except when you are dumping. Tiffin states it: "While using the motorhome, it is important to keep the black-water holding tank gate valve closed at all times, except when dumping. This allows an ample amount of liquid to remain in the tank to provide a smooth flow through the gate and the drain valves when dumping. Sufficient liquid in the tank causes a swirling action that takes any accumulated solid wastes with it. Accumulation of solid wastes in the black-water tank can be avoided by keeping the gate valve closed when connected to the sewer connection."

You also start the tank wet, before first use. Tiffin's instruction is a depth, not a volume: "Continue pulling the toilet levers until a depth of at least one inch of solution is directly under the toilet." The solution is water mixed with an odour-controlling chemical.

You dump when the tank is well filled, so the volume of liquid does the flushing. Tiffin says: "The holding tanks must be drained only when they are at least ¾ full. Doing this will provide a sufficient volume of water to allow the complete flushing of waste materials in the drain lines and hose. If the tanks are not ¾ full, add enough water to allow for sufficient flushing."

The University of Arizona Extension sets the floor slightly lower: "Leave blackwater tank valves closed and dump when half or more full. NOTE: Dumping with less than a half tank will seldom be an adequate volume to properly 'flush' solids, and you could experience a persistent paper/fecal build-up in the tank."

So the practical window is somewhere between half and three-quarters full, plus a wet start. The exact number is a habit, not a specification.

No manufacturer publishes a gallon figure for the black-tank pre-charge. Every manufacturer figure found is a depth or a description. Tiffin says one inch of solution. Prime Time and Forest River say "It is very important that you keep enough water in the black water tank to cover the bottom to prevent hardening of any waste residue" and add "Never dump the black water tank until it is 3/4 full." The toilet maker Dometic tells owners to keep an inch of water in the bowl to stop tank odour entering the living space.

The widely repeated figure of five gallons does not come from a manufacturer. It comes from a Heartland owners guide, not Heartland RV, and that guide disclaims itself: "This document has been created by Heartland Owners independently of the Heartland RV Company" and "Because the authors are Heartland owners, not engineers or service technicians, it's possible that this document could contain errors or omissions." The same guide then says: "After running clear for a while, close the black tank dump valve. Now add at least 5 gallons of fresh water back into the black tank." A treatment maker markets three to five gallons, and owners on a Grand Design forum report anything from about five gallons to as high as ten. Any single number depends on the size of the tank.
The pyramid problem

The pyramid is a cone of solids and paper that builds up under the toilet drop and hardens. It forms because liquid leaves the tank while solids stay. Each flush adds a little more solid on top, and the pile grows.

The usual cause is leaving the black valve open at a full-hookup site. With the valve open, liquid drains away continuously. The solids have nothing to float in, so they settle and dry. Tiffin describes the mechanism without using the word pyramid, and the fix is the closed valve quoted above: keep the gate valve shut and the swirling liquid carries the solids out.

The University of Arizona Extension gives the same advice in its own words: keep the valve closed and dump when at least half full, because a low-volume dump will not flush solids.

A leaky gate valve causes the same result more slowly. The Heartland owners guide puts it: "The more serious problem caused by a leaky gate valve is that the waste water can leak out, leaving solids behind to build up and solidify on the floor of the tank. When this happens, the tank outlet can easily become blocked".

The single habit that prevents a pyramid is keeping the black valve shut. A tank that always holds liquid cannot stack solids, because waste that lands in water spreads out instead of stacking on dry plastic. That explanation comes from an owners' resource, not a manufacturer, but it matches the manufacturer mechanism above.
Chemicals, and what never goes in

A bottle sold for black tanks is usually one of three things: a deodoriser that masks or suppresses smell; an enzyme or bacterial treatment meant to break down waste and paper; or a formaldehyde-based steriliser, often called blue chemical, that kills the bacteria causing odour.

The best-documented harm is to the park's system, not your own tank. California's Department of Toxic Substances Control says: "you should avoid purchasing and using any chemical toilet product that lists formaldehyde as an ingredient in any concentration." It explains why: "Formaldehyde also kills bacteria necessary to breakdown the wastes to decompose in septic tanks. When your holding tank wastes are disposed into a 'dump station'" the chemical "may kill the bacteria in the septic tank which can eventually clog the system."

Indiana's state health department is firmer still. Its guidelines say: "Portable holding tanks or on board holding tanks containing formaldehyde or similar chemicals shall not be disposed of into an on-site sewage disposal system." In other words, formaldehyde waste can be refused at a park dump.

Bleach is the other common harm, and it damages your own equipment. The University of Arizona Extension says: "Never use bleach to treat or 'sweeten' a tank. Bleach can severely and quickly damage valves, seals, and gaskets." The same fact sheet advises "Consider using only enzyme-based or bacterial-based products." and warns that "the term biodegradable does not necessarily mean that the product is safe for humans or the environment." Tiffin's manual is blunt about the toilet itself: "Do not use chlorine or caustic chemicals, such as bleach or drain opening chemicals in your motorhome's toilet. This will damage the seals in the toilet and dump valves."

Against all of this, a treatment is optional. The practitioner guide Let's RV states "Water does the heavy lifting." and "Treatment is optional. A good product may help with odors and waste breakdown, but plenty of RVers manage without one." It adds: "Treat odors as a clue. A tank additive can't repair a dry toilet seal, blocked vent, leak, clog, or dirty tank."

The manufacturer rule on what goes in is narrow. A Coachman Pursuit owner manual states: "Never put anything in the holding tanks other than normal drain water, body waste, and biodegradable products. Paper wrappers, gum, cigarettes, etc., no matter how small, should NEVER be placed into either the gray or black tanks as doing so could cause damage to your plumbing system and/or tanks."

The item most often named as a clog cause is the disposable wipe sold as flushable. Practitioner and content sources call flushable wipes the most common cause of clogs, but no official source for that ranking was retrieved, so treat the ranking as unproven. The underlying rule, that paper which does not break down does not belong in the tank, is manufacturer-stated. Two more categories are excluded by the sources above: bleach, chlorine, and drain openers, which damage seals and dump valves, and formaldehyde additives, which public-health guidance says to avoid.

Sources conflict on whether enzyme and bacterial treatments help or hurt. The University of Arizona Extension recommends them. The cleaning company Kleen Tank argues the opposite, that digesters turn the tank into a septic tank and create the sludge that fouls sensors. Both are real positions from real sources, and the question is not settled. What is settled is that water, a closed valve, and correct dumping matter more than any bottle.

Not established: whether coffee grounds, cooking grease, or septic-treatment packets are formally banned by manufacturers. The retrieved manufacturer language is general, covering only normal drain water, body waste, and biodegradable products. No manufacturer rule naming those specific items was found.
Dumping in the right order

Dumping is a fixed sequence. Do it the same way every time and the hose stays clean and the valves keep working.

  • 1. Connect and lock the sewer hose at both ends before you touch any valve.
  • 2. Confirm both gate valves are closed. Crack the cap only when you are ready to attach the hose.
  • 3. Optionally run grey for a few seconds first, to leak-test the fittings. This step is practitioner convention, not a manufacturer instruction.
  • 4. Open the black valve fully and let it drain.
  • 5. If the coach has a black-tank flush, run it with the black valve open until the clear fitting runs clear.
  • 6. Close the black valve.
  • 7. Open the grey valve and let it run. This scours the hose.
  • 8. Close the grey valve, disconnect, cap, and rinse the hose.

The order is black first, then grey, and the reason is the hose. Tiffin states: "Drain the black-water tank first before draining the gray-water tank. This procedure permits the water from the gray tank to wash the black-water residue from the drain lines and hose" and again: "After you have drained the black-water tank, immediately drain the gray-water tank. This procedure helps to flush the black-water from the sewage hose." The last thing through the shared hose is the cleaner grey water.

Tiffin gives two further instructions. On rinsing: "When both the tanks are empty, flush them with a freshwater rinse before you close the valves." On the cap: "Do not pull the holding tank gate valve OPEN when the protective cap is installed on the pipe." If you open a valve with the cap still on, you dump the tank into the cap and onto yourself.

On the flush: Tiffin says "Be sure the gate valve is OPEN when flushing the tank." The flush water needs somewhere to go.

Why not leave the black valve open. With the valve open at a full-hookup site, liquid drains as it arrives and solids stay behind and dry, which is how a pyramid forms. The grey valve is a different case, and some manufacturers and parks allow it to stay open. Black stays closed.

Dump when the black tank is between half and three-quarters full. Tiffin adds one more habit: "Whenever possible, drain both the holding tanks before traveling."

The standard outlet is a three-inch bayonet. A clear elbow at the hose end lets you see when flow stops and when rinse water runs clear. That detail comes from practitioner sources; no manufacturer specification for hose diameter or a clear elbow was retrieved.

The grey tank, and why a sewer smell is usually not the tank

Grey water is not clean and not sterile. It carries soap, food, grease, and hair, and because it is rarely flushed, it can smell as bad as black water. The trade magazine RV Enthusiast notes that "gray water can stink worse than black water".

When a smell appears inside, look somewhere other than the tank first.

The first suspect is a dry P-trap. Each sink and the shower has a water trap that seals tank gas out of the living space. Tiffin explains: "Each of the sink drains and the shower drain has a water trap (P-trap) to prevent holding-tank odors from entering the vehicle. These traps must have water in them to trap odors. When the vehicle is in motion, the water might splash out of the sink and shower drains. When the vehicle is stored, the water might evaporate from these traps allowing odors to enter the vehicle. If this occurs, run water from the faucet into the drain, allowing water to fill the traps again."

The second suspect is the air admittance valve, a one-way vent on the drain that lets air in so the P-trap is not siphoned dry. RV Enthusiast says: "When the air becomes tainted with noxious odors from the gray-water holding tank(s), the culprit is usually a bad air admittance valve." It explains the part: "When water is flowing through the P-trap, the AAV opens to allow air to flow into the pipe and closes when the flow stops. If the valve does not close fully, then an obnoxious odor is allowed to permeate the area around the sink and makes its way throughout the interior." A failed valve is cheap and simple to replace.

  • Smell strongest inside a sink cabinet: suspect the air admittance valve or a loose fitting there.
  • Smell strongest at the drain: suspect a dry or siphoned P-trap. Run water down it.
  • Smell anywhere, and the toilet bowl will not hold water: suspect the toilet seal.
  • Smell with none of the above: suspect a blocked roof vent, which can push odour back through the plumbing.
  • Only after all of those check out: the tank itself is the likely source, and the fix is cleaning and correct dumping, not another bottle of deodoriser.
A smell is confusing because it travels. Gas entering at one fitting can be noticed somewhere else entirely. Work from the cheapest checks upward: refill the traps, then test or replace the admittance valve, then check the toilet seal and the roof vent.
Fresh water: sanitising, hoses and pressure

Filling the fresh tank has no automatic shut-off on the coach Tiffin describes. Tiffin warns: "Since there is no automatic shut-off when filling the freshwater tank, check the level from the monitor panel while filling the freshwater tank on the motorhome. The excess water will be vented from an overflow in the vent pipe onto the ground when the capacity of that tank has been reached. This pipe is installed in the freshwater tank to prevent possible tank rupture from inadvertent overfilling." An overfilled fresh tank runs onto the ground through a vent rather than bursting. It will not flood the inside on its own, but you still should not walk away from a fill. Whether every coach lacks an auto shut-off is not established; some high-end coaches have auto-fill, so check your own manual.

Sanitising means putting a measured dose of chlorine into the fresh system, letting it sit, then flushing. The target is 50 parts per million. Tiffin's recipe: "Prepare a chlorine solution using one gallon of water and one-half cup of chlorine bleach (5% sodium-hypochlorite solution). Prepare enough of the chlorine solution to administer one gallon of solution for every 15 gallons of tank capacity" and "This mixture puts a 50 ppm (parts per million) residual chlorine concentration in the water system" with the warning "Concentrations greater than 50 ppm might damage the water lines and/or the tank." The solution stands "for at least four hours." Then you drain and flush, and Tiffin offers a rinse for the taste: "prepare a solution of one quart of vinegar to five gallons of water."

A practitioner reference, RV Tech Library, gives the same recipe and the same limit: "a chlorine solution of one gallon of water and 1/2 cup of chlorine bleach (Clorox) (5% sodium-hypochlorite solution) be prepared for every 15 gallons of fresh water tank capacity." It repeats: "Concentrations greater than 50 PPM may damage the water lines and/or the tank."

Sources conflict on the bleach amount. Tiffin and RV Tech Library use half a cup of bleach per 15 gallons. Many consumer pages say a quarter cup per 15 gallons, which is half as much. The likely reason is that household bleach has become stronger over time, from about 5.25% to between 6% and 8.25% sodium hypochlorite, so the same volume now makes a stronger mix. The safe instruction is to compute to about 50 parts per million using the percentage printed on your jug, and not to exceed it. That explanation appears on a calculator page that was not verified against a manufacturer, so treat the history as likely rather than proven.

One more step from Tiffin: the coach water filter "must be removed before disinfecting the fresh-water system" because "the filter will remove chlorine".

Do not borrow the cistern recipe. The CDC gives a much stronger mix for emergency cistern disinfection, three cups of unscented bleach per 100 gallons. Per gallon that is roughly three times the RV dose. The RV limit of 50 parts per million exists to protect the lines and the tank. The cistern strength is not for a potable RV system.

A drinking-water hose is for drinking water only. Tiffin says to "use the water hose" that is "manufactured and labeled for potable water service". These are usually white. Do not use a garden hose, which is not made for drinking water and can leave a plastic or rubber taste.

Keep a second hose for the black-tank flush, and never swap them. Tiffin says: "Do not use the same hose that is used for filling the fresh water tank for the No Fuss Flush." The University of Arizona Extension makes the same point from the sanitation side: "NOTE: don't use potable drinking water hoses for such activities."

City water arrives at whatever pressure the park supplies, and that varies. Tiffin's published limit is 60 psi: "If city water pressure is more than 60 psi, Tiffin Motor Homes recommends to use a water pressure regulator to prevent damage to the motorhome." A pressure regulator screws onto the supply and holds the pressure down.

The RV Tech Library gives the wider picture: "the plumbing systems are usually tested to a pressure of about 100 to 125 pounds per square inch (psi), but to prevent warranty problems, some RV manufacturers may recommend only 40-50 psi." It advises "You should limit your pressure to 60-65 psi to be safe". So the range across sources runs from a recommended 40 to 50 psi up to a cap of 60 to 65, against a test pressure of 100 to 125. Follow your own manual's limit. Park pressure varies for real reasons: wells, booster pumps, elevation, and demand change through the day. Owners report anything from the mid-30s to 60 psi and higher. Those are anecdotes, not measurements, but they explain why a regulator is worth carrying.

Where to fit the regulator is convention, not specification. Practitioner sources advise fitting it at the spigot or pedestal end rather than at the coach inlet, so the hose is protected too. No manufacturer statement on placement was retrieved.

A whole-coach filter has limits. Tiffin says "As installed, the filter will remove chlorine, dirt, and other matter" but "The water filter is not guaranteed to remove the tastes and odors of iron and sulfur." Filters should be changed "every 6-12 months depending on the quality and quantity of water that is used". The RV Tech Library adds that filters cost flow: "A small, in-line filter restricts flow more than a 10-inch standard canister, and the standard canister is more restrictive than a jumbo canister." A filter plus a cheap regulator is a common cause of a weak shower.

The classic ways a new owner floods an RV

Almost every flooded RV traces back to one of five situations. Each has a mechanism and a habit that avoids it.

1. City water left on with a leaking fixture and nobody watching. This is the big one. On city water the system is under pressure continuously. Any leak, whether at a toilet valve, a supply fitting, or a cracked line, keeps running until someone closes the spigot. Owners on the iRV2 and Thor forums put the habit simply: "I always turn off city water when I am outside of the coach." One often-quoted line from a Thor forum thread reads: "50 gallons of water will do as much damage as 500 gallons." The habit: close the city-water spigot whenever you leave for any length of time. If you want pressure while away, use the onboard pump and tank instead.

2. A stuck toilet fill valve. The toilet is the highest-consequence leak, because an overflow lands in the living space. An iRV2 thread describes a Dometic solenoid valve "stuck in the open position allowing water to continue to flow into bowl." A Thor forum thread reports a bowl overflowing on city water; the diagnosis was "your toilet valve wasn't seated" and the suggested fix was to "install a quarter-turn shutoff valve at the toilet." The habit: after each flush, watch the bowl until the water stops. Keep a shutoff at the toilet if there is no factory one. Shut off city water when you leave.

3. Fresh-tank fill left unattended. Tiffin says there is no automatic shut-off when filling. The tank overflows out a vent rather than bursting, but an unattended fill still wastes water and can flood a wet bay. The habit: never walk away from a fill. If your coach has an automatic fill, use a flow meter.

4. Winter freeze damage that becomes a spring flood. A cracked fitting or cracked water-heater tank shows up as a hidden leak the first time you pressurise in spring. The habit: winterise fully, and inspect before you pressurise. Winterising is covered in the next part.

5. Backflow through the pump check valve. A stuck pump check valve can let city water backfill the fresh tank and overflow it. This is usually an overflow rather than an interior flood, but it is common. Owner reports describe the fix as cycling the pump with a faucet open, or fitting a second inline check valve. This is a forum-documented failure, not a manufacturer statement found in the sources.

One test catches a slow toilet leak: leave water in the bowl and check it an hour later. If the bowl has drained, the seal or the valve is the problem. That test comes from a content source, not a manufacturer, but it is harmless and useful.

Seasons

What cold breaks and in what order, how to winterise and where half-jobs fail, what four-season really means, and how to stop condensation and mould.

What freezing breaks, and in what order

Water expands as it freezes, and some parts of the plumbing cannot stretch. PEX tubing, the flexible plastic pipe used for most RV water lines, survives a modest amount of expansion. The fittings, valves, faucet housings, the pump, and the water heater cannot. Camping World puts it: "Most RV water lines use PEX tubing, which has some flexibility and can absorb a modest amount of expansion. But the fittings, valves, faucet housings, and water pump that connect those lines cannot. Neither can your water heater tank (or the heat exchanger lines in tankless designs)." It adds that a single freeze can do it, and that "the damage often isn't visible until you pressurize the system in the spring."

Parts exposed to outside air freeze before parts tucked inside the heated shell. Camping World lists them: "the low point drains, the city water and tank flush inlets, outside showers or kitchens, and the water heater are all fully exposed to that freezing air".

The order in which things freeze follows that logic: exposed first, protected last. One content-farm wiki gives an explicit ranking: "RV systems freeze in a predictable order: (1) exterior hookup hoses, (2) low-point drains, (3) belly-pan plumbing, (4) holding tanks, (5) interior plumbing if heat fails." That exact ranking was not found in any manufacturer, standards, or agency source, so treat the specific list as unproven. The underlying principle, that exposed parts go first, is sound.

The components named again and again as freeze points are PEX lines, plastic fittings and elbows, faucets and shower valves, toilet valves, outside shower assemblies, water pumps, water heaters, filter housings, low-point drains, the city-water check valve, washer and ice-maker lines, and the black-tank flush line.

The water heater is the most expensive failure. Camping World and the dealer source Daisy RV both say the water heater is the costliest appliance to lose this way, because residual water in the tank freezes, expands, and cracks it, and the whole tank then needs replacing.

Winterising two ways, and the half-jobs that still freeze

There are two methods. Winnebago states: "The two most common ways to winterize an RV is either by the wet method or the dry method."

MethodHow it worksWhere it fails
Wet (antifreeze)RV antifreeze is pumped through the lines until it replaces the waterLeaves nothing to freeze if done fully, including the traps and the water-heater bypass
Dry (blow-out)Compressed air pushes the water out of the linesCan leave water in toilet valves and traps; still needs antifreeze in the traps

The wet method pumps RV antifreeze through the lines until the pink liquid replaces the water. Winnebago: "For the wet method, you will use RV antifreeze" that is "safe to use in your RV plumbing, including your freshwater lines." The dry method blows compressed air through the lines to push the water out. Winnebago: "The dry method uses compressed air to dry out all of the lines in your RV" and "once all of your lines are thoroughly blown out, there is no water left in your systems to freeze up." Either method still needs antifreeze in the traps and the toilet: "both [methods]" still require "the RV antifreeze to go into your P-traps" plus washer units and toilet.

Blow-out leaves a risk that antifreeze does not. The dealer Greeneway RV warns: "The blow-out method may fail during freezing temperatures due to the inability to completely remove all water in sensitive parts such as today's modern toilet water valves." The same dealer rates the options as "Blow-out (risky), Anti-freeze (good), Combo Anti-freeze/Blow-out (best)."

If you blow out, keep the pressure low. Greeneway says "not to exceed 45 PSI". The practitioner guide DoItYourselfRV sets "30 PSI" and attributes it to makers: "Both Keystone RV and Winnebago recommend keeping compressed air at or below that level when clearing RV water lines." It adds: "Do not connect an unregulated compressor directly to the RV plumbing." Across sources the safe band is roughly 20 to 45 psi, never above about 50.

Blow out with a fixture open so the air has an escape path. Greeneway explains the mechanism for the water heater: "By leaving a faucet open on the hot water side, air will equalize pressure and easily drain the tank." DoItYourselfRV gives the procedure: "Open one fixture at a time until only air comes out. Clear hot and cold sides separately, then close that fixture before moving to the next one."

One step is genuinely contested: whether the low-point drains stay open or closed during a blow-out. DoItYourselfRV says close them first, "close the faucets and replace the low-point drain caps" before blowing through the fixtures. A practitioner PDF says the opposite, that leaving them open pushes more water out. No manufacturer ruling on the ordering was found. The rule that matters, keeping fixture valves open, is well supported.

For the wet method, Winnebago's guide calls for "two gallons of RV antifreeze (check your manual, some RVs may require a bit more)". You must bypass the water heater so the antifreeze does not fill a six to ten gallon tank. Greeneway gives the order: start "from the furthest and working to the closest point to the water pump", and "Open the HOT side of the faucet ONLY" then repeat on the cold side. Purge the city-water intake check valve too, by pressing it until pink antifreeze appears.

Half-jobs that still freeze:
  • Blow-out without antifreeze in the P-traps. Compressed air flows through pipes but does not lift standing water out of a trap. One practitioner source calls skipping this the most common cause of freeze damage in supposedly winterised RVs. Even blow-out advocates add antifreeze to the traps: "Blowing out the supply lines removes most of the water, but RV/marine plumbing antifreeze still protects the P-traps and toilet".
  • Bypassing the water heater without draining it. Bypass keeps antifreeze out, but water left inside still freezes and can crack the tank. One dealer guide lists this as a "Common mistake": bypassing the water heater without draining it first. Tiffin's manual states the rule plainly: "Draining the water heater during winterizing is a MUST."
  • Forgetting the exposed fixtures. The outside shower, outdoor kitchen, black-tank flush line, city-water check valve, and washer or ice-maker lines each hold water that can freeze.

Use only RV or marine potable-water antifreeze, usually pink, and never automotive coolant. The safe type is propylene glycol. The federal ATSDR says: "The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has classified propylene glycol as an additive that is 'generally recognized as safe' for use in food." Automotive antifreeze is a different chemical. The retailer etrailer notes: "automotive antifreeze contains the toxic chemical ethylene glycol, whereas RV antifreeze is always either ethanol or propylene-glycol based." Camping World warns that ethylene glycol "is extremely toxic if ingested and should never be used in potable water systems."

Read the label for the temperature rating. A rating such as minus 50 degrees Fahrenheit is a burst-protection figure, not a promise that the liquid stays liquid that low. RV Living makes that point: "a rating of -50°F means" burst protection.

What four-season actually means

Four-season is a marketing term. No standards body defines or tests it. The fire and life safety standard for RVs, NFPA 1192, does not define four-season or all-season. Its stated scope is "fire and life safety criteria for recreational vehicles" and its purpose is "to provide protection from loss of life from fire and explosion." Its chapters cover administration, referenced publications, definitions, general requirements, fuel systems, fire and life safety, plumbing, and vehicular requirements. There is no climate, insulation, or season chapter.

The industry association RVIA does not define it either. The only RVIA item that mentions four-season is a press release about a certification run by a parts maker, Truma, which itself confirms there is no RVIA definition: "While many companies claim four-season status, a gold level certification from Truma further increases credibility". That certification is Truma's own single test, using a cold chamber at minus 15 degrees Celsius and measuring how long the coach takes to warm to 20 degrees, and it is not a general industry standard. ANSI defines no four-season standard either; its RV-related standards cover park model RVs, low-voltage systems, and exterior ladders.

Trade and practitioner sources say the same thing. Family RVing states that "manufacturers rarely test RVs in freezing conditions." and that "A 'four season' sticker on an RV may not mean much". The guide Let's RV says: "The truth is, there is no RV industry standard that sets apart extreme weather RVs from competitors. How a manufacturer defines 'four season RV' is entirely up to the company." and separately "'four-season' is not a universal industry standard." The Boondocker's Bible says there is "no industry standard definition of a 'Four Season' RV."

Manufacturers use their own names for their own feature bundles. Camping World lists the terms you will see: "'four-season', 'Arctic Protection', 'Climate Shield', 'Polar Package'". It also notes "four-season capability isn't just about staying warm. These RVs should also handle the extremes of a mid-summer heatwave." and that "not many manufacturers will confidently stand behind a singular R-value for their entire RV." Because there is no test, a buyer has to read features, not labels. A useful tell is whether the maker itself claims four-season or something weaker; one manufacturer, Lance, describes its package as being for the "shoulder seasons", which is not a deep-winter claim.

There is no published pass or fail test a buyer can cite. If a seller says a coach is four-season, the question to ask is what the features are: an enclosed and heated underbelly, ducted furnace heat to the tanks, dual-pane windows, and insulation values for the roof, floor, and walls. A label is not a measurement.

An enclosed and heated underbelly is closed in and warmed, usually by furnace ducts or by 12-volt or electric heating elements. Camping World describes the goal as protecting "tanks, valves, and plumbing from freezing." Ducted heat routes warm air to the holding tanks and valves, sometimes with tank pads or heat tape added. Lance words its own version: "Ducted heat is routed into individually insulated holding tanks to keep them, the valves and PEX lines warm." Dual-pane windows are two layers of glass or acrylic with an air gap, and Camping World says they "improve R-value, reduce drafts, limit condensation, and cut outside noise."

Heating pads alone are the weaker substitute. An enclosed, ducted underbelly uses the furnace you are already running. Tank heating pads need shore power and do less in deep cold. An RV Trader blog makes the point: "There are a lot of campers marketed as four-season RVs that don't have enclosed underbellies."

Insulation has to be everywhere to work. One practitioner guide notes that a year-round coach "needs insulation in the roof, floor, walls, front cap, and slide areas, not just one or two highlighted zones." Dual-pane windows help but are not magic in an RV, because RV units are built differently from house units.

Even a well-built coach has limits. Camping World says: "RV walls, ceilings, and floors will only ever be so thick, making it nearly impossible to rival residential housing. Even the best summer or winter set up will fail if not used and maintained properly, and within the manufacturer's stated limits." A coach used in a North Dakota winter needs skirting, a heated water hose, and active heat management; the same coach on the Gulf Coast is barely stressed. Camping World puts the difference as: "the RV owner who decides to spend the winter in North Dakota must do more to protect their camper" than the owner who travels to warmer coastal air.

Condensation and why it forms

Condensation forms when warm, moist air inside meets a cold surface, such as window glass, a thin wall, or a metal frame. The moisture in the air turns to liquid on the cold surface. RVs are small and tightly sealed, so the air inside loads up with moisture quickly.

People are the main source. Keystone states: "With normal activities like breathing, cooking, and bathing, a family of four can release up to three gallons of water vapor into the air each day. Without proper ventilation or a dehumidifier, this moisture can condense on cooler surfaces within the RV, potentially damaging your belongings and the RV itself." The cold surfaces are worst in cold weather, because the temperature gap between the inside air and, for example, a single-pane window is largest then. Cooking, showering, and drying clothes indoors add moisture fast.

  • Ventilate while you cook and shower. Run the range hood and the bathroom fan, and open a window a crack when the outside air is colder and drier than the inside air.
  • Use a dehumidifier when the outside air is warm and damp, because then opening windows brings moisture in rather than pushing it out. Keystone says: "If condensation persists despite ventilation, a dehumidifier can help manage excess moisture effectively."
  • Wipe down wet windows and frames so the water does not run into the walls.
  • Leave cabinet and closet doors partly open so air can move. Keystone notes this keeps those spaces drier.
  • Use a desiccant only in a sealed, unoccupied space such as a closed cabinet, and refresh it as directed.
Sealing a coach completely does not stop condensation; it can make it worse, because trapped moisture has nowhere to go. The goal is to move moist air out or to dry it, not to lock it in.
Storage, short and long term, and mould

Mould needs three things: moisture, a food source, and a moderate temperature. Keystone states: "Mold can develop in any environment with moisture and temperatures between 40°F and 100°F." The food source is ordinary dirt and dust, which is why a clean, dry coach stores better than a dirty one.

The control lever is humidity. Sources converge on keeping indoor relative humidity below roughly 50 to 60%, and several cite an ideal of 30 to 50%. That figure is often attributed to the EPA, but no primary EPA page confirming it was retrieved in this research, so treat the exact citation as unverified even though the number is sensible.

Short-term storage, meaning weeks between trips, is mostly about drying out. Empty and dry the black and grey tanks, run water into every P-trap, and leave a vent or window cracked for airflow. If the coach sits in sun, expect interior heat to drive moisture out of soft furnishings by day and condense it at night, so ventilation matters more than in a house.

Long-term storage, over a winter, adds the freeze work from earlier in this part: full winterising, water heater drained, traps filled with antifreeze. On top of that:

  • Clean the coach before storage. Crumbs, grease, and dust are the food mould needs.
  • Dry it thoroughly. Wipe wet surfaces and let soft furnishings air out before closing up.
  • Leave cabinet and closet doors partly open for airflow.
  • Keep some ventilation. Do not seal the coach completely, because trapped moisture raises condensation. One storage guide notes that "Completely sealing an RV without ventilation can actually increase condensation inside."
  • Use a dehumidifier or desiccant as appropriate. A powered dehumidifier needs power; desiccant packs suit a sealed, unpowered space and should be refreshed every 30 to 60 days.
  • Fix leaks before storage. A small roof or window leak over a winter is a mould start.

When moisture is bad enough to grow visible mould over a large area, that is a remediation job rather than a wipe-down. One practitioner guide cites an EPA figure of about 10 square feet as the threshold between a do-it-yourself clean and professional remediation, but that figure was not verified against the EPA directly, so treat it as a guideline rather than a rule.

Maintenance

The maintenance schedule

A new RV needs a schedule. Most of the work is inspection and cleaning, and it runs on a calendar rather than on mileage. Your owner's manual is the authority: it lists what to check and when for your exact model, and it also lists what the warranty expects you to do.

Two manuals put the responsibility plainly. Winnebago writes that "it is still important to check your manual for the complete list and anything specific to your RV." Grand Design writes that "You are responsible for the regular care and maintenance of your RV in accordance with this manual and the component manufacturer's instructions." KZ-RV adds a warning that matters at claim time: "if proper maintenance is not performed the warranty coverage can be denied."

Some jobs are not owner jobs, whatever the schedule implies.

  • Mounting or demounting a tire, and any puncture repair.   • Wheel alignment, wheel bearings and brake service.
  • The propane pressure test.
  • Winterizing the water system if the RV will see freezing temperatures.
  • Any water leak that is hidden inside a wall or close to wiring.
The manual wins. Where a general guide and your owner's manual disagree, follow the manual. It is written for your model, and it is the document a warranty claim is measured against.
Tires: why they fail and how to read them

RV tires are asked to do more than car tires. Goodyear's service bulletin explains why: "Tires used on RVs are typically subjected to a greater variety of service conditions than automobiles, light truck and truck applications - often at or near maximum loads and during hot weather." Running near the maximum load means there is very little margin. A small drop in pressure, or a hot day, eats into that margin quickly.

A failure is expensive because the body above the tire is thin. Repair shops describe the same picture: "In seconds, a failing tire can damage underbelly materials, wiring, plumbing, propane lines, flooring, insulation, sidewalls, appliances, and even structural components." The tread separates and flails at speed, so the damage lands on the wheel well and everything routed through it. Published repair costs, such as a few hundred to several thousand dollars, come from repair shops and trade press, not from tire makers or the government. They are estimates of what a blowout can cost, not measured data.

Load range and load index are the two numbers that describe how much a tire can carry.

  • Load range is a letter, such as Load Range E or G. "The load range identifies the tire's load and inflation limits."
  • Load index is a number, such as 120. "the load index is a numerical code associated with the maximum load a tire can carry."
  • Some light truck tires carry two load indices. The first applies to a single tire, the second to a tire used in a dual pair. Toyo warns that "When LT tires are fitted in dual assemblies, the load capacity is reduced by 9% of the single load capacity to account for the affect of road crown."
  • A bigger number is not automatically a safe substitute. The industry guidance is that "The load index should not be used independently to determine replacement tire acceptability for load capacity."

The sidewall also prints the maximum load and the cold pressure that matches it, for example a Load Range G tire rated to carry 4,675 lb at 110 psi in a single position. That printed figure is a ceiling, not a target. The pressure you actually run is set lower and is described below.

The date code. Every tire carries the week and year it was made, in the DOT code on the sidewall. Goodyear gives the format: "For example, a DOT code ending with '0921' indicates a tire made in the 9th week of 2021." The full code is often printed on only one side of the tire, usually the inner wall, so you may have to look at the inside of each tire to read it.

Check all four, plus the spare. Because the DOT code can be on the inner sidewall, a quick look at the outer walls can miss the oldest tire. The spare ages too, even though it never touches the road.
The tire age question

Rubber ages whether or not the tread wears. That is why tire makers give an age, not just a tread depth, and why the tread can look new on a tire that should be replaced.

The sources do not agree on the number. Each maker publishes its own figure, and the range is wide.

  • Goodyear says 6 years. "Goodyear recommends that any tire in service (meaning inflated and mounted on a rim of your vehicle, including your spare tire regardless of whether that tire is in contact with the roadway) 6 years or more be replaced even if such tire appears serviceable and even if it has not reached the legal treadwear limit." Goodyear also says a tire's replacement period should not exceed 6 years.
  • Michelin says inspect from 5 years and replace at 10. "Tires that have been in use for 5 years or more should continue to be inspected by a specialist at least annually." and "any tires in service 10 years or more from the date of manufacture, including spare tires, be replaced with new tires as a simple precaution even if such tires appear serviceable and even if they have not reached the legal wear limit."
  • Hankook, in its trailer tire guide, gives 10 years. "It is recommended that tires in service 10 years or more from the date of manufacture must be replaced even if it was never used."
  • NHTSA reports the range, not one number. Winnebago repeats it: "Some vehicle and tire manufacturers recommend replacing tires that are six to ten years old, regardless of treadwear." The range is attributed to the manufacturers, not set as a federal rule.
  • The tire makers' trade body says no single service life exists. The USTMA states: "USTMA is not aware of scientific or technical data that establishes or identifies a specific minimum or maximum service life for passenger and light truck tires."

The popular 5-7-10 rule is not published by RVIA or NHTSA. A common rule of thumb on the internet says to inspect at 5 years, replace at 7 and never go past 10, and attributes it to a consensus of NHTSA, RVIA and the Tire Industry Association. No RVIA or NHTSA document that states this was found. RVIA publishes construction and inspection standards, not a consumer tire age rule, and NHTSA has never issued one. NHTSA's own research explains that it chose not to add an aging requirement to its tire standard: "At this time, the agency does not believe it is necessary for motor vehicle safety to add a tire aging requirement to its light vehicle tire standard…" Treat the 7 year figure in particular as unverified.

What to do with the disagreement. Read the DOT date code on each tire. Replace any tire that reaches your tire maker's own number. Have the tires inspected by a specialist every year from year 5. Do not use tread depth alone as the test, and do not leave the spare out of the count.

Age matters because of what happens inside the tire. Michelin explains the mechanism: "an underinflated or overloaded tire will build up more heat that could go beyond the endurance limits of the rubber and radial cords. This could cause sudden tire failure." The visible version, cracking in the sidewall from ozone and sunlight, is a separate age mechanism. Shallow cracking is normal. Cracking deeper than about 2/32 inch means the tire should be replaced. The dangerous part is that internal damage can be invisible: the cords and the rubber can degrade while the outside looks fine.

Tread still matters, and the legal minimum is low. Goodyear states both limits: "Tires should be removed from service if the tread wears down to 2/32nds of an inch. Also, federal regulations state that tires on the front axle of vehicles with a GVWR in excess of 10,000 pounds should be removed when worn to 4/32nds of an inch tread depth."

Used tires are not a shortcut. Goodyear's warning is that "consumers should never purchase or install used tires of any age on their RV as the service, maintenance and storage history of used tires is largely unknown."

Inflating to the load table

The pressure printed on the sidewall is a ceiling, not the pressure you run. RV tires are inflated to match the actual weight the tire carries, and that pressure is read from a load and inflation table. Goodyear puts the rule simply: "The proper air inflation for your tires depends on how much your fully loaded RV or trailer weighs."

The method is the same at every maker. Goodyear: "actual air pressure required should be determined based on the actual load on each individual tire. Inflation pressure should be adjusted to handle the tire carrying the heaviest load, and all tires on the axle should be adjusted to this pressure." Michelin gives the same instruction: "weigh each axle end separately and use the weight of the heaviest end to determine the pressure for all tires on that axle."

  • Find your exact tire size and its load and inflation table.
  • Weigh the RV fully loaded: fuel, water, propane and cargo, as it will travel.
  • Weigh each end of an axle separately. If you can, weigh each wheel position, the front left corner, front right corner and so on.
  • On each axle, take the heavier end and read across the table to the lowest cold pressure that supports that weight.
  • Set both tires on that axle to the same pressure.
  • Never go above the maximum pressure stamped on the wheel, not just the tire.
  • Check the pressures cold.

Weighing each corner, not just each axle, matters because the weight is rarely even side to side. Michelin warns that "when you weigh one axle at a time, it's possible for one wheel position to be overloaded even though the G.A.W.R. has not been exceeded (we've seen as much as a 1200 pound difference between left and right front tires)."

The pressure you choose must respect the wheel, not just the tire. Michelin is explicit that "the cold inflation pressure for the tire must never exceed the maximum inflation rating that is stamped on the wheel." For control, both tires on an axle should run at the same pressure.

Underinflation is the main failure route. A soft tire flexes more, which builds heat, and heat damages the rubber and the cords from the inside. The damage threshold the industry uses is low: a Rubber Manufacturers Association figure quoted by Michelin says that "any tire that has been run at less than 80% of recommended air pressure for the load it is carrying should be inspected for possible damage." Goodyear's consumer version is that "Tires that have lost more than 20% of their recommended inflation pressure should be considered flat."

  • "Tire pressure should be checked when your tires are cold and haven't been driven more than one mile."
  • A hot tire can read as much as 10 to 15 psi higher than the same tire cold.
  • "Do NOT bleed air from hot tires." The rise is normal and the tire is not overinflated.
  • A 10 F rise in outside temperature raises pressure by about 2%, and a 10 F fall lowers it by about the same.
  • "Tire inflation pressure increases approximately .48 psi for every 1,000 feet of altitude."
  • Use a good truck tire gauge with a dual angled head.
TPMS, valve stems and storage

TPMS does not replace the hand check. A tire pressure monitoring system shows the pressures while you drive, but it is not a substitute for measuring them. Goodyear states that "Tires should be inspected monthly for proper inflation pressure, tread wear and overall condition", and that the monthly check should happen whether or not the vehicle has a monitoring system.

Valve stems are a weak point with screw-on sensors. A sensor screws onto the end of the stem and adds weight. Tire engineer commentary explains the result: "TPMS external sensors screw on the end of the stems, and while they are not very heavy, that extra bit of weight does result in vibration, which can result in the fatigue failure of the rubber stem." Bolt-in metal stems are the fix, and flow-through sensors are designed for metal stems only. Valve caps should be plastic, not metal, because metal caps can corrode and fuse to the stem.

Other sensor details are worth knowing. Keystone notes that a common alert setting is a low warning at 10% below the recommended pressure and a high warning at 20% above it. Sensor batteries commonly last about five years. Whether a sensor is heavy enough to require re-balancing the wheel is disputed, and no manufacturer rule was found.

Balancing and the spare. Goodyear says to balance a tire when new tires are mounted, when a tire and wheel move to another position, after a flat repair, and any time a tire is dismounted. On the spare, Goodyear recommends that a matching full-size spare be rotated into service as part of the regular rotation, and repeats that the spare is subject to the same 6 year rule.

Storage. Tires age fastest when the RV sits. Michelin explains: "rubber tires age when not being used. So, if you must store your RV, a cool, dry, sealed garage is your best bet. Also, some storage surfaces can cause tires to age faster. That's why Michelin recommends placing a barrier (cardboard, plastic or plywood) between your tire and the storage surface."

  • Clean the tires with soap and water first, then cover them from sunlight and ultraviolet rays.
  • Store away from high ozone areas. Do not park next to electric generators, transformers or mercury vapor lamps.
  • Put a barrier between the tire and the ground.
  • Goodyear says to inflate to operating pressure plus 25%, but not above the wheel rating, and to move the RV at least every three months to prevent flat spotting. Michelin instead says to inflate to the pressure shown on the sidewall. These two instructions differ.
  • Do not use tire dressings that contain petroleum products, alcohol or silicones. Michelin states they will "cause deterioration or cracking and accelerate the aging process."
Store on a level surface. Michelin warns that if a tire is not evenly supported, "the sidewall cables can become fatigued and damaged, resulting in a sidewall rupture and a complete, sudden loss of air pressure."
The factory-fresh plumbing problem

A new RV is not finished plumbing. A pattern reported widely by owners, dealers and repair shops is that factory water connections can be loose or wrong and start to leak after some miles of towing, once vibration has worked on them. The caveat is that no manufacturer or government document was found that states this generally. What does exist is specific manufacturer paperwork proving individual factory defects, plus a large body of owner and shop reports.

The mechanism is vibration. One repair source writes: "When you tow your RV, you're subjecting it to an earthquake-like experience. Even on a smooth highway, water lines will vibrate and shake, which can cause the fittings that connect the various water lines to come loose." The same source describes the origin of many of these joints: "Sometimes workers hurry when putting together an RV's plumbing system and do poorly connecting the water lines. In an ideal world, the manufacturer or the RV dealer would catch these during pre-delivery inspections, but that's not always the case."

A specific defect recurs: flexible braided or vinyl hose clamped onto a fitting meant for rigid PEX pipe. One shop describes it as "Poor factory connections: Some newer RVs were built with flexible vinyl tubing on fittings made for rigid PEX, creating slow leaks at junctions." The clamp cannot seal the softer, slightly larger hose as well as it seals pipe, so the joint weeps.

Manufacturer paperwork shows that factory water defects are real. In a customer satisfaction campaign, Brinkley said the "water supply line to the ice maker may pull loose (or out), creating a water leak." Forest River issued a bulletin for a water heater that was not winterized correctly and could leak from cracked internal parts. Dometic, the toilet maker, notes a factory test that can disturb a seal: "After installation at the RV or other vehicle factory, the entire toilet and waste tank system is flood-tested for leaks by filling the system with water to the rim…" Holding the flush ball open for that test can distort the seal, so a slow drip into the tank can appear later.

  • Pressure hold test. One expert method: "Pressurise the system, switch the pump off at the panel, and see whether pressure holds for 15 minutes. If it holds, the pump's check valve is the suspect rather than the plumbing." A typical 12 volt pump cuts off between 45 and 55 psi.
  • Paper towel tracing. Wrap a single layer of paper towel around the supply and drain lines under the sinks, around the toilet and by the water heater. The wet one points to the leak.
  • Watch shore water pressure. Keep the pressure at the hydrant below about 65 psi, and use a real pressure regulator rather than a cheap restrictor.
  • Check the known spots. Vinyl to PEX junctions in the wet bay, faucet supply connections, water heater lines, the pump and its check valve, and the low point drains.
  • Fix clamps correctly. A standard screw hose clamp does not compress a PEX joint evenly. Use the right clamp for the fitting, such as a stainless ear clamp or a copper crimp ring, and use a braided flex hose between the pump and the pipe to absorb vibration.
Warranty language often puts the fitting check on the owner. One owner's manual lists "including water leaks, related to misuse, mishandling, neglect or abuse" among the things not covered, and names tightening fittings as routine maintenance. A slow drip found early is a small job. One found after it has travelled through a floor is not.
Seals, roof and delamination

Sealant keeps water out of an RV, and water is the enemy. There are two kinds of lap sealant and they are not interchangeable. Dicor, which makes both, warns on the self leveling version: "DO NOT USE on vertical surfaces. DO NOT USE as splicing cement." The other version is labeled "Non-Sag (for vertical surfaces)". Self leveling sealant flows out flat and is for horizontal surfaces such as the roof. Non-sag sealant stays put and is for anything sloped or vertical, such as sidewalls and termination rails.

Sealant choice also depends on the roof material. On a rubber or TPO roof, do not use silicone. Alpha Systems, which makes roof membrane, states plainly: "Do not use silicone! Silicone will not adhere to the Superflex roof membrane." There is also a two layer idea behind factory roofs: "Butyl tape provides the 'Primary Seal' against water infiltration or penetration in these membrane roof assemblies. The lap sealant is a secondary seal." The tape under the molding is the real seal, and the lap sealant covers and protects it.

How often to inspect is a genuine disagreement, and no RVIA document fixes one.

  • Every 90 days: "Most manufacturers recommend inspecting your RV's exterior seams and seals every 90 days."
  • Three to four times a year: a roof maker advises that "During routine cleaning (3-4 times a year), all caulk and sealants should be inspected for voids or cracking."
  • Every 6 months, reseal annually: a practitioner source says to "inspect your RV roof seals carefully every six months (twice a year) and reseal it annually."
  • OEM: Winnebago's chart lists sealants to be inspected every 6 months and replaced annually.

The reason to inspect on a calendar rather than by mileage is that the sealant ages even when the RV does not move. As one repair source puts it, "UV dose, heat cycles and ozone break down sealant and rubber whether the unit rolls or sits". Look for "Deep cracks or voids in the lap sealant; Loose or missing transition molding; Cracked, damaged, or worn areas around roof vents, A/C units, solar panels, and other roof-mounted accessories."

Delamination is what happens to a laminated sidewall when water gets in. The definition: "Most modern RV sidewalls are laminated: a fiberglass or aluminum outer skin glued to foam or a wooden lattice, with a thin inner panel. Delamination is the outer skin separating from what it was glued to, and it shows as bubbles, waves or ripples in the wall, soft spots and sometimes a hollow sound when tapped." It is a failure of the glue, not of the fiberglass: "Sidewall delamination is an adhesive failure, not a fiberglass failure." And the cause is usually water: "Water is the usual cause: it gets in through a failed seam, vent or window seal, softens the glue and the wood, and spreads."

The warning signs are easy to check once you know what to look for.

  • Bubbles, waves, ripples or creases along a sidewall.
  • Sight down the wall in low, raking light. One shop's test: "Stand at one end of the trailer and look down the wall until it turns into a mirror."
  • Tap or press the wall. "A bonded panel rings. An unbonded panel thuds." A skin that flexes and springs back means the glue has failed. A skin that sinks and stays sunk means the wood behind it has rotted.
  • Use a moisture meter if you have one. "Sound sidewall reads in the 8 to 12 percent range. Anything over 18 percent is actively wet."
  • Check the usual entry points: roof to sidewall seams, window corners, awning rails, marker lights, storage door frames, slide-out openings and the front and rear caps.

Not every RV can delaminate. Units built with corrugated aluminum siding over a framed wall, sometimes called hung wall construction, "don't use laminated fiberglass panels." If your RV has that kind of siding, this particular failure mode does not apply.

Cleaning and care

Exterior cleaning is mostly about protecting the surface. Wash with a pH neutral or RV and marine grade soap and a soft brush or microfiber mitt. Wash the roof first so the runoff does not streak down the clean sidewalls. A common recommendation is to wash the full exterior about once a month, and the roof every three to four months.

  • "Avoid solvents at all costs."
  • "No pressure washing, period."
  • "Avoid dish soaps and other household detergents that can dry out and strip wax."
  • "Avoid using strong household solvents, undiluted bleach, or heavy rubbing compounds not labeled for gelcoat or fiberglass."
  • Do not use silicone products on a rubber or TPO roof.

Pressure washing is called out because of where the water goes. As one maker explains, "Excess pressure can force water behind seals and decals." Water forced behind a seal is how a seal starts to fail, which leads to the delamination problem described above.

Wax helps, but it does not last on fiberglass gel coat. One detailer notes that "wax may last only one to three months." Decals need their own care: use a UV protectant rather than an abrasive or solvent wax, because solvent and abrasive waxes wear the graphics. A ceramic coating is the longer lasting option.

  • Clean the roof and check the lap sealant for cracks and voids at each cleaning.
  • Do not use petroleum based or silicone products on a rubber or TPO roof.
  • Do not pressure wash an awning: "Do not use a pressure washer, which can delaminate vinyl fabric and damage seams." Let it dry fully in the extended position before rolling it up.
  • On acrylic awning fabric: "Avoid bleach, which breaks down acrylic fibers over time, and avoid petroleum-based solvents."

Black streaks are the dark lines that run down from the roof. A black streak cleaner will work, but a maker cautions that "these cleaners WILL strip wax from the surface. So, it is important to re-wax the area where you have used the cleaner." The technique matters too: "The best way to use a black streak cleaner is to spray the product onto a towel and attack the streak directly." If the streaks return after cleaning, the surface is oxidized and needs treatment rather than another wash.

Interior care is covered in a dedicated chapter of your owner's manual, which lists soft goods, cabinetry, vinyl wall paneling, flooring, bathroom and windows. Follow that chapter. No general product list for interior surfaces was found in the sources behind this guide, so the manual is the place to look.

Driving and towing

Driving a motorhome, towing a travel trailer, fifth wheel specifics, backing up and pop-up campers.

Driving a motorhome

A motorhome is longer than a car and it steers from the front, so the back end does not follow the front wheels exactly. The two ideas to learn are off-tracking and rear overhang. Off-tracking means "When the rear wheels follow a different path than the front wheels. The longer the wheelbase and the sharper the wheel cut, the greater the off-tracking." Rear overhang means "The part of the RV that sticks out behind the drive axle."

Off-tracking is what makes a turn feel tighter than it looks. On a long coach the rear axle can cut several feet inside the path of the front wheels on a sharp campsite or driveway turn.

The practical turn advice is to plan ahead: "Set up further away from the obstruction and start to turn earlier". Use the convex mirrors on the side you are turning toward, with the rule "Turn right, look right. Turn left, look left."

Tail swing is the other half of the same geometry. The overhang behind the rear axle swings the opposite way at the start of a turn, so the rear corner can clip something beside you. Newmar's rule of thumb is that "For every three feet behind your rear axle, you have the potential for one foot of tail swing heading in the opposite direction." On some coaches it can be as much as 30 inches. You can measure yours by parking parallel to a straight line, making a full lock turn away from the line, and seeing how far the tail crosses it. A shortcut is to divide the overhang length by three.

Braking. More weight takes longer to stop, and a motorhome carries a lot of it. One thing that surprises new owners: the coach's service brakes are sized for the coach, not for whatever it is towing. One expert explains that "the brakes on their coach are rated for operation at the gross vehicle weight rating (GVWR)", not for the combined weight of coach and towed vehicle, and that "It is NOT the amount of weight that can be stopped by the coach's brakes alone." That is why a towed vehicle needs its own supplemental braking system, which is required above about 3,000 lb in most states.

Drive within a margin. Stopping distance grows with load, and a coach that is at its legal limit on a long downgrade has little reserve. Use the transmission and the exhaust brake or engine braking on long grades rather than riding the service brakes.

  • Start turns later and wider than you would in a car.
  • Watch the side you are turning toward, and remember the tail swings the other way.
  • Give a motorhome braking room, and expect to need more when descending.
  • Use a spotter and mirrors when parking (see the backing section).
  • Do not force a turn when the geometry is tight. Reposition and try again.
Towing a trailer

Hitching, in order. The sequence below is assembled from manufacturer manuals. The one rule that never changes is that the wheel chocks stay in place until the whole procedure is finished, because the trailer can roll the moment the coupler is not seated on the ball.

  • Chock the trailer wheels, and leave the chocks in until the end.
  • Open the coupler latch before backing up, not after.
  • Raise the tongue with the tongue jack so the coupler clears the ball, higher than you think you need.
  • Back the tow vehicle until the ball is under the coupler.
  • Lower the coupler onto the ball until it is fully seated, then close the coupler latch and fit the safety pin.
  • Install the weight distribution bars and sway control as the hitch maker directs, then lower the jack.
  • Retract the tongue jack fully, and be sure the stabilizer jacks are retracted.
  • Connect the breakaway cable to the tow vehicle.
  • Cross the safety chains under the coupler and attach them to the tow vehicle.
  • Plug in the trailer connector and test the lights, turn signals, brakes and running lights.
  • Remove the chocks, then do the tug test: with everything connected, pull forward gently against the chocked wheels.

The manuals give the reason for each step. Lance requires that the trailer not move until "The coupler is secured and locked", the chains are secured and the jacks are retracted. Winnebago states that "Leave your wheel chocks in place until you have completed the entire hitching procedure."

Safety chains cross under the tongue. Crossing them makes a cradle that catches the tongue if the coupler separates, instead of letting it drop to the pavement. Lance specifies that the chains "Fasten to frame of tow vehicle, not to hitch or ball." Winnebago adds that "The chains should be crossed under the hitch in an 'X' pattern to keep the trailer from hitting the ground in case of a disconnection." A retailer prints the rule in capitals: "NEVER ATTACH SAFETY CHAINS TO THE HITCH BALL OR ANY REMOVABLE PART OF THE HITCH!"

The breakaway cable is a separate connection. Winnebago is explicit: "Attach the breakaway cable. This must be a separate connection, not hooked to the safety chains." The breakaway switch pulls a pin and applies the trailer's own battery powered brakes if the trailer separates, so it only works if the cable is pulled, which it will not be if it is tied to the chains. Purdue Extension explains the same point: "the breakaway switch only works if the brake cable is attached to the towing truck's frame, not the safety chains. If the cable were attached to the safety chain, the cable would not be pulled when the chain is pulled." The switch also needs a charged trailer battery. A bench test is described by the generic manual: "Simply pull the pin out of the switch by hand and then try to pull the trailer. If you feel a significant drag force the brakes are activated. Be sure to re-insert the pin in the breakaway switch."

Unhitching is the reverse. Level the trailer, chock the wheels, reduce any sway bar tension, disconnect the chains and cables, raise the tongue jack with the jack to lift the coupler off the ball, remove the pin and slide the latch open, double check, then pull the tow vehicle forward. On a new trailer, Lance also says to "Check coupler tightness after towing 50 miles." and to "check to make sure they are tightened to the proper torque after the first 10, 25 and 50 miles of driving and before each tow thereafter." for the lug nuts.

Tongue weight. Tongue weight is how much of the trailer's weight rests on the hitch. It controls stability, so it matters more than almost anything else about loading. Ford gives the targets: "For trailers up to 2,000 lbs., tongue load not to exceed 200 lbs. For conventional trailers over 2,000 lbs., tongue load is 10% of loaded trailer weight. For 5th-wheel trailers, king pin weight is 15% of loaded trailer weight." Lance gives a range instead of one figure: "The hitch weight of a loaded trailer (including cargo) should be between 10-15% of the total weight of the trailer on a bumper pull trailer. The hitch weight of a loaded trailer (including cargo) should be between 20-25% of the total weight of the trailer on a 5th wheel trailer." Note that the two 5th-wheel figures differ, 15% against 20 to 25%.

Too little or too much tongue weight both cause trouble. Ford warns that "Too much tongue load or king pin weight can cause suspension/drivetrain damage and can press the vehicle down in back causing the front wheels to lift to the point where traction, steering response and braking can be severely decreased. Too little tongue load or king pin weight can reduce rear-wheel traction and cause instability, which may result in tail wagging or jackknifing."

Weight distribution. A weight distribution hitch uses spring bars to spread the tongue weight across the tow vehicle's axles so the pair sits level. It does not raise any rating. One retail source states plainly: "A weight-distribution hitch does not increase the truck's GVWR, rear gross axle weight rating, tire capacity, receiver rating, trailer GVWR, or legal towing capacity. It also cannot make a tail-heavy or overloaded trailer safe." Ratings are fixed at the factory, and adding parts does not change them: "Adding components such as air bags or overload springs does not allow these rating to be increased or exceeded."

Loading. Where the cargo sits sets the tongue weight. Common advice is "Load 60% of your cargo weight in front of the axle, 40% behind." and "Put the heaviest items in first, centered and low." The hitch maker Equal-i-zer publishes a different split: "About 85 percent of the trailer's total weight should be positioned forward of the axle or axles, with no more than 15 percent behind", and says it should not be confused with the 60/40 rule. The two percentages disagree. The one point every source agrees on is the outcome: 10 to 15% tongue weight for a bumper pull, measured, not guessed. A general rule that both sides share is "Heavy items should be placed low and as close to the axle positions as reasonable."

Sway. Sway is the trailer swinging side to side behind the tow vehicle. Lance lists the triggers: "Anticipate the trailer 'swaying.' Swaying can be caused by excessive steering, wind gusts, roadway edges, or by the trailer reaction to the pressure wave created by passing trucks and buses." The root cause is usually loading: too little tongue weight makes the trailer unstable, and the hitch maker Equal-i-zer says that "sway control is not a cure-all." and that "Sway begins with weight placement, not hardware."

If sway starts, the manual's instruction is specific. Lance: "When encountering trailer sway, take your foot off the accelerator, and steer as little as possible in order to stay on the road. Use small 'trim like' steering adjustments." And then: "Do not attempt to steer out of the sway; you'll only make it worse. Also, do not apply the tow vehicle brakes to correct trailer swaying. The application of the trailer brakes alone will tend to straighten out the combination, especially when going downhill." So the correction is the manual trailer brake lever, not the brake pedal and not the accelerator. Do not speed up to try to pull out of it, and avoid sudden lane changes.

The trailer brake controller is the tool for this. California's DMV describes what it does: "Most conventional and fifth-wheel trailers have electric brakes, activated by a controller in the tow vehicle. The controller automatically coordinates the tow vehicle and trailer braking so the two systems work together when the brake pedal is applied. The controller can also be helpful in stabilizing a trailer that sways because of bad road conditions." The same source adds that "Manually applying the trailer brakes by using the hand lever on the controller will stabilize a trailer that is likely to sway." Two related rules: the sway control bar, if fitted, must be disengaged before backing up, because as Equal-i-zer warns, "the sway control must be disengaged before backing up. Leaving it engaged while reversing forces the system to fight the turn, which can lead to binding, premature wear, or outright damage." And the hitch ratings must exceed the trailer: the ball, the spring bars and the sway control all have to be rated above the fully loaded trailer weight.

Fifth-wheel specifics

A fifth wheel carries part of its weight over the tow vehicle's rear axle, on a king pin that sits in a hitch in the bed. That placement is why a fifth wheel is steadier than a bumper pull: the pivot point is over the axle rather than behind it, so the trailer tracks behind the truck more predictably and sways less. The trade off is height, which raises the center of gravity and brings the trailer closer to the cab, so clearance has to be checked.

The lube plate. A fifth wheel hitch can be greased, or a plastic lube plate can be used instead to avoid the mess. The component maker gives the spec: "The plastic lube plate must not exceed 3/16 of an inch in thickness to ensure hitch will operate properly. Lube plate must be 12 inches in diameter or larger to properly distribute king pin weight." Without a lube plate, the same manual says to grease the skid plate and pivot pin, and to add grease to the jaw fitting every 6 months.

Hitch height and clearance. The hitch and pin box are set so the trailer rides about level. The maker's figure is "Allow approximately 6 inches clearance between the top of the pickup walls and the underside of the front of the trailer for pitch and roll of the trailer." For hitch placement, the distance from the back of the truck cab to the center of the rear axle "should be approximately 4 inches greater than one-half the trailer width."

Cab strike when turning. A fifth wheel can swing into the back of the truck cab on a sharp turn, which is why clearance is checked before driving. PullRite is the most explicit: "The distance between the cab and trailer should be greater than two inches at all points of the turn." It warns that "if you make a turn greater than 90 degrees the trailer will contact your truck and you WILL damage various parts of the hitch". The fix on short-bed trucks is a sliding hitch, which lets the pivot move back during a turn. A common mistake to avoid is called high hooking: "'High hooking' occurs when backing your hitch into a trailer that is set too high, resulting in the lower flange of the king pin to wedge itself against the metal edge of the lower horseshoe piece or against the Lock Jaw Assembly." Set the trailer low enough that the pin enters the jaws cleanly.

Backing up

Backing a trailer or a motorhome is where most damage happens, and it is a skill learned with practice rather than reading. The working method has three parts: properly adjusted side mirrors, a camera system if the RV has one, and a spotter on the ground at the rear corner who is working to agreed signals.

The mirrors are the main tool. Set them so you can see down both sides of the RV and the trailer or the rear corners. When you turn, look to the side you are turning toward. A camera helps but does not replace the mirrors: a single view can still hide the extreme rear corners when the RV is angled.

Use a spotter, and agree on signals first. Winnebago's setup guide states the rule as "Always use a spotter." Before you move, decide what each signal means, and decide on a stop signal that means stop now. If you cannot see the spotter in a mirror, stop. When you are unsure, the safest instruction is the one from an experienced driver: "When in doubt while backing up, get out and look." Getting out and looking is always faster than repairing a dent. No standard hand signal set for a recreational spotter was found in the sources behind this guide, so agree on your own before you start.

Two geometry points help. A trailer pivots opposite to the tow vehicle's steering: turn the truck left and the trailer's rear goes right. Make small corrections and let the trailer catch up rather than chasing it with big steering inputs. If you have a helper, walk the site first and identify the inside rear swing point before you position the RV.

Pop-ups and hybrids

A pop-up camper is a box with a lifting roof and folding beds. Setup runs in a fixed order, and the order protects the mechanism: level first, then lift, then stabilize. Leveling happens before anything is raised, because a tilted camper loads the lift arms unevenly. Stabilizers go down after the roof is up, and they only steady the camper, they do not lift it.

The roof is raised with a crank or a power lift. The maker is strict about what the roof will carry. Outfitter warns that "You should never raise or lower your roof if there is anything on top of it! The system was not designed to raise or hold any additional weight." When cranking by hand, the caution is "Make sure the crank is pushed forward into the spur gear. (If the crank gears become free, the roof will fall.)" A widely repeated rule is never to raise or lower the roof with the stabilizers down, and never to go inside before the stabilizers are down. Model manuals vary, so check yours.

Canvas care. The cardinal rule is that a pop-up must never be closed wet. Outfitter and OPUS both state it. OPUS: "Please ensure canvas is dry before packing down." and "A wet/damp canvas will develop mildew/mould very quickly and damage your canvas permanently." If you have to close up in the rain, open it again and dry it out as soon as you can.

Cleaning the canvas is where the sources disagree, and the safest advice is the canvas maker's own manual. OPUS, a tent maker, forbids all cleaners: the tent can be cleaned only with fresh water and a damp cloth, and "UNDER NO CIRCUMSTANCES MUST DETERGENTS OR CHEMICAL CLEANERS be used". Practitioner sources forbid bleach specifically: "Do NOT use household bleach. Bleach degrades canvas fibers at the molecular level, causes permanent discoloration". Meanwhile a forum post attributed to a fabric maker recommends a dish soap, bleach and water mix. That directly contradicts the canvas makers, so follow the manual for your specific canvas. New canvas can seep at the seams the first few times it is wetted, and some makers say this is normal and will stop as the threads swell. Drying time estimates, such as three to five hours in strong sun and six to eight hours on a cloudy day, come from practitioner sources rather than manufacturers.

The lift system. The roof rides on telescoping posts and cables routed over pulleys. Forest River's lifter guide gives the maintenance: "Clean and recoat lifter posts externally, with a rust prohibitive, non-drying lubricant.", "Annually grease zerks on front, bottom and top of master tube.", and to lubricate the springs annually by raising the roof and oiling each segment of the post, with the caution "To help avoid tent damage, immediately remove any excess oil on the external portion of the post." One parts supplier adds that the posts should get a light silicone spray rather than oil, because oil attracts dirt, and "Do not use oil/grease on posts (attracts dirt)."

The system's weak point is the pulleys and cables. A service document explains why: "The pulley separator package is the first routing point of all four individual lift arm main cables. In essence, this pivot point is the most crucial, having the most pressure or strain put forth by raising the top assembly." and "Total number of steel bearing pulleys in system: 24." A binding roof when raising or lowering usually points to a camper that is not level, worn bushings, seized or broken pulleys, a bent lift arm or twisted cables. After any lift system part is replaced, the repair manual requires a test: "WHEN REPLACING ANY LIFT SYSTEM PART, YOU SHOULD ALWAYS RAISE AND LOWER THE UNIT 4 OR 5 TIMES TO MAKE SURE IT IS OPERATING PROPERLY."

Tank capacity is the honest gap. No manufacturer owner's manual for a soft-sided tent trailer was found that states its fresh, gray and black tank sizes, so no figure is given here. What is known is adjacent rather than primary. Pop-ups are built lightly enough that some are exempt from trailer brake rules by weight, and small towables typically carry a cassette toilet or a porta-potty instead of a true black tank. Treat any tank number you read for a specific pop-up as unverified until you check that model's own manual. The practical effect is that pop-ups lean on campground bathhouses, dump stations and water spigots, but that is an inference, not a sourced figure.

Questions people ask first

The things a new owner searches for, answered from the sections above.

How often should I dump the black tank?

When it is about two thirds full, and never by leaving the valve open at a full-hookup site. Leaving it open lets the liquid drain away and the solids stay, which is how a tank becomes a pile. The manual is explicit that the valve stays closed: "keep the black-water holding tank gate valve closed at all times, except when dumping".

Precharge it afterwards. A tank that starts dry is a tank that starts with a problem: "After dumping, always precharge your tank before using it again." The same manual gives the recipe as one to two quarts in the bowl, then a flush that puts at least two gallons into the tank.

Dump black first, then grey. The grey water rinses the hose, and the order is in the manual: "Drain the black-water tank first before draining the gray-water tank."

Do I need a water pressure regulator?

Yes, on city water. Campground supply pressure varies and the plumbing in an RV is built for less than a house system. The manual states the threshold: "NOTE: If city water pressure is more than 60 psi, Tiffin Motor Homes recommends to use a water pressure regulator to prevent damage to the motorhome." It goes on the tap end of your drinking-water hose, not inside the compartment.

How long do RV tires last?

The manufacturers disagree, and anybody who tells you there is one answer is repeating a rule nobody publishes. Goodyear's service bulletin says "6 years" from the date of manufacture. Michelin says inspect from five years and replace by ten. NHTSA describes manufacturer practice as six to ten.

The "5-7-10 rule" is not an RVIA or NHTSA rule. It circulates widely and traces to no published document from either body. Tire age is decided by the maker of your tire, not by a rule of thumb.

Age is read from the DOT code on the sidewall, not from tread depth. A tire with plenty of tread and a 2016 date code is a ten-year-old tire.

What should I do on the very first day?

Level first, before the slide-outs move and before an absorption fridge runs. Fill the water heater before it is switched on. Keep the electrical load off while the shore cord goes in. The twelve items at the top of this page are the ones that cost money if they are got wrong, and each of them is a minute of work.

The 13 mistakes that cost new RV owners money

Never plug into a pedestal you have not tested

Test the pedestal before your cord goes anywhere near it. Use a ground monitor, or a surge protector with one built in. If it shows reverse polarity or an open ground, do not connect. Do not plug into a receptacle that shows outward signs of heat damage, either: report it rather than use it. A ground monitor is the cheapest thing you will ever buy for an RV, and it is the only thing standing between a bad pedestal and your converter. The outlets and GFCI guide covers what to do when the damage is already done.

Never run the generator while you are plugged in

Never run the generator while the RV is plugged into shore power. One source or the other, never both: they are two separate supplies feeding one panel. Start the generator, check its own breaker, and switch the RV over to it, or plug in, but not both at once.

Fill the water heater before you switch it on

An empty water heater burns out its tank the first time it is switched on, and that damage is not covered by warranty. Fill the tank first. Open a hot tap and let it run until the flow is steady, with no spitting or sputtering. That is the confirmation the tank is full, and only then does the electric element or the burner get switched on.

Never leave the fridge running long in an RV that is badly out of level

Never leave the fridge running for long in a badly out-of-level RV. Driving does not matter: the motion of the road keeps the coolant moving, so the RV does not need to be level while you travel. Parked is what counts. An absorption fridge has no pump and relies on gravity to circulate the coolant around a sealed loop, and sat at a slant for days with the fridge running, the circulation stalls and the boiler overheats. Norcold's limit is 3 degrees off level side to side and 6 front to back, and running past it "can cause damage to the cooling system and create a risk of personal injury or property damage." A night or two slightly off is not the problem. Weeks at a slant is. If the RV is going to sit well out of level, level it or switch the fridge off. The fridge guide covers what to do when one stops cooling.

Fit a water pressure regulator before you hook up

Campground water pressure varies, and some of it is high enough to split a line. A regulator screws onto the tap end of your drinking-water hose and holds the pressure down. It costs very little and it is the difference between a quiet weekend and finding water running out from under the cabinets. If the park runs above about 60 psi you want one, and you cannot tell by looking at the tap.

Never leave the RV while filling the fresh water tank

Do not walk away from a tank that is filling, and do not overfill it. Overfilling pressurises the tank, and that causes leakage and water damage. It also voids the warranty. Do not cap, block or modify the overflow tubes: they are what lets the pressure out.

Never leave the black tank valve open

Dump the black tank, then close it. Leaving the valve open at a full-hookup site lets the liquid drain away and leaves the solids behind, and that is how a tank turns into a pile you have to dig out. So keep it shut, and dump when the tank is at least two thirds full so the rush of water carries the solids with it. The toilet guide covers what to do when it has already gone wrong.

Never leave the awning out in wind

An awning is a sail, and it does not have to be a storm to destroy one. A gust that feels like nothing standing on the ground puts a load on an awning that its arms and its attachment to the wall were never built for. Roll it in before you leave the RV, before you sleep, and any time the forecast mentions gusts. The same goes for leaving it out in heavy rain, which pools on it and can bend the arms.

Know your height before you drive

Write your clearance on a sticky note and put it where you will see it. Height is the one number a new owner never knows and the one that does the most damage the fastest: a low bridge, a drive-through, a car-park barrier or a tree branch over a campsite road will take the roof, the air conditioner and the vent covers off before you can stop. Measure the RV yourself with it loaded rather than trusting the specification, and add the air conditioner if it was not factory fitted.

Never drive with a slide out

A slide room travels in. There is no exception and there is no short move: the room is not built to carry load sideways, the seals will not survive it, and on the road it is a wall sticking out past your mirrors. If a room will not retract, and you have to move the RV, that is the one case where you read the slide-out guide first: the room has to be secured by its own system, not by pushing it in.

Check your tire pressure before every trip

Under-inflation is what kills trailer tires, and it does not look like anything from the outside. Check all of them cold, against the pressure on the RV's own placard rather than the figure moulded into the sidewall, then set the final figure from the load and inflation table for the weight each axle actually carries. Check the spare while you are there. A tire that is visibly bulging or that has lost pressure between trips has a fault to find, not a pressure to top up. The tire guide covers reading them and the tire date decoder tells you how old they are.

Never travel with full waste tanks

Never travel with full black or grey tanks. A full tank is weight moving at the worst possible place in the RV, and the structure and the tank mounts were not built for that load shifting. Dump before you drive, not after you arrive.

Bring the stabilisers up before you change the RV's level

Bring the stabilisers up before you change the RV's level. Stabilisers steady an RV; they do not carry it. So before you touch the tongue jack, the landing gear or any levelling block, wind the stabilisers up. Leave them down and raise or lower the RV and you are putting its weight onto legs that were never built to take it, which bends them and stresses the frame around them. The order is always the same: stabilisers up, adjust the level, stabilisers back down.

Which RV system to learn first, and why

First: shore power, because it is the one you meet first

The 120-volt side is the part of an RV that can harm you, and it is the first thing you connect, so it gets learned first. The main thing to consider before your first hookup is that the pedestal is somebody else's wiring: test it before you trust it, and switch the breaker on the pedestal rather than the plug. The outlets and GFCI guide covers the chain from the pedestal inward, including why a GFCI will not reset.

Second: 12 volts, because a low battery looks like several faults at once

Half the RV runs on 12 volts, so a battery that is low makes the water pump weak, the furnace fan slow and the fridge's board unhappy. That is why it is learned second: low voltage imitates three unrelated faults, and knowing this system stops you chasing the wrong part before you start. One thing to know early: do not pull a slide room in on a low battery. The motors run unevenly when the voltage sags, and the room can come in crooked. Charge first if there is any doubt. The 12-volt guide has the layout and the voltage drop test, and the battery guide works out which of the four charging sources has failed when the battery will not hold charge.

Third: water in, then water out

Two systems that share a set of pipes and a set of habits. Water in is city water or the tank and its pump, and water out is two tanks and a valve. Learn them in that order because the fresh side is where you will first notice a leak, and because every fault on the waste side is a smell or a reading rather than a flood. The winterising guide doubles as the map of where the water actually sits, and the toilet guide covers the three faults that sound alike at the other end of it.

Fourth: propane, heat and the fridge

Propane is the one fuel you can learn by smell. The furnace and the water heater are the two appliances that burn most of it; an absorption fridge runs on propane as well, but it uses very little. Two things are worth knowing before the first cold night: how the furnace proves itself safe to light, and that an absorption fridge needs the RV close to level to work at all. The furnace guide carries the carbon monoxide hard stop, and the fridge guide explains why level matters.

Fifth: the extras, and what can wait

Solar, a generator and an inverter are all ways of making or moving power, and none of them is needed to spend a first weekend at a hookup. When one of them stops working, solar and the generator both have their own guide, and both begin by checking two things before anything is tested.

How the 12-volt and 120-volt systems connect

Two systems share one battery and one ground, and almost every confusing electrical symptom comes from that arrangement. 120 volts arrives at the pedestal and goes to the outlets, the air conditioner and any heating element. The converter turns some of that into 12 volts to charge the battery. Everything else runs off the battery at 12 volts.

Several components touch both halves, and that is where a fault crosses from one to the other: the converter, which turns 120 into 12; an inverter, if one is fitted, which turns 12 back into 120; and every appliance with a 120-volt element and a 12-volt board, which is the fridge in its electric mode, a dual-fuel water heater, and the air conditioner's controls.

Quick answers: the questions new owners ask first

Short answers to the questions new owners ask first. Each one links to the section that covers it in full.

How often should I dump the black tank?

When it is about two thirds full, and never by leaving the valve open at a full-hookup site. Leaving it open lets the liquid drain away and the solids stay, which is how a tank becomes a pile. Keep the black-water holding tank gate valve closed at all times, except when dumping.

Precharge it afterwards. After dumping, always put water back in before the tank is used again: start with one to two quarts in the bowl, then add about five gallons of water to the tank.

Dump the black tank first, then the grey, so the grey water rinses the hose.

Do I need a water pressure regulator?

Yes, on city water. Campground supply pressure varies and the plumbing in an RV is built for less than a house system. The manual states the threshold: "NOTE: If city water pressure is more than 60 psi, Tiffin Motor Homes recommends to use a water pressure regulator to prevent damage to the motorhome." Fit it at the tap end where the RV allows it, and do not leave it out.

How long do RV tires last?

The manufacturers disagree, and anybody who tells you there is one answer is repeating a rule nobody publishes. Goodyear's service bulletin says "6 years" from the date of manufacture. Michelin says inspect from five years and replace by ten. NHTSA describes manufacturer practice as six to ten.

The "5-7-10 rule" is not an RVIA or NHTSA rule. It circulates widely and traces to no published document from either body. Tire age is decided by the maker of your tire, not by a rule of thumb.

Age is read from the DOT code on the sidewall, not from tread depth. A tire with plenty of tread and a 2016 date code is a ten-year-old tire.

What should I do on my very first day with an RV?

Level first, before the slide-outs move and before an absorption fridge runs. Fill the water heater before it is switched on. Keep the electrical load off while the shore cord goes in.

Can I leave my RV plugged in all winter?

Plugged in is not the same as stored. Power keeps the battery charged and can run a dehumidifier, but the plumbing still has to be winterised and the inside still has to breathe, because a closed RV collects condensation all winter whether or not it has power. See storing an RV and winterising for the two halves of it.

How long will the battery last when the RV is parked?

Weeks, not months. The detectors, the fridge control board, the radio memory and the thermostat all draw current continuously, and that is enough to flatten a parked battery in a few weeks. A battery disconnect switch or a trickle charger is the fix. See the two electrical systems.

Why does the fresh water taste of plastic?

Almost always the hose. A garden hose is not made to carry drinking water and it leaves a plastic or rubber taste, so use a hose labelled for potable water, and keep a second hose for the black-tank flush. See fresh water.

Can I run the fridge on propane while I drive?

The rule here is to shut the propane off at the tank while the RV is moving, and both the pre-trip walkaround and the propane section set it out. An absorption fridge holds its temperature for a few hours with the door kept shut, and the tank has to be off before a tunnel, a bridge, a ferry or a fuel stop in any case. See propane.

Can I run the air conditioner on battery power?

Not on a normal battery bank. Air conditioning is a 120-volt load that draws more than a realistic battery and inverter combination can supply for long, so it needs shore power, a generator, or a large inverter. See the appliances.

How often should I sanitise the fresh water tank?

Annually in the working schedule, and again after storage or any stretch when the system has sat unused. The recipe and the limit are in fresh water.

Do RV water heaters need an anode rod?

It depends on the tank. Suburban steel tanks use one and want it replaced about yearly; Atwood and Dometic aluminium tanks do not use one at all, and fitting one can damage the tank. See the appliances.

Can I run the generator while I drive?

On a motorhome, yes: the generator is built to run while the vehicle is moving. What you must never do is run the generator while the RV is plugged into shore power. See shore power.

What is the difference between 30-amp and 50-amp service?

Capacity. A 30-amp supply gives about 3,600 watts and a 50-amp supply about 12,000, close to three times as much, which is why one air conditioner is comfortable on 30 amps and two are not. See shore power.

How level does an RV fridge need to be?

Norcold publishes the figure: within "3° off level side-to-side and 6° off level front-to-back", and operating past it "can cause damage to the cooling system and create a risk of personal injury or property damage." Other makers describe the same requirement as level enough to live in comfortably. It applies while parked, not while driving. See the appliances.

Do I need wheel chocks?

Yes, every time, and even on level ground. They go in before the stabilisers or the levelers rather than after, and a motorhome on its jacks is still chocked. See the first night.

How do I keep the RV from freezing when camping in the cold?

Keep heat inside, leave the plumbing compartments open to it, and do not let the interior drop below freezing overnight. Underbelly insulation and heated tanks are what decide whether a given RV can do it at all. See the freezing section, and underbelly insulation and heated tanks.

How do I winterise an RV?

Two ways, and both do the same job: getting every drop of water out of a system that will freeze and split. Either blow the lines out with air, or fill them with antifreeze. A winterising job that stops halfway is what still freezes. See winterising.

Do I need a brake controller to tow a travel trailer?

If the trailer has electric brakes, yes. The controller is what coordinates trailer braking with the tow vehicle, and what lets you apply the trailer brakes alone when the combination starts to sway. Whether brakes are required at a given weight is its own guide: when trailer brakes are required.

How do I dump the tanks at a dump station?

Connect and lock the hose at both ends first. Then open the black valve and let it drain, open the grey to rinse the hose, and close both. Wear gloves, fit a clear elbow, and keep the sewer hose clear of anything that touches drinking water. See dumping in the right order.

Can I use regular toilet paper in an RV toilet?

Any paper that breaks down in water is fine. Failures come from paper that does not break down, and from using too little water. Paper wrappers, gum, cigarettes and the wipes sold as flushable do not belong in the tank. See black tank chemicals.

How often should the roof be resealed?

Inspect sealant every six months and replace what has failed, and clean the roof every three months. Do not wait for a leak, because the sealant is the only thing keeping water out of the structure. See seals, roof and delamination and the maintenance schedule.

How do I get internet in an RV?

A cellular plan with a hotspot or a router, or a satellite service, or both with a way to switch between them. Shop the coverage, not the marketing claims. See internet, TV and power.

Does an RV need a cover for winter storage?

A cover keeps sun, dirt and tree sap off it, and it does nothing at all about the moisture inside, which is what actually damages a stored RV. If you cover it, keep it ventilated as well. See storing an RV.

How long can I camp without hookups?

There is no single number, because it depends on the battery bank, the fresh tank and how sparing you are. The furnace is usually what ends a dry camp first: its blower runs on 12 volts all night. See the two electrical systems.

Should I level the RV with the stabilisers?

No. Stabilisers steady the RV, they do not carry it. Level with a jack or the leveling system first, then bring the stabilisers down to stop the rocking. See leveling and stabilising.

Do I need a water filter?

Not always. A filter helps with chlorine, dirt and taste, but a whole-RV filter is not guaranteed to remove the taste and odour of iron or sulfur, and it should be changed every 6 to 12 months. See fresh water.

Why does the tank gauge read wrong?

Because it is not a gauge. The panel steps in thirds and reads empty, one third, two thirds or full, and the sensors are the least reliable part of the system. Treat it as a rough warning light. See reading the monitor panel.

Why does the RV smell of sewer inside?

Usually not the tank. A dried-out P-trap or a black valve left open is the common cause. See sewer smell inside.

Can I leave the grey tank valve open?

Grey is a different case, and some makers and parks allow it to stay open. Black never does: leaving it open lets the liquid drain away and leaves the solids to dry and harden in the tank. See dumping in the right order.

What is the one maintenance job I should not skip?

Sealant, and the roof under it. Water getting in is what destroys an RV, and it is the cheapest failure to prevent and the most expensive one to find late. See the maintenance schedule and seals, roof and delamination.

How to plug in your RV on the first night

The RV has to be level before the slide-outs move and before an absorption fridge runs, the water heater has to be full before it is switched on, and the electrical load should be off while the cord goes in. Work in this order and each step makes the next one safe.

  • Park, then level. Level side to side first, then front to back. On a travel trailer, side to side is set with blocks or ramps under the wheels, and front to back is the small adjustment made last, with the tongue jack or the landing gear. On a motorhome it is the leveling system, hydraulic or air, and the order it runs in is the maker's, so read that page of the manual before the first time you use it. Either way, the RV is level before a slide-out moves.
  • Parking brake, and the engine off, on a motorhome. The engine goes off and the parking brake is set before the jacks go down or a slide moves. Some leveling systems will not operate otherwise, and the brake is what keeps the RV from rolling while the jacks are taking weight.
  • Chock the wheels. Chock them even on level ground, and put them in before the stabilisers or the levelers rather than after. A motorhome on its jacks is still chocked.
  • Look at the pedestal, then test it. Test the outlet with a surge protector that has diagnostics built in, before the cord goes anywhere near it. A pedestal that is scorched, cracked or loose is one to report rather than use.
  • Make the power connection with the pedestal breaker off. The RV end of the cord goes in first, then the pedestal end, and the breaker goes on last. Running off a generator instead of a hookup: start the generator first, check the breaker on the generator itself, then switch the RV over to it.
  • Check the RV's own breaker panel for anything tripped, and switch the converter on if it has its own switch.
  • Confirm the battery is charging. Watch the battery gauge on the monitor panel. If it read three quarters before you plugged in and it reads full a little later, the charger is working. The battery guide covers what to do when the reading does not move.
  • Water next. Connect the drinking-water hose through a pressure regulator, then open a cold tap inside and let it run to push the air out of the lines. Do not walk away from a tank that is filling.
  • Fill the water heater before it is switched on. Open a hot tap and let it run until the flow is steady, with no spitting or sputtering. That is what confirms the tank is full. Only then does the electric element or the burner get switched on.
  • Propane last. Open the main valve slowly, and if you smell gas, stop and find out why before anything is lit.
  • Check the detectors. The propane and carbon-monoxide alarms run on the 12-volt system, so they should be working off the house battery from the moment you arrive, not only once the RV is plugged in.
May cause damageTwo of these steps are the ones that cause damage when they are done out of order: the cord goes in with the pedestal breaker off, and the water heater stays off until a hot tap runs without sputtering. The rest are habits rather than a strict sequence.
The RV pre-trip walkaround checklist

A walkaround is a fixed loop around the RV before every departure, done in the same direction each time so that nothing is skipped. The point is not a detailed inspection. It is to catch the handful of things that are dangerous or expensive when they are left loose or left behind.

  • Tires. Check pressure against the figure on the RV's placard, not the number on the sidewall, and look for cuts and bulges. Two age facts matter. The build date is the last four digits of the DOT code: the first two digits are the week from 01 to 52, and the last two are the year. A tire can be too old while still looking new, so replace a tire that has reached your tire maker's own age figure, even if it looks serviceable and has not reached the treadwear limit. Check tread depth with a coin: a tire is unsafe when the minimum tread depth reaches 2/32 inch, the distance between the top of Lincoln's head and the edge on a penny.
  • Height. Know the tallest point of the RV before you drive anywhere, because an awning, an antenna and an air conditioner all add to it. Measure from the ground all the way to the top of the highest object on the RV, then post that number on your dashboard so you do not forget it.
  • The hitch or the coupling. For a trailer, the tongue weight wants to be roughly a tenth to a sixth of the loaded trailer weight, which is 10 to 15 percent. Load the heavy items low and forward, and keep them out of the back. Never load heavy items in the back of the trailer or on the back bumper.
  • Everything that folds, slides or latches. Awning in, antenna down, steps up, slide-outs in, roof vents closed, compartment doors latched. Use a checklist every time you arrive at or leave a campsite, because it is easy to forget simple things like unplugging power cords or lowering the TV antenna.
  • Propane while moving. Turn the propane supply off at the tank while driving.
A checklist is not a sign of inexperience. The failures it prevents are the familiar ones: an awning left out, a television antenna left up, or a cord still plugged in as the RV pulls away.
How to hook up an RV at a campsite

There are three hookups, and one habit sets the order for all of them: make and break each connection at the point that can arc or spill, with the power off while you do it. Water and power can be done in either order. The sewer goes last.

Water. The order is spigot, regulator, hose, filter, RV. Some RVs take the regulator at the tap and some at the inlet; the point is that it sits between the park's pressure and your plumbing.

  • Use a dedicated drinking-water hose. A garden hose is not made to carry water you drink.
  • Fit the pressure regulator at the spigot end.
  • Add an inline filter if the RV does not already have one.
  • Connect to the city-water inlet. The fresh-tank fill and the city-water inlet are often two separate fittings, and on some RVs a single fitting and a diverter valve do both jobs, so find out which arrangement yours has.
  • Turn the park tap on slowly, then crack a cold tap inside and let it run to push the air out of the lines. A line full of air lets go in a hard, spitting rush, so open the tap a little at a time rather than wide open, and stay with it while it runs.

Power. The RV end of the cord goes in first and comes out last, so the live end is always the one in your hand. Between the pedestal and the cord there is one more device, and it is yours: a surge protector or EMS goes into the pedestal outlet first, and the RV's cord goes into it.

  • Switch off the air conditioner, the water heater and other heavy loads, and switch off the RV's main breaker.
  • Turn the pedestal breaker off.
  • Inspect the pedestal outlet, and do not use one that is scorched, cracked or loose. Report it.
  • Plug your surge protector or EMS into the pedestal outlet, then turn the pedestal breaker on.
  • Let the EMS run its check and show a pass before the RV is connected to anything. If it does not pass, do not connect.
  • Connect the RV end of the shore cord to the RV first, so the live end is the last thing in your hand.
  • Plug the other end into the EMS, not into the pedestal.
  • Switch the RV's main breaker on, then bring appliances on one at a time.
  • Running off a generator instead of a pedestal: start the generator first and check the breaker on the generator itself, then switch the RV over to it.

The protector is separate from the RV, and it sits in the middle. A portable inline unit goes into the pedestal and the RV's cord goes into it, which is the arrangement to buy: a unit mounted behind the wall protects the outlet but not the cord, and the cord is what sits on the wet ground. What it is really doing is not absorbing lightning. It reads the pedestal's voltage and wiring and refuses to pass current when the ground is open, the neutral is open, the polarity is reversed, or the voltage is outside range. Those are the faults a campground pedestal actually develops, and none of them is visible by looking at it. Progressive Industries is one widely used maker of the portable kind. The shore power section below covers what each reading means.

Sewer. Connect the hose at both ends before you open anything, and keep the black valve closed. Never leave it open through your stay. The order never changes: black first, then grey. Drain the black tank first and let it empty, then open the grey, because the grey water is what rinses the black residue out of the shared hose. Dump when the black tank is at least two thirds full.

  • Wear gloves. Fit a clear elbow at the outlet so you can see what is moving: it is the only window into the black tank.
  • At a dump station with a smooth inlet, a rubber donut, sometimes called a sewer doughnut or hose adapter, makes the hose fit snugly against the station inlet. Some campgrounds require one.
  • If you want a leak check before you start, crack the grey valve for a few seconds and close it again, so a bad fitting shows up as grey water rather than black. That is a check, not the dump: when you dump, black still goes first and grey follows it.
  • Weight the elbow with a rock or a brick so the hose cannot kick loose. Most dump stations have a rock nearby for exactly this purpose.
  • Dump black, then grey, then close both valves.
  • Precharge the empty tank so it does not start dry. After dumping, add 5 to 10 gallons of water and treatment chemical before using it again.
  • Never rinse, set down or store the sewer hose near a fresh-water tap, and never let it touch the hose you drink from. Cross-contamination is the one mistake on this list that can make you ill rather than just cost you money.

Disconnecting is the reverse. Loads off, breakers off, pedestal end first, then the RV end. A generator is the same: shut it down, or switch the RV off it, before the cord is touched.

Arriving at a campsite: what to do when you pull in

Arrival is a sequence too, and the first step happens before you drive onto the site.

  • Check in, then look at the site on foot. Walk the pad before you commit the RV to it, and note which side the pedestal and the sewer connection are on, so you park with the connections within reach.
  • Do not cut across your neighbour. Do not cut through another occupied site to reach the bathroom, the lake, or a trail.
  • If your vehicle is in somebody's way, move it before they ask. An RV backing into a tight site has very little room, and the van parked opposite is often the thing making it hard. Offering costs you nothing and it is what everyone on a campground expects of everyone else.
  • Back or pull in with a spotter. Use someone outside who can see what you cannot, and agree on the hand signals before you start.
  • If you are backing in after dark, put lights on the ground. A torch laid on the ground at each corner you are aiming for, or one at the far end of the pad, gives you something fixed to steer by. It is far easier than judging distance against the dark.
  • Level, then chock. Side to side first, then front to back, as set out above.
  • Hook up water, power and sewer in the order given earlier, keeping the black valve closed.
  • Keep the noise reasonable. If you can hear it two sites away, it is too loud. Official quiet hours might end at 8 AM, but generators and slide motors before 8 AM are rude even if they are technically permitted.
Some rules apply before you leave home. How long an RV can be parked in a residential driveway varies by municipality and HOA, so it is worth a quick check of your local ordinance.
New RV owner mistakes to avoid in the first week

The first-week mistakes are remarkably consistent, and most of them are planning mistakes rather than operating mistakes. The machines can be learned in an afternoon. A rushed plan cannot be fixed at the campsite.

  • Going too far on the first day. Most experienced RVers prefer traveling between 200 and 330 miles per day. A new owner who plans a long first day arrives tired, in the dark, at a site they have never seen.
  • Skipping the shakedown trip. Take a short, close-to-home shakedown trip before venturing off on a longer one. A first trip that is also the first use of everything is a first trip with no margin.
  • Making the first trip hard. Book somewhere forgiving, a full-hookup park with pull-through sites rather than a tight loop. Pick a destination within one to three hours from home and stay two or three nights.
  • Not capturing the walkthrough. The dealer handover is the one time someone who knows the RV shows you how it works. Record the orientation so you can refer to it later.
  • Overpacking. Every RV has weight limits, and staying under them is vital to a smooth trip.
  • Taking delivery with known faults. Before you sign, the problems belong to the dealer. If your inspection finds significant issues, refuse to take delivery until they are resolved.
  • Missing the warranty window. After 90 days, these items are no longer covered. The 90-day provision usually covers the small adjustment items a first season shakes loose, so write the list down as you find things rather than remembering at the end.
  • Leaving the black valve open at a full-hookup site, which is covered above and belongs on this list anyway.

RV electrical systems: 12 volt and 120 volt explained

An RV carries two electrical systems at once, and almost every beginner confusion comes from that one fact. Every RV has two separate electrical systems: a 12-volt DC system and a 120-volt AC system. The 12-volt system is powered by the RV's battery bank and runs things like lights, water pumps, exhaust fans, and control boards for appliances. The 120-volt system powers heavier appliances and outlets, similar to a home, and comes from shore power, a generator, or an inverter. This is what runs air conditioners, microwaves, and standard plug-in devices.

SystemPowered byTypical loads
12 V DCThe house battery, kept charged by the converter, the alternator, solar, the generator or the tow vehicleLights, water pump, furnace blower and controls, vent fans, slide and leveler motors, the electric tongue jack, fridge control boards, propane and carbon-monoxide detectors, thermostats
120 V ACShore power, a generator, or an inverterAir conditioning, microwave, the electric water-heater element, household outlets, a household-style television

The distinction to hold onto is about when each one works. The 12-volt system works all the time as long as the batteries have charge. The 120-volt system only works when you are plugged into shore power, running a generator, or using an inverter. A furnace is the clearest example of an appliance that uses both: the burner runs on propane, but the fan that moves the heat runs on 12 volts, so a furnace will not work off a flat battery even with a full propane tank.

What charges the 12-volt system. The battery is not charged by one thing, and a battery that will not hold its charge usually means one of them has stopped. On shore power it is the converter, and the generator charges it the same way, because the generator feeds the converter. Solar is another source where it is fitted. On a motorhome the engine alternator charges the house battery while you drive on most models, through an isolator or a combiner. That is common but not universal, so check your own manual. On a travel trailer the 7-pin cord carries a charge line from the tow vehicle, and Scamp's owner's manual lists the tow vehicle as a charge source that will "begin charging your house battery." Scamp's manual adds that the result depends on the tow vehicle's wiring and its output, so treat it as a trickle charge rather than a recharge: "the charging of the trailer batteries via this method is best considered trickle or maintenance." An electric tongue jack runs on 12 volts as well, so a flat battery takes the jack with it.

The battery goes flat with everything switched off. The propane and carbon-monoxide detectors, the fridge's control board, the radio's memory and the thermostat all draw a small current all the time, and over days parked that is enough to flatten a battery. It is why a stored RV wants a battery disconnect switch or a trickle charger. It is also the reason to think before you put a slide out: arrive with a low battery, extend the room, and by the morning there may not be enough charge left to bring it back in.

Converter, inverter, battery. The four words that confuse new owners are easier once the direction of travel is clear. A converter goes from AC to DC; an inverter goes from DC to AC; an inverter/charger does both; the battery stores DC. A converter converts 120-volt AC to 12-volt DC to power low-voltage DC loads, charges the battery, and distributes both 12-volt and 120-volt current to the various branch circuits in the RV. Almost every RV has a converter, because it is what charges the battery on shore power. An inverter is a different device and it is not universal: plenty of travel trailers have no inverter at all, and where one is fitted it is usually part of a package bought for a residential fridge or for running 120-volt loads off the battery. A quick way to tell the two faults apart: flat batteries while plugged in is converter territory, while dead outlets while boondocking is inverter territory. An inverter only feeds the outlets wired to it, and circuits that are generally not included in inverter loads are air conditioners, refrigerators, washer-dryers, and other high-current-draw loads.

The rule: if a load plugs into a wall outlet, or is a heavy appliance such as an air conditioner, a microwave or a water-heater element, it is 120 volts AC and works only with shore power, a generator or an inverter. If a load is a light, a pump, a fan or a control board, it is 12 volts DC and works whenever the house battery has charge.

When something stops working, check it in a fixed order: name the failed load, identify whether it needs AC, DC or both, confirm the source, check the protection, and inspect the wiring and the device last.

Battery sizing is the one electrical question with no published universal answer. The amp-hour capacity an RV needs depends on its loads and how it is used, and no manufacturer gives a formula. The sizing calculators that circulate are practitioner tools rather than specifications, so they can guide a choice but cannot settle one.

Can injure youMore than one source can be live at once. An RV may be energised by shore power, an inverter and the batteries at the same time, so every source has to be found before any wiring is touched. Shore power and the generator are the exception: those two are never run together. De-energise before you open anything, and do not trust a switch. A correct shutdown works through every source: turn the pedestal breaker off before the cord comes out, unplug the shore cord, shut the generator down, disconnect the negative battery cable, and then deal with the inverter. The inverter is the one that matters most, because it is the source that stays live after everything else is off: it turns battery power back into 120 volts, and it can keep selected outlets energised with no shore power and no generator running. Switching it off at the unit is not enough. Xantrex warns that "Turning off controls will not reduce this risk" and that the switch on an inverter/charger does not turn off the charger section. Disconnect the inverter's AC and DC inputs, which is what Xantrex calls for, and allow the several minutes its manual may ask for before the capacitors are safe to touch. Before working on anything, confirm the circuit is dead with a meter rather than by looking at a switch position, because an inverter usually feeds only the outlets that were wired to it, and you cannot guess which outlets those are. To work out what any one appliance draws on either system, the watts to amps calculator does the arithmetic.
RV shore power: 30 amp vs 50 amp, and testing the pedestal

The pedestal is the post at the campsite that carries the receptacles and, usually, the breakers. It carries some combination of a household-style outlet, a 30-amp RV outlet and a 50-amp RV outlet, and which ones are present varies by park and by the age of the site. Or a generator, which does the same job. A generator makes its own 120 volts and feeds the same panel and the same outlets the pedestal would. Two things change: you have to start it, and you have to check the breaker on the generator itself. Everything below about loads, breakers and protection applies to a generator exactly as it does to a pedestal.

The arithmetic is why the two services are not close. A 30-amp shore cord is rated at 120 volts, so the most it can deliver is 3,600 watts. A 50-amp cord is rated at 240 volts, so the most it can deliver is 12,000 watts. A 50-amp supply therefore carries close to three times the power of a 30-amp one.

Test it before the cord goes in. The pedestal breaker goes off first, then the outlet is tested with a surge protector that has diagnostics built in, or with a multimeter if you carry one. Three green lights mean the tester found none of the faults it can see; they are not a clearance, because the fault described below is invisible to it. On a fail, do not connect: move the RV to another pedestal, or have the fault corrected. A pedestal that is scorched, cracked or loose is one to report rather than use.

  • Turn off high-draw appliances and the RV's main breaker.
  • Turn the pedestal breaker off.
  • Inspect and test the pedestal.
  • Connect the RV end of the cord first, then the pedestal end.
  • Turn the pedestal breaker on.
  • Let the EMS approve the power, then switch the RV's main breaker on and bring appliances on gradually.

Adapters: stepping down is normal, combining is not. A dogbone adapter changes the shape of a plug; it does not create power. Running a 30-amp RV on a 50-amp pedestal, or a 50-amp RV on a 30-amp pedestal, is routine. In the second case you are limited to 3,600 watts and will probably trip breakers if you run several heavy appliances at once, because the pedestal's 30-amp breaker is now the smallest thing protecting you.

A cheater box is the one that is not safe. A cheater box or Y-adapter combines two smaller outlets to feed a 50-amp inlet, and it is not approved for RV use. The 20-amp outlet on a pedestal has to carry GFCI protection, so the cheater trips that breaker and leaves you on the 30-amp side only. The worse case is miswiring: if one of the 30-amp receptacles is wired with reverse hot and neutral, plugging in a Y-adapter makes a hot-to-neutral short, and the arc flash can melt the contacts and injure your eyes. The 30-plus-20 version is not a workaround.

Do not chain a step down and back up. Going from a 50-amp outlet down to a 30-amp extension cord and back up to a 50-amp inlet removes the protection: nothing stops you drawing more than 30 amps, and the pedestal will supply it. A 15-amp cord in that chain is worse, because 30 amps through it reaches the boiling point of water in about five minutes. Match the adapter to the difference in plug shape, and size the protection device to the RV's own shore cord, not to the pedestal.

Surge protector or EMS: these are not the same device. A basic surge protector absorbs power spikes and does nothing else. It does not check wiring faults, voltage or ground. An EMS monitors voltage continuously, protects against surges and spikes, and detects open ground, open neutral and reverse polarity. When it finds a problem it disconnects the RV before damage happens. The thresholds most units publish are a cut-out below 102 volts or above 132 volts, with power restored automatically once the voltage settles.
The fault most likely to cost you money is not lightning. Sustained low voltage is. It makes motors draw more current, and it destroys air conditioner compressors, so a device that disconnects on low voltage is worth more than a plain surge protector. One device, sized to the RV, is enough: a 50-amp RV takes a 50-amp protector and a 30-amp RV takes a 30-amp one, with dogbone adapters to cover whatever the pedestal offers.

The hazards behind the device. On a 50-amp supply a broken neutral is the worst case, because the two 120-volt legs stop sharing the load evenly. One leg can see 80 volts while the other sees 160, and anything connected to the high leg can be destroyed almost instantly. Reverse polarity is a wiring fault and a fail, and it is worth reporting rather than living with, because a pedestal wired backwards was never tested after it was installed, and there is no telling what else is wrong with it.

Can injure youOne hazard no consumer device detects. A reverse polarity bootleg ground is not discoverable by a standard meter test, a three-light outlet tester, or the most expensive EMS units on the market. Whether current protection units have closed that gap is not established.
Where the numbers get soft: the 80 percent rule. The usual guidance is to keep a continuous draw under 80 percent of the breaker, which on a 30-amp circuit means about 24 amps. That is the electrical code's own continuous-load rule, which limits a continuous draw to 80 percent of the breaker, applied here to a 30-amp service.
How the RV water system works

Water enters an RV in two different ways, and they do different jobs. A freshwater connection adds potable, safe to drink, water to fill the RV's freshwater tank. Most freshwater connections are gravity fill, meaning there is an opening in the side of the RV for filling the tank with fresh water. The other way is the city-water connection, which pressurizes the RV's plumbing directly from a tap. In many and even most RVs, the fresh-water and city-water connections are separate, so there are two connections. Some RVs use a single inlet and a diverter valve, so a new owner has to find out which arrangement the RV has before the first hookup.

  • Tank and pump. Off the tank, a demand pump pressurises the system. When you are not hooked up to city water, the 12-volt fresh-water pump pressurizes the system from the onboard tank, typically outputting 1 to 4 gallons per minute at 35 to 55 PSI.
  • City water. On a city-water connection the park's pressure does the work, and the pump should be off. On a combined system, city-water mode supplies water from the city-water connection to your fixtures, bypassing the freshwater tank, so the pump is not needed; tank mode lets you pump water from the freshwater tank to your fixtures.

The pressure regulator is not optional. When connected to any pressurized water source, such as a city-water connection at a campground, using a water pressure regulator is essential. RV manufacturers generally recommend that the pressure supplied to an RV not exceed 60 psi, with a comfortable recommended pressure of around 40 to 50 psi, and the regulator itself is usually designed to hold pressure between 40 and 50 psi. Always use a pressure regulator when connecting to city water, especially when traveling or camping, because the pressure at some campgrounds can be significantly higher than recommended. Standard RV plumbing is designed to operate between 40 and 60 PSI, so a regulator is an absolute must-carry.

  • Fit the regulator at the spigot.
  • Then the drinking-water hose.
  • Then the inline filter, if the RV does not already have one. Many owners wisely add an inline water filter if their RV is not already equipped with a built-in whole-house filter.
  • Then the city-water inlet on the RV.
May cause damageDo not run the pump on city water. The two systems are meant to be used one at a time, and most maker manuals say the same thing: connected to city water, the pump stays off. It is not needed, because the park's pressure is already doing the work. One manual puts it as "Do not turn the pump on if you are connected to city water." Another manual warns that switching the pump on with the pressure line still connected can damage it. If a check valve leaks, the city water backfills the fresh tank through the pump and can overflow it. Low park pressure is the common reason people want to run both. Do not: fill the fresh tank from the city connection first, then turn the city water off and run on the pump alone. The same warning applies off-grid: running the pump dry will burn it out.
RV propane: what to check and when

Propane runs the absorption fridge, the water heater, the furnace and the range. The system itself is simple: a tank, a regulator, a main valve, and appliances with their own burner valves. The rule that matters most is the one for a leak, and it is worth knowing before the first tank is opened.

  • Put out any flame, and do not operate a switch, a light, an appliance or a phone that could spark.
  • Get everyone out of the RV immediately.
  • Shut the gas off at the tank only if you can do it safely.
  • Leave the area and call for help.

The reason to smell low is a property of the gas: propane vapor is heavier than air and may collect initially at floor levels, so a check of the RV means checking the low spots first. When the supply goes back on, open the main valve slowly, which lets the tank's excess-flow device settle rather than trip. While the RV is moving, turn the propane supply off at the tank.

A propane flame should burn blue. A yellow flame means the burner is not getting the air it needs, and that is a service item rather than something to live with.
RV appliances: fridge, water heater, furnace and air conditioning

The absorption fridge, and why level matters. Most RV fridges of this kind cool by a gravity-fed process with no compressor, which is why the RV has to be level for one to work well. Because the absorption system uses no mechanical pumps or compressors to circulate the refrigerant, proper leveling is required to keep the refrigerant flowing in the gravity-feed system. Without proper leveling, refrigerant collects and stagnates in the cooling coils and the cooling process stops. The practical requirement is that the vehicle be leveled so it is comfortable to live in, with no noticeable sloping of the floor or walls, and any time the vehicle is parked for several hours with the refrigerator running it should be leveled to allow proper cooling. The fridge can run on two energy sources: an automatic energy selector control system chooses between 120 volts and propane. At altitude, use the electric source: always operate the refrigerator on electric power at altitudes higher than 5,500 feet. A compressor fridge is different: it does not care about level. Why the fridge cares about level, and how much it matters, is its own guide: RV fridge leveling.

The tilt limit is published, and it is a number. Norcold's owner's manuals give it directly: the refrigerator is made to operate within "3° off level side-to-side and 6° off level front-to-back", and operating it beyond those limits "can cause damage to the cooling system and create a risk of personal injury or property damage." Other makers describe the same requirement without the numbers, as level enough to live in with no noticeable slope of the floor or walls. The limit applies while the RV is parked, not while it is being driven.

The water heater: fill it before you switch it on. This is the most expensive mistake in the appliance section, because the electric element burns out in seconds when it is dry. The tank must be filled with water before the water heater is operated: operating it without water can damage the tank or the controls, and that damage is not covered by the limited warranty. To fill and confirm it, fill the tank with water, open both the hot and cold faucets to expel air from the tank, and when water flows from the faucets, close both and check every connection for leaks. The electric side is the fragile one, because elements can burn out quickly when they are switched on with an empty tank. On gas there is no pilot light to light by hand: the appliance has an ignition device that lights the burner automatically, so do not try to light it by hand. On a first start after the gas lines have been open, the water heater may need several ignition cycles before all the air is purged.

May cause damageCheck the brand before you buy or fit an anode rod. Suburban steel tanks use one, and it wants replacing about yearly. Atwood and Dometic aluminum tanks do not use one at all, and fitting one can damage the tank. In Dometic's own words: "Dometic & Atwood Brand Water Heaters DO NOT use Anode Rods!" This brand split is the most useful single thing to know about RV water heaters.

The furnace. A furnace is a good example of an appliance that uses two systems at once: the heater runs on propane, but the fan that blows hot air through the RV runs on the RV batteries, so the furnace uses both the propane and battery systems. To start it, open the LP gas tank valve by turning it fully counter-clockwise, then set the thermostat. The furnace fan should start to blow immediately after the thermostat is set, and the burner lights about 30 seconds later. A furnace with a low battery will often not light at all, because the blower has to spin before the burner will.

Air conditioning. Air conditioning is a 120-volt load, so it needs shore power, a generator or a large inverter. Its one peculiarity is the restart delay: trying to restart the air conditioner compressor before the pressure equalizes is known as short-cycling, and some but not all RV air conditioner thermostats have built-in time delays to prevent it. A generator has to be sized for the surge of a compressor starting, not just its running draw.

RV slide-outs: when to extend them and what breaks

A slide-out runs on 12-volt motors, so it wants a healthy battery or another source of 12-volt power while it moves. On a motorhome that usually means the engine running: the ignition key must be in the on or run position to operate the slideout rooms, and the park brake must be applied for the rooms to run. Keep the engine running while extending or retracting the slideout rooms so the engine alternator can provide maximum power for proper operation of the slideout mechanisms. Ample voltage can also come from attaching the RV to shore power or turning on the generator. On a trailer there is no engine, so the slide runs off the house battery, or off the tow vehicle through the 7-way cord if the battery is low.

Extend and retract the room all the way, never part way, because a slide-out must be fully open or closed to maintain its seal and keep the interior waterproof. Keep people and objects clear of the inside and outside of the slideout room when operating: "Failure to comply could result in death or serious injury." And never drive with a room extended.

Which slide system you have decides what breaks and what you must not do. They are not interchangeable, and the advice for one is wrong for another. The panel inside a compartment, or the maker's manual for the RV, will name yours.

Lippert's hydraulic slide-out manual says the leveling or stabilizing system should be actuated first, "to ensure unit will not move during operation of slide-out system." Other makers have reportedly reversed their own published order between model years. There is no universal rule, so the only defensible instruction is to follow the manual for your own make and model.

Two smaller habits are worth knowing. A Schwintek-style slide wants the switch held after the room stops: hold the switch for 5 seconds once the room is fully open or closed so the control board can complete its calculations, or the room will go out of synchronization over time. And the outside corner of a slide sits at head height on many RVs, which is why owners fit a slit foam noodle over it: the corners can be sharp and pointy, and a lack of attention could put someone into one. Remove the noodle before you close the slides.

How to level an RV, and why stabilisers are not jacks

Jacks lift. Stabilisers stop the rocking. A levelling jack, or the tongue jack on a trailer, carries the weight of the RV and changes its height. A stabiliser, which a lot of people call a stabiliser jack, does neither: it comes down to touch the ground and stops the RV moving when you walk around inside it. That is the whole difference, and it is worth holding on to, because both get called jacks and the mistake is expensive. Level with a jack. Steady with a stabiliser. Never level with a stabiliser, because you would be hanging the RV's weight on legs that were never built to take it.

  • Position the trailer, then level side to side with blocks or ramps under the wheels.
  • Level front to back with the tongue jack or the landing gear: place the trailer in the designated position, use leveling blocks or ramps to reach the desired level before stabilizing, then deploy the stabilizers or scissor jacks once the RV is leveled.
  • Chock the wheels.
  • Lower the stabilisers last, to contact only. If the RV begins lifting when the jack is applied, too much pressure is being used.

A stabiliser that lifts the RV is doing a job it was not made for. If the stabilizer manual says contact only, stop when the foot is planted and the slack is removed. Chocks are separate again and are used whatever the ground is like: install the wheel chocks first and the wheel stabilizers second. And a leveler is never a parking brake, because levelers are not designed for any type of vehicle braking.

The reasons to level: the absorption fridge is the best-supported one, and comfort is the other. The claims that a tilted RV drains its tanks badly or twists its frame are widely repeated but are not established.
RV internet, TV and power: what actually works

The television is the easy part. Most RV televisions are a 120-volt load, so they work on shore power or a generator, and over-the-air reception comes from an amplified antenna on the roof. The harder part is connectivity, and this is the area with the most marketing and the least honesty, so the useful material is the material that argues against buying something.

A cellular booster does one thing: it amplifies a signal that already exists. It cannot create one. Boosters are often promoted online as a game changer that every RV and boat should have onboard, but in many situations they might not make any difference to your internet performance, and they can sometimes make data slower. The technical reason is that a booster uses a single exterior antenna: it cannot take advantage of MIMO, because it can only work with a single signal at a time. It is the equivalent of plugging one of your ears: you can still hear, but not as well. Testing has found that MIMO antennas are the optimal signal-enhancing choice for data more than 80 to 90 percent of the time.

  • A booster helps with distance to the tower, with obstacles, and with a metal-bodied RV. It does not help when there is no signal, when the tower is congested, or when you are being throttled.
  • It boosts cellular frequencies only. It cannot help with getting a better Wi-Fi signal.
  • The bands a legal consumer booster may use are limited: the bands legally approved for mobile consumer boosters include Bands 2, 4, 5, 12, 13, and 25, but many cellular carriers use and depend on several bands that are not on this list. Some boosters sold online boost bands that are not permitted, and using them can result in a knock on your door by the FCC.
  • Placement does as much work as the device: exterior pickup, interior router location, and cable runs can make or break real-world Wi-Fi performance.
The three tools people call a booster are not the same tool. RV WiFi booster is a broad, often imprecise term: depending on the product, it may mean a cellular signal booster, an RV WiFi extender, or a router that creates WiFi inside the RV from a cellular connection. These are different tools, and choosing the wrong one will not solve the connectivity problem you actually have. Start by deciding whether the problem is weak cellular or weak campground Wi-Fi, because the two need different gear. And distrust the headline numbers: marketing materials often cite up to 50 dB gain, or coverage of 5,000 square feet, or working with all carriers, but these are laboratory or ideal-case numbers.

Part 3: Water, waste and tanks

How the fresh, grey and black tanks work, how to read the panel, how to dump without clogging, where a sewer smell really comes from, and how to keep clean water and dry floors.

RV holding tanks: fresh, grey and black explained

Every RV has three tanks. One holds the clean water you use. Two hold the water you have used. The two waste tanks are kept separate on purpose, and confusing which is which causes most of the trouble that follows.

TankWhat it holdsWhat it is for
FreshClean potable waterSinks, shower, toilet flush, drinking
GreyDrain water from the sinks and the shower: soap, food scraps, grease, hairHolding used wash water until you dump
BlackToilet waste: urine, faeces, toilet paperHolding sewage until you dump

The two waste systems are separate. The grey-water system takes wastewater from the sinks and the shower. The black-water system takes sewage from the toilet. Each tank has its own control valve, and both drain through a common sewer hose.

The fresh tank is separate from both. It feeds the freshwater system including the water heater, faucets, and toilet when the RV runs on city water. The toilet is a fixture that uses fresh water. It is not an inlet to either waste tank; it drops its contents into the black tank.

The distinction matters for two reasons. First, only black waste carries the clogging, odour, and septic hazards, so only the black tank needs the careful habits in the sections below. Second, most parks let you leave the grey valve open while you are parked, but almost none let you leave the black valve open.

You can often leave the grey tank valve open while hooked to a sewer connection, but some parks require both valves closed at all times. Check the rule where you are parked.
RV tank sensors reading wrong, and what to do

The wall panel is not a gauge. It is a set of lights that step up in thirds. The vertical columns for fresh, grey, and black water show fluid levels in roughly one-third increments. So the panel reads empty, one-third, two-thirds, or full. Treat it as a rough warning light, not a measurement.

Most panels use conductive probes set through the tank wall. A probe lights when liquid bridges the gap between that probe and a ground sensor lower down. The problem is that anything conductive coating the probe does the same job. Waste film, toilet paper, soap scum, and mineral scale all bridge the circuit, so a tank that is empty can read as full and a tank that is full can read as empty.

Tank sensors mainly fail because of a buildup of waste inside the tank. Failed electronics, broken or unattached connector wires, or failed reading boards can also cause misreads, but it is almost always thick, sticky waste built up in the tank. In black tanks that is human waste and dissolved or broken-down toilet paper. In grey tanks it is moulds and mildews growing in the hot, moist environment.

There is a simple physical check on a direct-drop toilet, which is one whose bowl empties straight into the black tank. Holding tank gauges are seldom accurate, so use a flashlight and look down from above the stool to judge how full the tank is.

That trick does not work on a macerator or vacuum toilet. Those designs do not open a straight path into the tank, so there is nothing to look at. On those, you rely on the panel and on your own sense of how long the tank lasts.

Low water is often called the number one contributor to sensor fouling. Water level and waste buildup drive the readings more than the electronics do.
How much water to put in your RV black tank

A black tank is meant to hold a mix of solids and liquid, not solids alone. Water is what lets the tank empty. When the tank is mostly liquid, the rush of draining water swirls the solids out through the valve and the hose. When the tank is dry, solids sit on the plastic, harden, and stay.

The rule that keeps water in the tank is to leave the black valve closed except when you are dumping. Keeping the gate valve closed at all times while you use the RV leaves enough liquid in the tank for a smooth flow through the gate and the drain valves when you dump. The liquid causes a swirling action that takes accumulated solids with it. Keeping the valve closed even when hooked to a sewer connection stops solids from building up in the tank.

You also start the tank wet, before first use. Fill until there is at least one inch of solution directly under the toilet. The solution is water mixed with an odour-controlling chemical.

You dump when the tank is well filled, so the volume of liquid does the flushing. Drain the tanks only when they are at least two thirds full, which gives enough water to flush waste materials completely out of the drain lines and hose. If a tank is less than three-quarters full, add enough water to get a full flush.

The safe minimum is two thirds of a tank: leave the black tank valve closed and dump when it is at least two thirds full. Dumping with less than half a tank seldom gives enough volume to flush solids, and can leave a persistent paper and faecal build-up.

So the practical window is from two thirds of a tank upwards, plus a wet start. The exact number is a habit, not a specification.

There is no standard gallon figure for the black-tank pre-charge. Published figures are depths or descriptions: at least one inch of solution under the toilet, or enough water to cover the bottom of the tank so waste residue cannot harden. Wait until the tank is at least two thirds full before dumping it. Keep an inch of water in the bowl to stop tank odour entering the living space.

The widely repeated figure of five gallons is a starting rule, not a specification. A common method is to run the flush until it clears, close the black tank dump valve, then add at least five gallons of fresh water back into the tank. Some treatments are marketed at three to five gallons, and owners report anything from about five to ten gallons. The right number depends on the size of the tank.
The RV poop pyramid: how it forms and how to avoid it

The pyramid is a cone of solids and paper that builds up under the toilet drop and hardens. It forms because liquid leaves the tank while solids stay. Each flush adds a little more solid on top, and the pile grows.

The usual cause is leaving the black valve open at a full-hookup site. With the valve open, liquid drains away continuously. The solids have nothing to float in, so they settle and dry. Keep the gate valve shut and the swirling liquid carries the solids out.

Keep the valve closed and dump when the tank is at least half full, because a low-volume dump will not flush solids.

A leaky gate valve causes the same result more slowly: waste water leaks out and leaves solids behind to build up and solidify on the floor of the tank, which can block the tank outlet.

The single habit that prevents a pyramid is keeping the black valve shut. A tank that always holds liquid cannot stack solids, because waste that lands in water spreads out instead of stacking on dry plastic.
RV black tank chemicals: what to use and what never goes in

A bottle sold for black tanks is usually one of three things: a deodoriser that masks or suppresses smell; an enzyme or bacterial treatment meant to break down waste and paper; or a formaldehyde-based steriliser, often called blue chemical, that kills the bacteria causing odour.

The best-documented harm is to the park's system, not your own tank. Avoid any chemical toilet product that lists formaldehyde as an ingredient in any concentration. Formaldehyde kills the bacteria that break down waste in septic tanks, so holding-tank waste containing it can kill the bacteria in a septic tank and eventually clog the system.

Some states go further: tanks containing formaldehyde or similar chemicals must not be disposed of into an on-site sewage disposal system. Formaldehyde waste can be refused at a park dump.

Bleach is the other common harm, and it damages your own equipment. Never use bleach to treat or sweeten a tank: it can severely and quickly damage valves, seals, and gaskets. Use only enzyme-based or bacterial-based products, and note that the term biodegradable does not necessarily mean a product is safe for humans or the environment. Do not use chlorine or caustic chemicals such as bleach or drain openers in the toilet, because they damage the seals in the toilet and the dump valves.

Against all of this, a treatment is optional. Water does the heavy lifting. A good product may help with odours and waste breakdown, but plenty of RVers manage without one. Treat odours as a clue: a tank additive cannot repair a dry toilet seal, blocked vent, leak, clog, or dirty tank.

The rule on what goes in is narrow. Never put anything in the holding tanks other than normal drain water, body waste, and biodegradable products. Paper wrappers, gum, and cigarettes, no matter how small, should never go into either the grey or black tank, because they can damage the plumbing or the tanks.

The item most often named as a clog cause is the disposable wipe sold as flushable. Any paper that does not break down does not belong in the tank. Bleach, chlorine, and drain openers are also excluded, because they damage seals and dump valves, and formaldehyde additives should be avoided.

Whether enzyme and bacterial treatments help or hurt is disputed. Some recommend them; others argue digesters turn the tank into a septic tank and create the sludge that fouls sensors. The question is not settled. What is settled is that water, a closed valve, and correct dumping matter more than any bottle.

Whether coffee grounds, cooking grease, or septic-treatment packets are formally banned by manufacturers is not established. The rule language is general, covering only normal drain water, body waste, and biodegradable products.
How to dump your RV tanks in the right order

Dumping is a fixed sequence. Do it the same way every time and the hose stays clean and the valves keep working. The black tank guide covers the whole job, including the chemicals and the flush.

  • 1. Connect and lock the sewer hose at both ends before you touch any valve.
  • 2. Confirm both gate valves are closed. Crack the cap only when you are ready to attach the hose.
  • 3. Optional leak check before you start: crack the grey valve for a few seconds and close it, so a bad fitting shows up as grey water. The dump itself is always black first, then grey.
  • 4. Open the black valve fully and let it drain.
  • 5. If the RV has a black-tank flush, run it with the black valve open until the clear fitting runs clear.
  • 6. Close the black valve.
  • 7. Open the grey valve and let it run. This scours the hose.
  • 8. Close the grey valve, disconnect, cap, and rinse the hose, and rinse it well away from any fresh-water tap.

The order is black first, then grey, because the grey water is what rinses the black residue out of the shared hose. The last thing through it is the cleaner grey water.

When both tanks are empty, flush them with a freshwater rinse before you close the valves. Do not open a holding-tank gate valve when the protective cap is installed on the pipe. If you open a valve with the cap still on, you dump the tank into the cap and onto yourself.

Be sure the gate valve is open when flushing the tank. The flush water needs somewhere to go.

Why not leave the black valve open. With the valve open at a full-hookup site, liquid drains as it arrives and solids stay behind and dry, which is how a pyramid forms. The grey valve is a different case, and some manufacturers and parks allow it to stay open. Black stays closed.

Dump when the black tank is at least two thirds full. Whenever possible, drain both holding tanks before travelling.

The standard outlet is a three-inch bayonet. A clear elbow at the hose end lets you see when flow stops and when rinse water runs clear.

Can injure youThe sewer hose never goes near a fresh-water source. Rinse it, cap it and store it well away from any drinking-water tap, potable hose or hydrant, and never let it touch the hose you drink from. Cross-contamination is the one mistake in this section that can make you ill rather than simply cost you money.
RV sewer smell inside: why it is usually not the tank

Grey water is not clean and not sterile. It carries soap, food, grease, and hair, and because it is rarely flushed, it can smell as bad as black water, or worse.

When a smell appears inside, look somewhere other than the tank first.

The first suspect is a dry P-trap. Each sink and the shower has a water trap that seals tank gas out of the living space. These traps must have water in them to trap odours. When the vehicle is moving, water can splash out of the sink and shower drains, and when it is stored, the water can evaporate, letting odours into the vehicle. If that happens, run water from the faucet into the drain to refill the traps.

The second suspect is the air admittance valve, a one-way vent on the drain that lets air in so the P-trap is not siphoned dry. When water flows through the P-trap, the valve opens to let air into the pipe and closes when the flow stops. If the valve does not close fully, odour permeates the area around the sink and spreads through the interior. A failed valve is cheap and simple to replace.

  • Smell strongest inside a sink cabinet: suspect the air admittance valve or a loose fitting there.
  • Smell strongest at the drain: suspect a dry or siphoned P-trap. Run water down it.
  • Smell anywhere, and the toilet bowl will not hold water: suspect the toilet seal.
  • Smell with none of the above: suspect a blocked roof vent, which can push odour back through the plumbing.
  • Only after all of those check out: the tank itself is the likely source, and the fix is cleaning and correct dumping, not another bottle of deodoriser.
A smell is confusing because it travels. Gas entering at one fitting can be noticed somewhere else entirely. Work from the cheapest checks upward: refill the traps, then test or replace the admittance valve, then check the toilet seal and the roof vent.
How to sanitise your RV fresh water tank

Filling the fresh tank may have no automatic shut-off. Watch the monitor panel while filling. Excess water vents from an overflow in the vent pipe onto the ground when the tank is full, which prevents the tank from rupturing if it is overfilled. An overfilled fresh tank runs onto the ground through the vent rather than bursting. It will not flood the inside on its own, but you should not walk away from a fill. Some high-end RVs have auto-fill, so check your own manual.

Sanitising means putting a measured dose of chlorine into the fresh system, letting it sit, then flushing. The target is 50 parts per million. Use a solution of one gallon of water and half a cup of chlorine bleach (5% sodium hypochlorite), and add one gallon of solution for every 15 gallons of tank capacity. That mix puts a 50 ppm residual chlorine concentration in the water system. Let the solution stand for at least four hours, then drain and flush. To remove the chlorine taste, use a solution of one quart of vinegar to five gallons of water. Tiffin warns: "Concentrations greater than 50 ppm might damage the water lines and/or the tank."

The same recipe and limit are standard: one gallon of solution per 15 gallons of tank capacity, and no more than 50 ppm.

Recipes conflict on the bleach amount. Half a cup of bleach per 15 gallons and a quarter cup per 15 gallons are both published, which is a two-to-one difference. Household bleach has become stronger over time, from about 5.25% to between 6% and 8.25% sodium hypochlorite, so the same volume now makes a stronger mix. The safe instruction is to compute to about 50 parts per million using the percentage printed on your jug, and not to exceed it.

One more step: remove the RV water filter before disinfecting the fresh-water system, because the filter will remove the chlorine.

Do not borrow the cistern recipe. Emergency cistern disinfection uses a much stronger mix, three cups of unscented bleach per 100 gallons, which is roughly three times the RV dose per gallon. The 50 ppm RV limit exists to protect the lines and the tank; the cistern strength is not for a potable RV system.

A drinking-water hose is for drinking water only. Use a hose manufactured and labelled for potable water service; these are usually white. Do not use a garden hose, which is not made for drinking water and can leave a plastic or rubber taste.

Keep a second hose for the black-tank flush, and never swap them. Do not use the same hose for filling the fresh tank and for the flush, and do not use potable drinking-water hoses for flushing.

City water arrives at whatever pressure the park supplies, and that varies. If city water pressure is more than 60 psi, use a water pressure regulator to prevent damage to the RV. A regulator screws onto the supply and holds the pressure down.

Plumbing systems are usually tested to about 100 to 125 psi, but to avoid warranty problems some manufacturers recommend only 40 to 50 psi, and a safe cap is 60 to 65 psi. Follow your own manual's limit. Park pressure varies for real reasons: wells, booster pumps, elevation, and demand change through the day. Owners report anything from the mid-30s to 60 psi and higher, which is why a regulator is worth carrying.

Fit the regulator at the spigot or pedestal end rather than at the RV inlet, so the hose is protected too.

A whole-RV filter has limits. As installed, the filter removes chlorine, dirt, and other matter, but it is not guaranteed to remove the tastes and odours of iron and sulfur. Change it every 6 to 12 months depending on the quality and quantity of water used. Filters also cost flow: a small in-line filter restricts flow more than a 10-inch standard canister, and a standard canister is more restrictive than a jumbo canister. A filter plus a cheap regulator is a common cause of a weak shower.

How RV water damage happens, and how to avoid it

Almost every flooded RV traces back to one of five situations. Each has a mechanism and a habit that avoids it.

1. City water left on with a leaking fixture and nobody watching. This is the big one. On city water the system is under pressure continuously. Any leak, whether at a toilet valve, a supply fitting, or a cracked line, keeps running until someone closes the spigot. Turn off the city water whenever you are outside the RV, and fifty gallons of water will do as much damage as 500. If you want pressure while away, use the onboard pump and tank instead.

2. A stuck toilet fill valve. The toilet is the highest-consequence leak, because an overflow lands in the living space. A solenoid valve stuck in the open position keeps water flowing into the bowl, and a bowl can overflow on city water if the toilet valve is not seated. After each flush, watch the bowl until the water stops. Install a quarter-turn shut-off valve at the toilet if there is no factory one. Shut off city water when you leave.

3. Fresh-tank fill left unattended. There may be no automatic shut-off when filling. The tank overflows out a vent rather than bursting, but an unattended fill still wastes water and can flood a wet bay. Never walk away from a fill. If your RV has an automatic fill, use a flow meter.

4. Winter freeze damage that becomes a spring flood. A cracked fitting or cracked water-heater tank shows up as a hidden leak the first time you pressurise in spring. The habit: winterise fully, and inspect before you pressurise. Winterising is covered in the next part.

5. Backflow through the pump check valve. A stuck pump check valve can let city water backfill the fresh tank and overflow it. This is usually an overflow rather than an interior flood, but it is common. Cycle the pump with a faucet open, or fit a second inline check valve.

One test catches a slow toilet leak: leave water in the bowl and check it an hour later. If the bowl has drained, the seal or the valve is the problem.

Part 4: Cold, heat and storage

What cold breaks and in what order, how to winterise and where half-jobs fail, what four-season really means, and how to stop condensation and mould.

What freezing does to an RV, and in what order

Water expands as it freezes, and some parts of the plumbing cannot stretch. PEX tubing, the flexible plastic pipe used for most RV water lines, survives a modest amount of expansion. The fittings, valves, faucet housings, the pump, and the water heater cannot. Water heater tanks, or the heat exchanger lines in tankless designs, cannot either. A single freeze can do the damage, and it often is not visible until you pressurise the system in the spring.

Parts exposed to outside air freeze before parts tucked inside the heated shell. Those are the low-point drains, the city water and tank flush inlets, outside showers or kitchens, and the water heater.

The order in which things freeze follows that logic: exposed first, protected last. A common ranking is exterior hookup hoses, then low-point drains, then belly-pan plumbing, then holding tanks, then interior plumbing if heat fails.

The components named again and again as freeze points are PEX lines, plastic fittings and elbows, faucets and shower valves, toilet valves, outside shower assemblies, water pumps, water heaters, filter housings, low-point drains, the city-water check valve, washer and ice-maker lines, and the black-tank flush line.

The water heater is the most expensive failure. Residual water in the tank freezes, expands, and cracks it, and the whole tank then needs replacing.

How to winterize an RV: the two methods, and the half-jobs that still freeze

There are two ways to winterise an RV, and both are in service of the same thing: getting every drop of water out of a system that will freeze and split. Which one suits your RV, how much antifreeze it takes, what pressure a blow-out runs at, and the order to work in are all in the winterising guide, which is the full job.   • Bypassing the water heater without draining it. Bypass keeps antifreeze out, but water left inside still freezes and can crack the tank. Bypassing the water heater without draining it first is a common mistake. Draining the water heater during winterising is a must.
  • Forgetting the exposed fixtures. The outside shower, outdoor kitchen, black-tank flush line, city-water check valve, and washer or ice-maker lines each hold water that can freeze.
What a four-season RV actually means

Four-season is a marketing term. No standards body defines or tests it. The fire and life safety standard for RVs, NFPA 1192, does not define four-season or all-season. Its scope is fire and life safety criteria for recreational vehicles, and its purpose is to provide protection from loss of life from fire and explosion. Its chapters cover administration, referenced publications, definitions, general requirements, fuel systems, fire and life safety, plumbing, and vehicular requirements. There is no climate, insulation, or season chapter.

The industry association RVIA does not define it either. A certification run by the parts maker Truma claims to add credibility to a four-season label, but it is Truma's own single test, using a cold chamber at minus 15 degrees Celsius and measuring how long the RV takes to warm to 20 degrees, and it is not a general industry standard. ANSI defines no four-season standard either; its RV-related standards cover park model RVs, low-voltage systems, and exterior ladders.

Manufacturers rarely test RVs in freezing conditions, and a four-season sticker on an RV may not mean much. There is no RV industry standard that sets apart extreme-weather RVs from competitors; how a manufacturer defines four-season is entirely up to the company.

Manufacturers use their own names for their own feature bundles: terms such as four-season, Arctic Protection, Climate Shield, and Polar Package. Four-season capability is not just about staying warm; these RVs should also handle the extremes of a mid-summer heatwave. Not many manufacturers will confidently stand behind a single R-value for their entire RV. Because there is no test, read features, not labels. A useful tell is whether the maker itself claims four-season or something weaker. One maker, Lance, describes its package as being for the shoulder seasons, which is not a deep-winter claim.

There is no published pass or fail test a buyer can cite. If a seller says an RV is four-season, the question to ask is what the features are: an enclosed and heated underbelly, ducted furnace heat to the tanks, dual-pane windows, and insulation values for the roof, floor, and walls. A label is not a measurement.

An enclosed and heated underbelly is closed in and warmed, usually by furnace ducts or by 12-volt or electric heating elements, to protect the tanks, valves, and plumbing from freezing. Ducted heat routes warm air to the holding tanks and valves, sometimes with tank pads or heat tape added; on one maker's version, ducted heat is routed into individually insulated holding tanks to keep the tanks, valves, and PEX lines warm. Heating pads alone are the weaker substitute. An enclosed, ducted underbelly uses the furnace you are already running. Tank heating pads need shore power and do less in deep cold. Many campers marketed as four-season RVs do not have enclosed underbellies.

Insulation has to be everywhere to work. A year-round RV needs insulation in the roof, floor, walls, front cap, and slide areas, not just one or two highlighted zones. Dual-pane windows help but are not magic in an RV, because RV units are built differently from house units.

Even a well-built RV has limits. RV walls, ceilings, and floors will only ever be so thick, which makes it nearly impossible to rival a residential house, and even the best winter set-up will fail if it is not used and maintained properly and within the manufacturer's stated limits. An RV used in a North Dakota winter needs skirting, a heated water hose, and active heat management; the same RV on the Gulf Coast is barely stressed. The owner who spends the winter in North Dakota must do more to protect the RV than the owner who travels to warmer coastal air.

RV condensation: why it forms and how to stop it

Condensation forms when warm, moist air inside meets a cold surface, such as window glass, a thin wall or a metal frame, and the moisture in the air turns back into liquid on that surface. RVs are small and tightly sealed, so the air inside loads up with moisture quickly. Window glass is colder than the wall around it, which is why the windows are usually the first place it shows.

Where it lands is where the cold is. In a metal-framed RV the frame is itself a path for cold. As Winnebago's service guide puts it, "metal structure within the sidewalls and around openings conducts the cold into the vehicle where it condenses the moisture in the air." That is why the worst places are the corners of exterior walls, the surrounds of openings, and any surface with a metal member behind it. Cold corners behind furniture and inside storage areas, cabinets and closets collect it too, because the air there sits still and slightly cooler. A side effect worth knowing: condensation on an interior wall traces the lines where the frame runs.

Where the water comes from. It is not a leak. It is you, and everything you do inside:

  • Breathing and perspiration. Winnebago's service guide quotes an estimate that breathing and perspiration alone can produce "as much as three pounds of vapor per person per day." Keystone puts the household figure at up to three gallons of water a day for a family of four, once cooking, bathing and washing are counted.
  • Cooking and washing up, which put steam straight into the air.
  • Showers, the largest single source in a normal day.
  • Drying clothes or towels inside, which returns everything a vented dryer would have sent outside.
  • Unvented propane burners, such as a stovetop or a catalytic heater: Airstream's guidance is that "for every gallon of fuel consumed, approximately one gallon of water vapor is evaporated into the air."

What actually works, roughly in order of how much it helps.

  • Ventilate while you cook and shower. Run the range hood when cooking and the bath fan when showering, and keep the bathroom door shut so the steam leaves instead of spreading. Keep the fan running after the shower ends rather than only during it. Fifteen minutes is the minimum, and an hour is better.
  • Crack a roof vent or a window even when it is cold outside. Sealing the RV up in cold weather is the instinct and it is backwards: it is the tightly closed RV that fills with moisture.
  • Move the air into the dead spaces. A small fan left running stops condensation pooling where the air does not move. Corners and the far ends of a slide-out are where air movement stops first, and they are where water collects.
  • Wipe windows and frames dry, and do not let the water run down into the wall.
  • Leave cabinet and closet doors partly open, so the air inside them is the same temperature as the rest of the room.
  • Keep things off the exterior walls. Airstream's guidance is to "allow an air space between stored items and the exterior wall to promote air circulation". A few inches is enough, and it matters most in under-seat storage, wardrobes and the ends of slide-outs. A cushion or a box pressed against a cold wall is a place for water to form behind it.
  • Do not hang wet towels or clothes to dry inside. Damp things go outside, or into a vented dryer.
  • Do not wind the heat down at night to save propane. Airstream's rule is to "avoid nighttime setbacks of 10 degrees or more": cooling the surfaces quickly is exactly what makes the moisture land on them.

The dehumidifier, and how big. When ventilation cannot keep up, typically on a wet coast where the outside air is damp anyway, a dehumidifier is the next step, and bigger is better than smaller. ENERGY STAR's advice is simple: "it's better to oversize than undersize!" Its sizing table starts at 20 to 30 pints per day for a space under 2,000 square feet that is only slightly damp, and runs to 30 to 50 pints per day where the space is genuinely wet, and higher again for larger spaces. An RV is small, so the bottom of that scale is the starting point, and a unit at the top of its band runs less and lasts longer than one that never quite catches up. One caveat matters in winter: coils can frost below about 65°F, so a unit rated for low-temperature operation is the one to look for if it will run in the cold. A cheap hygrometer tells you there is a problem before you can see one; Winnebago puts the target at 60 percent relative humidity or less.

Sealing an RV completely does not stop condensation. It makes it worse, because trapped moisture has nowhere to go. The goal is to move moist air out or to dry it, not to lock it in.
How to store an RV, short term and long term

Mould needs three things: moisture, a food source, and a moderate temperature. Mould can develop in any environment with moisture and temperatures between 40°F and 100°F. The food source is ordinary dirt and dust, which is why a clean, dry RV stores better than a dirty one.

The control lever is humidity. Keep indoor relative humidity below roughly 50 to 60%, and ideally between 30 and 50%.

Short-term storage, meaning weeks between trips, is mostly about drying out. Empty and dry the black and grey tanks, run water into every P-trap, and leave a vent or window cracked for airflow. If the RV sits in sun, expect interior heat to drive moisture out of soft furnishings by day and condense it at night, so ventilation matters more than in a house.

Long-term storage, over a winter, adds the freeze work from earlier in this part: full winterising, water heater drained, traps filled with antifreeze. On top of that:

  • Clean the RV before storage. Crumbs, grease, and dust are the food mould needs.
  • Dry it thoroughly. Wipe wet surfaces and let soft furnishings air out before closing up.
  • Leave cabinet and closet doors partly open for airflow.
  • Keep some ventilation. Do not seal the RV completely, because trapped moisture raises condensation. Completely sealing an RV without ventilation can actually increase condensation inside.
  • Use a dehumidifier or desiccant as appropriate. A powered dehumidifier needs power; desiccant packs suit a sealed, unpowered space and should be refreshed every 30 to 60 days.
  • Fix leaks before storage. A small roof or window leak over a winter is a mould start.

When moisture is bad enough to grow visible mould over a large area, that is a remediation job rather than a wipe-down. Visible mould over about 10 square feet is the usual threshold between a do-it-yourself clean and professional remediation.

RV tires: how to read them and why they fail

RV tires are asked to do more than car tires. They face a greater variety of service conditions than automobiles, light truck and truck applications, often at or near maximum loads and during hot weather. Running near the maximum load means there is very little margin. A small drop in pressure, or a hot day, eats into that margin quickly.

A failure is expensive because the body above the tire is thin. In seconds, a failing tire can damage underbelly materials, wiring, plumbing, propane lines, flooring, insulation, sidewalls, appliances, and even structural components. The tread separates and flails at speed, so the damage lands on the wheel well and everything routed through it. A blowout can cost a few hundred to several thousand dollars to repair.

Load range and load index are the two numbers that describe how much a tire can carry.

  • Load range is a letter, such as Load Range E or G. It identifies the tire's load and inflation limits.
  • Load index is a number, such as 120. It is a numerical code for the maximum load a tire can carry.
  • Some light truck tires carry two load indices. The first applies to a single tire, the second to a tire used in a dual pair. When LT tires are fitted in dual assemblies, the load capacity is reduced by 9% of the single load capacity to account for the effect of road crown.
  • A bigger number is not automatically a safe substitute. The load index should not be used on its own to decide whether a replacement tire can carry the load.

The sidewall also prints the maximum load and the cold pressure that matches it, for example a Load Range G tire rated to carry 4,675 lb at 110 psi in a single position. That printed figure is a ceiling, not a target. The pressure you actually run is set lower and is described below.

The date code. Every tire carries the week and year it was made, in the DOT code on the sidewall. A DOT code ending in 0921 indicates a tire made in the 9th week of 2021. The full code is often printed on only one side of the tire, usually the inner wall, so you may have to look at the inside of each tire to read it.

Check all four, plus the spare. Because the DOT code can be on the inner sidewall, a quick look at the outer walls can miss the oldest tire. The spare ages too, even though it never touches the road.
How old are my RV tires? Reading the DOT date code

Rubber ages whether or not the tread wears. That is why tire makers give an age, not just a tread depth, and why the tread can look new on a tire that should be replaced.

The sources do not agree on the number. Each maker publishes its own figure, and the range is wide.

  • Goodyear says 6 years. "Goodyear recommends that any tire in service (meaning inflated and mounted on a rim of your vehicle, including your spare tire regardless of whether that tire is in contact with the roadway) 6 years or more be replaced even if such tire appears serviceable and even if it has not reached the legal treadwear limit." Goodyear also says a tire's replacement period should not exceed 6 years.
  • Michelin says inspect from 5 years and replace at 10. "Tires that have been in use for 5 years or more should continue to be inspected by a specialist at least annually." and "any tires in service 10 years or more from the date of manufacture, including spare tires, be replaced with new tires as a simple precaution even if such tires appear serviceable and even if they have not reached the legal wear limit."
  • Hankook, in its trailer tire guide, gives 10 years. "It is recommended that tires in service 10 years or more from the date of manufacture must be replaced even if it was never used."
  • NHTSA reports the range, not one number. Winnebago repeats it: "Some vehicle and tire manufacturers recommend replacing tires that are six to ten years old, regardless of treadwear." The range is attributed to the manufacturers, not set as a federal rule.
  • The tire makers' trade body says no single service life exists. The USTMA states: "USTMA is not aware of scientific or technical data that establishes or identifies a specific minimum or maximum service life for passenger and light truck tires."

The popular 5-7-10 rule is not published by RVIA or NHTSA. A common rule of thumb on the internet says to inspect at 5 years, replace at 7 and never go past 10, and attributes it to a consensus of NHTSA, RVIA and the Tire Industry Association. RVIA publishes construction and inspection standards, not a consumer tire age rule, and NHTSA has never issued one. NHTSA's own research explains that it chose not to add an aging requirement to its tire standard: "At this time, the agency does not believe it is necessary for motor vehicle safety to add a tire aging requirement to its light vehicle tire standard…" Treat the 7 year figure in particular as unverified.

What to do with the disagreement. Read the DOT date code on each tire. Replace any tire that reaches your tire maker's own number. Have the tires inspected by a specialist every year from year 5. Do not use tread depth alone as the test, and do not leave the spare out of the count.

Age matters because of what happens inside the tire. An underinflated or overloaded tire builds up more heat that can go beyond the endurance limits of the rubber and radial cords, which can cause sudden tire failure. The visible version, cracking in the sidewall from ozone and sunlight, is a separate age mechanism. Shallow cracking is normal. Cracking deeper than about 2/32 inch means the tire should be replaced. The dangerous part is that internal damage can be invisible: the cords and the rubber can degrade while the outside looks fine.

Tread still matters, and the legal minimum is low. Remove a tire from service when the tread wears down to 2/32 of an inch. Federal regulations also require removing tires on the front axle of vehicles with a GVWR over 10,000 pounds when worn to 4/32 inch tread depth.

Used tires are not a shortcut. Never buy or install a used tire of any age on your RV, because its service, maintenance and storage history is largely unknown. The arithmetic is easy to get wrong by hand, so the tire date decoder takes the four digits and gives you the week and the year.

RV tire pressure: how to use the load and inflation table

The pressure printed on the sidewall is a ceiling, not the pressure you run. RV tires are inflated to match the actual weight the tire carries, and that pressure is read from a load and inflation table. The right pressure depends on how much your fully loaded RV or trailer weighs.

The method is the same at every maker. Determine the required pressure from the actual load on each individual tire. Set the pressure for the tire carrying the heaviest load, and set all tires on the axle to that same pressure. Weigh each axle end separately, then use the weight of the heaviest end to set the pressure for all tires on that axle.

  • Find your exact tire size and its load and inflation table.
  • Weigh the RV fully loaded: fuel, water, propane and cargo, as it will travel.
  • Weigh each end of an axle separately. If you can, weigh each wheel position, the front left corner, front right corner and so on.
  • On each axle, take the heavier end and read across the table to the lowest cold pressure that supports that weight.
  • Set both tires on that axle to the same pressure.
  • Never go above the maximum pressure stamped on the wheel, not just the tire.
  • Check the pressures cold.

Weighing each corner, not just each axle, matters because the weight is rarely even side to side. Weighing one axle at a time can hide an overloaded wheel position even when the G.A.W.R. has not been exceeded. Side-to-side differences on a front axle can reach 1,200 pounds.

The pressure you choose must respect the wheel, not just the tire. The cold inflation pressure must never exceed the maximum inflation rating stamped on the wheel. For control, both tires on an axle should run at the same pressure.

Underinflation is the main failure route. A soft tire flexes more, which builds heat, and heat damages the rubber and the cords from the inside. The damage threshold is low: a tire run at less than 80% of the recommended pressure for the load it is carrying should be inspected for possible damage. A tire that has lost more than 20% of its recommended inflation pressure should be considered flat.

  • Check tire pressure when the tires are cold and have not been driven more than one mile.
  • A hot tire can read as much as 10 to 15 psi higher than the same tire cold.
  • Do not bleed air from hot tires. The rise is normal and the tire is not overinflated.
  • A 10 F rise in outside temperature raises pressure by about 2%, and a 10 F fall lowers it by about the same.
  • Tire pressure increases about 0.48 psi for every 1,000 feet of altitude.
  • Use a good truck tire gauge with a dual angled head.
RV tire pressure monitoring, valve stems and storage

TPMS does not replace the hand check. A tire pressure monitoring system shows the pressures while you drive, but it is not a substitute for measuring them. Inspect tires monthly for proper inflation pressure, tread wear and overall condition, whether or not the vehicle has a monitoring system.

Valve stems are a weak point with screw-on sensors. A sensor screws onto the end of the stem and adds weight. External TPMS sensors are light, but the extra weight causes vibration, and vibration can cause the rubber stem to fail from fatigue. Bolt-in metal stems are the fix, and flow-through sensors are designed for metal stems only. Valve caps should be plastic, not metal, because metal caps can corrode and fuse to the stem.

Other sensor details are worth knowing. A common alert setting is a low warning at 10% below the recommended pressure and a high warning at 20% above it. Sensor batteries commonly last about five years. Whether a sensor is heavy enough to require re-balancing the wheel is disputed.

Balancing and the spare. Balance a tire when new tires are mounted, when a tire and wheel move to another position, after a flat repair, and any time a tire is dismounted. A matching full-size spare should be rotated into service as part of the regular rotation, and the spare is subject to the same age limit as the rest.

Storage. Tires age fastest when the RV sits. If you must store your RV, a cool, dry, sealed garage is your best bet. Some storage surfaces can cause tires to age faster, so place a barrier (cardboard, plastic or plywood) between the tire and the storage surface.

  • Clean the tires with soap and water first, then cover them from sunlight and ultraviolet rays.
  • Store away from high ozone areas. Do not park next to electric generators, transformers or mercury vapor lamps.
  • Put a barrier between the tire and the ground.
  • Goodyear says to inflate to operating pressure plus 25%, but not above the wheel rating, and to move the RV at least every three months to prevent flat spotting. Michelin instead says to inflate to the pressure shown on the sidewall. These two instructions differ.
  • Do not use tire dressings that contain petroleum products, alcohol or silicones. They cause deterioration or cracking and accelerate the aging process.
May cause damageStore on a level surface. If a tire is not evenly supported, the sidewall cables can become fatigued and damaged, which can result in a sidewall rupture and a complete, sudden loss of air pressure.
RV plumbing leaks: the factory fittings that work loose

A new RV is not finished plumbing. Factory water connections can be loose or wrong and start to leak after some miles of towing, once vibration has worked on them.

The mechanism is vibration. Towing subjects the RV to an earthquake-like experience. Even on a smooth highway, water lines vibrate and shake, which can loosen the fittings that connect them. The origin of many of these joints is the factory: workers can rush the plumbing and connect the lines poorly. Pre-delivery inspections should catch this, but that does not always happen.

A specific defect recurs: flexible braided or vinyl hose clamped onto a fitting meant for rigid PEX pipe. Some newer RVs were built with flexible vinyl tubing on fittings made for rigid PEX, which creates slow leaks at junctions. The clamp cannot seal the softer, slightly larger hose as well as it seals pipe, so the joint weeps.

Factory water defects are real. A water line to an ice maker can pull loose, creating a water leak. A water heater that was not winterized correctly can leak from cracked internal parts. A factory test can disturb a toilet seal: after installation, the whole toilet and waste tank system is flood-tested for leaks by filling it with water to the rim. Holding the flush ball open for that test can distort the seal, so a slow drip into the tank can appear later.

  • Pressure hold test. Pressurise the system, switch the pump off at the panel, and see whether pressure holds for 15 minutes. If it holds, the pump's check valve is the suspect rather than the plumbing. A typical 12 volt pump cuts off between 45 and 55 psi.
  • Paper towel tracing. Wrap a single layer of paper towel around the supply and drain lines under the sinks, around the toilet and by the water heater. The wet one points to the leak.
  • Watch shore water pressure. Keep the pressure at the hydrant below about 65 psi, and use a real pressure regulator rather than a cheap restrictor.
  • Check the known spots. Vinyl to PEX junctions in the wet bay, faucet supply connections, water heater lines, the pump and its check valve, and the low point drains.
  • Fix clamps correctly. A standard screw hose clamp does not compress a PEX joint evenly. Use the right clamp for the fitting, such as a stainless ear clamp or a copper crimp ring, and use a braided flex hose between the pump and the pipe to absorb vibration.
Costs you moneyWarranty language often puts the fitting check on the owner. Water leaks related to misuse, mishandling, neglect or abuse are not covered, and tightening fittings counts as routine maintenance. A slow drip found early is a small job. One found after it has travelled through a floor is not.
RV roof sealant and delamination: what to look for

Sealant keeps water out of an RV, and water is the enemy. There are two kinds of lap sealant and they are not interchangeable. Self leveling sealant must not be used on vertical surfaces or as splicing cement. Non-sag sealant is labeled for vertical surfaces. Self leveling sealant flows out flat and is for horizontal surfaces such as the roof. Non-sag sealant stays put and is for anything sloped or vertical, such as sidewalls and termination rails.

Sealant choice also depends on the roof material. On a rubber or TPO roof, do not use silicone: it will not adhere to the membrane. Factory membrane roofs have two layers of sealing. Butyl tape provides the primary seal against water infiltration in these roof assemblies, and the lap sealant is a secondary seal. The tape under the molding is the real seal, and the lap sealant covers and protects it.

How often to inspect is a genuine disagreement.

  • Every 90 days: many manufacturers recommend inspecting your RV's exterior seams and seals.
  • Three to four times a year: during routine cleaning, inspect all caulk and sealants for voids or cracking.
  • Every 6 months, reseal annually: inspect your RV roof seals carefully every six months and reseal annually.
  • OEM: inspect sealants every 6 months and replace annually.

Sealant ages even when the RV does not move, so inspect on a calendar rather than by mileage. UV dose, heat cycles and ozone break down sealant and rubber whether the unit rolls or sits. Look for deep cracks or voids in the lap sealant, loose or missing transition molding, and cracked, damaged or worn areas around roof vents, A/C units, solar panels and other roof-mounted accessories.

Delamination is what happens to a laminated sidewall when water gets in. Most modern RV sidewalls are laminated: a fiberglass or aluminum outer skin glued to foam or a wooden lattice, with a thin inner panel. Delamination is the outer skin separating from what it was glued to, and it shows as bubbles, waves or ripples in the wall, soft spots and sometimes a hollow sound when tapped. It is a failure of the glue, not of the fiberglass. The cause is usually water: it gets in through a failed seam, vent or window seal, softens the glue and the wood, and spreads.

The warning signs are easy to check once you know what to look for.

  • Bubbles, waves, ripples or creases along a sidewall.
  • Sight down the wall in low, raking light: stand at one end of the trailer and look down the wall until it turns into a mirror.
  • Tap or press the wall. A bonded panel rings; an unbonded panel thuds. A skin that flexes and springs back means the glue has failed. A skin that sinks and stays sunk means the wood behind it has rotted.
  • Use a moisture meter if you have one. A sound sidewall reads in the 8 to 12 percent range; anything over 18 percent is actively wet.
  • Check the usual entry points: roof to sidewall seams, window corners, awning rails, marker lights, storage door frames, slide-out openings and the front and rear caps.

Not every RV can delaminate. Units built with corrugated aluminum siding over a framed wall, sometimes called hung wall construction, do not use laminated fiberglass panels. If your RV has that kind of siding, this particular failure mode does not apply. To see what a given depth of snow is actually putting on the roof, the snow load calculator does the arithmetic.

How to clean and care for an RV

Exterior cleaning is mostly about protecting the surface. Wash with a pH neutral or RV and marine grade soap and a soft brush or microfiber mitt. Wash the roof first so the runoff does not streak down the clean sidewalls. A common recommendation is to wash the full exterior about once a month, and the roof every three to four months.

  • Avoid solvents.
  • Do not pressure wash.
  • Avoid dish soaps and other household detergents that can dry out and strip wax.
  • Avoid strong household solvents, undiluted bleach, and heavy rubbing compounds not labeled for gelcoat or fiberglass.
  • Do not use silicone products on a rubber or TPO roof.

Pressure washing is called out because of where the water goes. Excess pressure can force water behind seals and decals. Water forced behind a seal is how a seal starts to fail, which leads to the delamination problem described above.

Wax helps, but it does not last on fiberglass gel coat. Wax may last only one to three months. Decals need their own care: use a UV protectant rather than an abrasive or solvent wax, because solvent and abrasive waxes wear the graphics. A ceramic coating is the longer lasting option.

  • Clean the roof and check the lap sealant for cracks and voids at each cleaning.
  • Do not use petroleum based or silicone products on a rubber or TPO roof.
  • Do not pressure wash an awning: a pressure washer can delaminate vinyl fabric and damage seams. Let it dry fully in the extended position before rolling it up.
  • On acrylic awning fabric, avoid bleach, which breaks down acrylic fibers over time, and avoid petroleum-based solvents.

Black streaks are the dark lines that run down from the roof. A black streak cleaner will work, but these cleaners strip wax from the surface, so re-wax the area where you used the cleaner. The best way to use a black streak cleaner is to spray the product onto a towel and attack the streak directly. If the streaks return after cleaning, the surface is oxidized and needs treatment rather than another wash.

Interior care is covered in a dedicated chapter of your owner's manual, which lists soft goods, cabinetry, vinyl wall paneling, flooring, bathroom and windows. Follow that chapter.

How to drive a motorhome

A motorhome is longer than a car and it steers from the front, so the back end does not follow the front wheels exactly. The two ideas to learn are off-tracking and rear overhang. Off-tracking means the rear wheels follow a different path than the front wheels; the longer the wheelbase and the sharper the wheel cut, the greater the off-tracking. Rear overhang is the part of the RV that sticks out behind the drive axle. There is a full guide on how to drive a motorhome, including the height note and the tail swing.

Off-tracking is what makes a turn feel tighter than it looks. On a long RV the rear axle can cut several feet inside the path of the front wheels on a sharp campsite or driveway turn.

The practical turn advice is to plan ahead: set up further away from the obstruction and start to turn earlier. Use the convex mirrors on the side you are turning toward: turn right, look right; turn left, look left.

Tail swing is the other half of the same geometry. The overhang behind the rear axle swings the opposite way at the start of a turn, so the rear corner can clip something beside you. As a rule of thumb, for every three feet behind your rear axle you have the potential for one foot of tail swing heading in the opposite direction. On some RVs it can be as much as 30 inches. You can measure yours by parking parallel to a straight line, making a full lock turn away from the line, and seeing how far the tail crosses it. A shortcut is to divide the overhang length by three.

Braking. More weight takes longer to stop, and a motorhome carries a lot of it. One thing that surprises new owners: the RV's service brakes are sized for the RV, not for whatever it is towing. The brakes are rated to operate at the gross vehicle weight rating (GVWR), not for the combined weight of RV and towed vehicle, and that figure is not the amount of weight the brakes alone can stop. That is why a towed vehicle needs its own supplemental braking system, which is required above about 3,000 lb in most states.

Drive within a margin. Stopping distance grows with load, and an RV that is at its legal limit on a long downgrade has little reserve. Use the transmission and the exhaust brake or engine braking on long grades rather than riding the service brakes.

  • Start turns later and wider than you would in a car.
  • Watch the side you are turning toward, and remember the tail swings the other way.
  • Give a motorhome braking room, and expect to need more when descending.
  • Use a spotter and mirrors when parking (see the backing section).
  • Do not force a turn when the geometry is tight. Reposition and try again.

Where to go next. The checks to make before a wheel turns are in the pre-trip walkaround, and tires are the other half of driving something this heavy: when to replace RV tires covers age, load and pressure.

How to tow a travel trailer safely

Hitching, in order. The one rule that never changes is that the wheel chocks stay in place until the whole procedure is finished, because the trailer can roll the moment the coupler is not seated on the ball. The towing guide takes the hitching procedure, sway and reversing step by step.

  • Chock the trailer wheels, and leave the chocks in until the end.
  • Open the coupler latch before backing up, not after.
  • Raise the tongue with the tongue jack so the coupler clears the ball, higher than you think you need.
  • Back the tow vehicle until the ball is under the coupler.
  • Lower the coupler onto the ball until it is fully seated, then close the coupler latch and fit the safety pin.
  • Install the weight distribution bars and sway control as the hitch maker directs, then lower the jack.
  • Retract the tongue jack fully, and be sure the stabilizer jacks are retracted.
  • Connect the breakaway cable to the tow vehicle.
  • Cross the safety chains under the coupler and attach them to the tow vehicle.
  • Plug in the trailer connector and test the lights, turn signals, brakes and running lights.
  • Do the tug test with the chocks still in place: with everything connected, pull forward gently, and the trailer must not move.

The trailer must not move until the coupler is secured and locked, the chains are secured and the jacks are retracted. Leave your wheel chocks in place until you have completed the entire hitching procedure.

Safety chains cross under the tongue. Crossing them makes a cradle that catches the tongue if the coupler separates, instead of letting it drop to the pavement. Fasten the chains to the frame of the tow vehicle, not to the hitch or ball. Cross them under the hitch in an X pattern to keep the trailer from hitting the ground in case of a disconnection. Never attach safety chains to the hitch ball or any removable part of the hitch.

The breakaway cable is a separate connection. Attach the breakaway cable as its own connection, not hooked to the safety chains. The breakaway switch pulls a pin and applies the trailer's own battery powered brakes if the trailer separates, so it only works if the cable is pulled, which it will not be if it is tied to the chains. The switch only works if the cable is attached to the towing vehicle's frame, not the safety chains; if the cable were attached to the chain, pulling the chain would not pull the cable. The switch also needs a charged trailer battery. To test it, pull the pin out of the switch by hand, then try to pull the trailer. If you feel a significant drag force, the brakes are activated. Re-insert the pin afterward.

Unhitching is the reverse. Level the trailer, chock the wheels, reduce any sway bar tension, disconnect the chains and cables, raise the tongue jack to lift the coupler off the ball, remove the pin and slide the latch open, double check, then pull the tow vehicle forward. On a new trailer, check coupler tightness after towing 50 miles, and check that the lug nuts are tightened to the proper torque after the first 10, 25 and 50 miles of driving and before each tow thereafter.

Tongue weight. Tongue weight is how much of the trailer's weight rests on the hitch. It controls stability, so it matters more than almost anything else about loading. Ford gives the targets: "For trailers up to 2,000 lbs., tongue load not to exceed 200 lbs. For conventional trailers over 2,000 lbs., tongue load is 10% of loaded trailer weight. For 5th-wheel trailers, king pin weight is 15% of loaded trailer weight." Lance gives a range instead of one figure: "The hitch weight of a loaded trailer (including cargo) should be between 10-15% of the total weight of the trailer on a bumper pull trailer. The hitch weight of a loaded trailer (including cargo) should be between 20-25% of the total weight of the trailer on a 5th wheel trailer." Note that the two 5th-wheel figures differ, 15% against 20 to 25%.

Too little or too much tongue weight both cause trouble. Too much tongue load or king pin weight can cause suspension or drivetrain damage and press the vehicle down in back, lifting the front wheels to the point where traction, steering response and braking can be severely decreased. Too little tongue load or king pin weight can reduce rear-wheel traction and cause instability, which may result in tail wagging or jackknifing.

Weight distribution. A weight distribution hitch uses spring bars to spread the tongue weight across the tow vehicle's axles so the pair sits level. It does not raise any rating. A weight-distribution hitch does not increase the truck's GVWR, rear gross axle weight rating, tire capacity, receiver rating, trailer GVWR, or legal towing capacity. It also cannot make a tail-heavy or overloaded trailer safe. Ratings are fixed at the factory, and adding parts does not change them: adding components such as air bags or overload springs does not allow these ratings to be increased or exceeded.

Loading. Where the cargo sits sets the tongue weight. Two common rules are to load 60% of your cargo weight in front of the axle and 40% behind, and to put the heaviest items in first, centered and low. The hitch maker Equal-i-zer publishes a different split: "About 85 percent of the trailer's total weight should be positioned forward of the axle or axles, with no more than 15 percent behind", and says it should not be confused with the 60/40 rule. Whatever the split, the outcome is the same: 10 to 15% tongue weight for a bumper pull, measured, not guessed. Heavy items should be placed low and as close to the axle positions as reasonable.

Sway. Sway is the trailer swinging side to side behind the tow vehicle. Sway can be caused by excessive steering, wind gusts, roadway edges, or by the trailer's reaction to the pressure wave created by passing trucks and buses. The root cause is usually loading: too little tongue weight makes the trailer unstable. Sway control is not a cure-all, and sway begins with weight placement, not hardware.

If sway starts, the response is specific. Take your foot off the accelerator and steer as little as possible to stay on the road, using small trim-like steering adjustments. Do not try to steer out of the sway; that only makes it worse. Do not apply the tow vehicle brakes to correct the sway. Applying the trailer brakes alone tends to straighten out the combination, especially going downhill. So the correction is the manual trailer brake lever, not the brake pedal and not the accelerator. Do not speed up to try to pull out of it, and avoid sudden lane changes.

The trailer brake controller is the tool for this. Most conventional and fifth-wheel trailers have electric brakes, activated by a controller in the tow vehicle. The controller automatically coordinates the tow vehicle and trailer braking so the two systems work together when the brake pedal is applied, and it can help stabilize a trailer that sways because of bad road conditions. Manually applying the trailer brakes with the hand lever on the controller will stabilize a trailer that is likely to sway. Two related rules: the sway control bar, if fitted, must be disengaged before backing up, because leaving it engaged while reversing forces the system to fight the turn, which can lead to binding, premature wear, or outright damage. And the hitch ratings must exceed the trailer: the ball, the spring bars and the sway control all have to be rated above the fully loaded trailer weight. For your own combination, the weight calculator works payload and pin weight out of the figures on your stickers.

Where to go next. Three numbers decide whether a tow is legal and safe before any of the rest matters: towing capacity and payload, pin weight for a fifth wheel, and when trailer brakes are required. The mechanics of the hitch and of reversing are in the towing guide, and the most common roadside failure of all is covered in repacking trailer bearings.

Fifth wheel towing: what is different

A fifth wheel carries part of its weight over the tow vehicle's rear axle, on a king pin that sits in a hitch in the bed. That placement is why a fifth wheel is steadier than a bumper pull: the pivot point is over the axle rather than behind it, so the trailer tracks behind the truck more predictably and sways less. The trade off is height, which raises the center of gravity and brings the trailer closer to the cab, so clearance has to be checked.

The lube plate. A fifth wheel hitch can be greased, or a plastic lube plate can be used instead to avoid the mess. The plastic lube plate must not exceed 3/16 of an inch in thickness to ensure the hitch operates properly, and must be 12 inches in diameter or larger to properly distribute king pin weight. Without a lube plate, grease the skid plate and pivot pin, and add grease to the jaw fitting every 6 months.

Hitch height and clearance. The hitch and pin box are set so the trailer rides about level. Allow approximately 6 inches of clearance between the top of the pickup walls and the underside of the front of the trailer, for pitch and roll of the trailer. For hitch placement, the distance from the back of the truck cab to the center of the rear axle should be approximately 4 inches greater than one-half the trailer width.

Cab strike when turning. A fifth wheel can swing into the back of the truck cab on a sharp turn, which is why clearance is checked before driving. The distance between the cab and trailer should be greater than two inches at all points of the turn. If you make a turn greater than 90 degrees, the trailer will contact your truck and damage various parts of the hitch. The fix on short-bed trucks is a sliding hitch, which lets the pivot move back during a turn. A common mistake to avoid is called high hooking: backing your hitch into a trailer that is set too high, so the lower flange of the king pin wedges against the metal edge of the lower horseshoe piece or against the Lock Jaw Assembly. Set the trailer low enough that the pin enters the jaws cleanly.

Where to go next. What limits a fifth wheel is the pin weight rather than the trailer's total, and it is the number most often got wrong: pin weight and payload. Total capacity is in towing capacity, and hitching and reversing follow the same rules as any trailer in the towing guide.

How to back up a trailer

Backing a trailer or a motorhome is where most damage happens, and it is a skill learned with practice rather than reading. The working method has three parts: properly adjusted side mirrors, a camera system if the RV has one, and a spotter on the ground at the rear corner who is working to agreed signals.

The mirrors are the main tool. Set them so you can see down both sides of the RV and the trailer or the rear corners. When you turn, look to the side you are turning toward. A camera helps but does not replace the mirrors: a single view can still hide the extreme rear corners when the RV is angled.

Use a spotter, and agree on signals first. Always use a spotter. Before you move, decide what each signal means, and decide on a stop signal that means stop now. If you cannot see the spotter in a mirror, stop. When in doubt while backing up, get out and look. Getting out and looking is always faster than repairing a dent. There is no standard hand signal set for a recreational spotter, so agree on your own before you start.

Two geometry points help. A trailer pivots opposite to the tow vehicle's steering: turn the truck left and the trailer's rear goes right. Make small corrections and let the trailer catch up rather than chasing it with big steering inputs. If you have a helper, walk the site first and identify the inside rear swing point before you position the RV.

Where to go next. The towing guide treats hitching, sway and reversing as one job, and the pre-trip walkaround is the checklist for the moment before you pull out.

Pop-up campers and hybrids: setup and care

A pop-up camper is a box with a lifting roof and folding beds. Setup runs in a fixed order, and the order protects the mechanism: level first, then lift, then stabilize. Leveling happens before anything is raised, because a tilted camper loads the lift arms unevenly. Stabilizers go down after the roof is up, and they only steady the camper, they do not lift it.

The roof is raised with a crank or a power lift. Never raise or lower the roof if there is anything on top of it: the system was not designed to raise or hold any additional weight. When cranking by hand, make sure the crank is pushed forward into the spur gear. If the crank gears become free, the roof will fall. Never raise or lower the roof with the stabilizers down, and never go inside before the stabilizers are down. Model manuals vary, so check yours.

Canvas care. The cardinal rule is that a pop-up must never be closed wet. Make sure the canvas is dry before packing down: a wet or damp canvas will develop mildew and mould very quickly and damage the canvas permanently. If you have to close up in the rain, open it again and dry it out as soon as you can.

Cleaning the canvas is where the advice conflicts, so follow the canvas maker's own manual. One tent maker forbids all cleaners: the tent can be cleaned only with fresh water and a damp cloth, and detergents or chemical cleaners must never be used. Other sources forbid bleach specifically: household bleach degrades canvas fibers and causes permanent discoloration. A dish soap, bleach and water mix is also recommended in some places, which directly contradicts the canvas makers, so follow the manual for your specific canvas. New canvas can seep at the seams the first few times it is wetted; some makers say this is normal and will stop as the threads swell. Drying can take three to five hours in strong sun and six to eight hours on a cloudy day.

The lift system. The roof rides on telescoping posts and cables routed over pulleys. Clean and recoat the lifter posts externally with a rust prohibitive, non-drying lubricant. Annually grease the zerks on the front, bottom and top of the master tube. Lubricate the springs annually by raising the roof and oiling each segment of the post, and immediately remove any excess oil on the external portion of the post to avoid tent damage. One supplier recommends a light silicone spray rather than oil on the posts, because oil attracts dirt.

The system's weak point is the pulleys and cables. The pulley separator package is the first routing point of all four individual lift arm main cables, and it is the most crucial pivot point, carrying the most strain when the top assembly is raised. A typical system has 24 steel bearing pulleys. A binding roof when raising or lowering usually points to a camper that is not level, worn bushings, seized or broken pulleys, a bent lift arm or twisted cables. After any lift system part is replaced, raise and lower the unit 4 or 5 times to make sure it is operating properly.

Tank capacity is the honest gap. Pop-ups are built lightly enough that some are exempt from trailer brake rules by weight, and small towables typically carry a cassette toilet or a porta-potty instead of a true black tank. No manufacturer owner's manual for a soft-sided tent trailer states its fresh, grey and black tank sizes, so check that model's own manual. The practical effect is that pop-ups lean on campground bathhouses, dump stations and water spigots.

Where to go next. A pop-up is towed and stored like any other trailer, so the towing guide and when trailer brakes are required both apply. Its short season makes winterising and battery storage the two jobs that decide whether it survives the off-season.

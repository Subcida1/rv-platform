RV roof snow load calculator

Snow is water with air in it, so what it weighs is what the water in it weighs. Enter how deep it is on your roof and this gives you the load in pounds per square foot, and compares it with your maker’s rating if you have one.

How to get it: push a tape measure or a straight rod down to the roof surface at three or four points, and take the average. Snow depth varies a lot over a short distance, so one reading can be well off. If you cannot reach the roof safely, do not go up for this: use the deepest drift you can see and treat the answer as the high end.

How to get it: it is in your owner's manual, usually under specifications or roof. The manuals directory links the official copies by system and by brand. Most makers publish nothing here, and that is normal rather than an omission: no maker is obliged to print a roof limit, and a manual that stays silent is not the maker saying the roof is fine.

If you know the water equivalent, use it instead

This is the depth of water the snow would melt down to, and it is the more precise input because it is weighed rather than inferred. A weather office may have it for your area. Filling this in overrides the depth.

Where the number comes from

Depth alone cannot give a weight, because a foot of dry powder and a foot of wet snow are not the same load. So the depth here is bridged to a weight by a figure a maker publishes: Keystone rates the roofs on its towables at 30 pounds per square foot, and states that as about two feet of snow. Thirty divided by twenty-four is 1.25 pounds per square foot for every inch of snow, and that is what the depth is multiplied by.

Two things about that figure, because it is one maker’s and not a law. It describes snow that has settled, so fresh powder weighs less and wet snow weighs more, which is why the result is the middle of a range rather than the top of it. And it is a rating for Keystone’s own roofs, so it tells you the weight on yours, not what yours is built to carry.

What RV roofs are rated at, in general

Two makers publish a roof figure for a towable, and between them they give you the scale even though neither is your roof:

MakerFigureWhat kind of number it is
Keystone30 lb/ft²A roof rating for its own towables, stated as about two feet of snow
Boxabl20 lb/ft²A live-load rating for the flat roof of one factory-built towable

Those are not the same kind of number as each other, and neither is your roof's rating. Taking the lower of two figures published for two other RVs does not produce a rating for a third: a roof with no published limit may be built to carry less than either. What the pair does tell you is roughly where the line falls for a light towable roof, which is in the tens of pounds per square foot rather than the hundreds.

If your manual publishes one, that figure governs and you can put it in the box above. If it does not, the honest reading of no figure is that the roof was built to shed water rather than to carry snow, so clear it early.

Why it does not ask for your roof pitch

Every figure this tool gives is for a flat roof. A sloped roof carries less, by the cosine of the pitch, and RV roofs are shallow or curved enough that most have no single pitch to enter. The flat figure is the higher one, which is the safe direction to be wrong in.

If you have to measure it yourself

The same note gives the method, and it needs one piece of kit: a long cylinder with a flat bottom and a cap, deep enough to hold the snow. A capped length of PVC pipe works. Push it down through the snow all the way to the roof, turn it over, and let what you have caught melt indoors. The depth of the water left in the cylinder is your water equivalent in inches.

Measure on the roof if you can rather than on the ground. Snow depth varies a great deal over a short distance, and the depth on your roof is the one that matters.

What it will not tell you

It assumes the snow has aged the same way across the area, which is not always true, and it calls the result a rough estimate that should be used with care. Not a structural assessment, and if the number is close to a rating you have, the answer is to clear the snow rather than to keep measuring it.

Sources

  • National Weather Service, Snow Loads (PDF): the water equivalent method, the 62.4 pounds per cubic foot and 5.2 pounds per square foot per inch of water figures, the pitched-roof cosine correction with its worked 2.0 inch example, the cylinder method for measuring water equivalent, and the cautions about uniform metamorphosis and the result being a rough estimate.

Your truck's numbers are on the yellow sticker inside the driver's door and in the owner's manual, and your trailer's are on its own sticker.

Why this matters

Most towing math is only a guess

Dry weight is what the trailer weighed leaving the factory, before water, propane, bikes, camp chairs, and whatever your family loads in. RV Safety & Education Foundation reports that over 50% of existing RVs exceed at least one safety rating. This calculator runs the loaded-weight math against the 80% rule that keeps the margin real. For how each rating is set and what it means, the RV towing capacity guide walks through them.

Verify at a certified scale

Where to get the real numbers

This calculator gives estimates. Weighing at a certified scale is the only way to verify your actual weights. This is a curated map of weigh-stations with links to each source's locator.

Prefer to find it yourself? Search "scale near me", and any certified truck-stop scale works for an RV. Weigh as a normal axle group and read the printout against your ratings.

Step by step

How to weigh your RV at a CAT Scale

Truck drivers have used these certified scales for decades. With CAT Scale's free Weigh My Truck app, the whole thing is contactless, and your weight ticket is emailed to you. Here is exactly how it works for an RV.

1 Set up the app before you leave

Install the free Weigh My Truck app (iOS and Android) and create an account with a payment card. Add your RV's details. Do this at home, not while parked on a scale with a line of trucks behind you.

2 Load the RV the way you really travel

Fill the truck's fuel tank. If you normally travel with water in the fresh tank, fill that too. Load your gear and passengers. Weigh the setup you actually drive, not a bare one.

3 Pull onto the scale, one axle group per platform

Drive slowly over the yellow lines and stop with each axle group centred on its own platform. The signs at the scale tell you where to stop, and you can hop out and check the lines before you settle.

What goes on which platform depends on what you are driving:

  • Travel trailer or fifth wheel. The truck's front axle on the first platform, the truck's rear axle on the second, the trailer's axles on the third. That is the whole combination in one pass.
  • Motorhome. Front axle on the first platform, rear axle on the second. There is no third group unless you are towing something.
  • Motorhome towing a car. Front axle, rear axle, then the towed car on the third platform. Weigh it hitched, because the tow bar puts load on the motorhome.
4 Open the app and confirm the scale

The app uses your location to find the scale. If it does not auto-detect, enter the scale number printed on the intercom sign. Confirm your truck's details and follow the prompts. You do not leave the cab and you do not need the intercom.

5 Weigh, pay, and get your ticket

The app captures the weight, charges the card on file, and emails you a PDF of the official CAT Scale ticket showing each axle group and the gross total. CAT Scale lists its own prices in its FAQ: $15.25 for a first weigh, and $5.25 for a reweigh.

6 For tongue weight, weigh the truck alone

Drive fully off the scale first, park the trailer in the lot, then drive the truck back onto the scale. Reweighs at the same location within 24 hours are $5.25. The difference between the truck's rear axle loaded and unhitched is your real tongue or pin weight. Never uncouple while on the scale.

Plain English

Towing terms, explained without the jargon

Every weight number on your tow vehicle and trailer has a name, and most of them sound alike. Here is what each one means and which is which, so the ratings on your stickers make sense.

Gross Vehicle Weight Rating (GVWR)

The most your vehicle is allowed to weigh when fully loaded. It includes the vehicle itself, everyone inside, and everything carried. Think of it as the ceiling: you are not allowed above it. Every truck and trailer has its own GVWR, printed on a sticker.

Gross Combined Weight Rating (GCWR)

The most the whole combination may weigh: the truck, the trailer, everyone riding in them, and everything you have loaded, all added together. Where GVWR is a ceiling for one vehicle, GCWR is the ceiling for the pair, and it is printed in the truck's owner's manual towing table.

Towing capacity (max towing rating)

The most weight your truck is rated to pull behind it, and the number most likely to mislead you. People quote it as "my truck can tow 10,000 pounds", but it is measured under ideal conditions and it says nothing about whether the truck can carry the tongue weight, stay under its own GVWR, or stop what is behind it. Your towing rating is one of four limits, and it is rarely the one you hit first.

Payload capacity

How much weight your truck may carry inside it and in the bed, including passengers, cargo, and the tongue weight pushing down on the hitch. This is the limit that usually bites first, and the one most drivers have never read. Everything in the cab and the bed spends it, and the trailer's tongue weight is part of the bill before anything else is loaded. Found on the yellow and white sticker inside the driver's door.

Curb weight

How much your vehicle weighs empty, with a full tank of fuel and no people or cargo. It is listed in the owner's manual or the maker's spec sheet, and it matters because payload capacity equals GVWR minus curb weight.

Dry weight, unloaded vehicle weight (UVW)

How much the trailer weighs as it left the factory: no water, no gear, but with the full propane cylinders counted in, because 49 CFR 571.110 requires the maker to include them there. Printed on the trailer sticker, and almost always lighter than what is actually rolling down the road.

Tongue weight

The downward weight the trailer puts on your hitch ball. For a travel trailer it usually runs 10 to 15% of the loaded trailer weight. Too little, and the trailer sways; too much, and it overloads the back of your truck.

Pin weight

The same idea as tongue weight, but for a fifth wheel. The trailer pins down into a hitch in your truck bed instead of a bumper ball, and the usual target is higher, commonly 15 to 25% of loaded weight.

Bumper pull (travel trailer)

Any trailer that hitches behind the truck on a receiver ball. That is the classic camper with a hitch tongue, as opposed to a fifth wheel.

Fifth wheel

A trailer with a kingpin that locks into a hitch mounted in the center of the truck bed, not on the bumper. It rides more stable and has less sway than a bumper pull, which is why large trailers are usually fifth wheels.

Gross Axle Weight Rating (GAWR)

The most weight a single axle may support. Each axle on the truck and trailer has its own rating, in case a heavy load is not split evenly. This is what a certified scale's per-axle printout checks against.

Weight distribution hitch

A hitch with bars that spread the tongue weight between the truck's front and rear axles, instead of dumping it all on the rear. Common on travel trailers when the truck and trailer are close in weight, it keeps the steering and braking predictable. It also holds both vehicles level and stops the rear from sagging, which is what lets the front axle carry its share instead of leaving all of it on the rear.

The 80% rule

A convention rather than a manufacturer's specification: keep trailer weight at or under 80% of your truck's rated towing capacity. No maker publishes an 80% rating, and nothing requires it. Ratings are measured on ideal roads, flat, no wind, at sea level, and real driving has hills, passes, gust fronts and panic stops. The margin is what survives that, and it is the habit worth having if you are new to tow. The ratings it applies to are set under the SAE J2807 standard.

FAQ

The questions everyone asks

What's the 80% rule?

Max towing ratings are measured on ideal roads, flat, no wind, sea level. Real driving has hills, passes, gust fronts, and panic stops. The 80% rule keeps loaded weight at or under 80% of rated capacity so the margin survives reality. It is a convention rather than a manufacturer specification: no maker publishes an 80% rating, and the ratings it applies to are set under the SAE J2807 standard.

Why is tongue weight estimated instead of asked?

Almost nobody knows their trailer's tongue or pin weight without a scale, so we estimate it from the loaded weight, using the usual targets of 10 to 15% for a travel trailer and 15 to 25% for a fifth wheel. Those are conventions rather than rules, and your own trailer's sticker or manual wins where it differs. Weighing at a certified scale gives the real number, and if you have one, the measured field above replaces the estimate with it.

Dry weight vs loaded?

UVW is the weight as the factory built it, and the propane is already inside that number. Loaded adds fresh water at 8.34 lb per gallon, anything sitting in the waste tanks, and everything you carry. A lightweight trailer can gain hundreds of pounds before it ever sees a hitch ball.

Is this a guarantee?

No, nothing here replaces a certified scale and your owner's manuals. This is informational math to catch the obvious mismatch before you buy, pack, or tow. Weigh loaded at a certified scale whenever the load changes.

The limits

A calculator, not a scale

This page does arithmetic on the numbers you type in. It has never seen your truck, your trailer or your load. It cannot know whether the figure you entered is the right one for your vehicle, or whether the sticker you read was the payload sticker or the tire sticker. Nobody has tested it against your setup, and nobody can.

Treat the result as a first look rather than a verdict. The ratings printed on your own vehicle and trailer are the authority, and a certified scale is the only thing that measures what you are actually rolling with. Where this page and your sticker disagree, your sticker is right and this page is wrong.

The arithmetic it uses, the sources behind it, and the hundred worked setups it is checked against are all in the site's own source, which is public.

RV tire date code decoder

Every tire built since 2000 carries the week and year it was made in the last four digits of its DOT code. Type those four digits and this gives you the date and your tire's age.

How to get it: the last four digits of the DOT code molded into the sidewall. It may be on only one side of the tire, so check the inner face if the outer one has nothing.

Enter the last four digits of the DOT code, like 0308.

Where the four digits are

The code is molded into the sidewall and starts with the letters DOT. The four digits at the very end are the date. NHTSA describes the whole number:

"This number begins with the letters DOT and indicates that the tire meets all Federal standards. The next two or three numbers or letters are the plant code where the tire was manufactured. The last four numbers represent the week and year the tire was built. For example, the numbers 3107 means the 31st week of 2007."

Two things trip people up. The code may be on only one side of the tire, so if you cannot find it on the outside, look on the inner sidewall. And the date digits are the last four, not the first four, which are the plant code.

How the four digits read

The first two are the week of the year and the last two are the year, the regulator's wording:

"The last four digits of the TIN indicate the week and year the tire was made. If the TIN reads 0308 it was made in the third week of 2008."

So 0308 is the third week of 2008, and 3107 is the thirty-first week of 2007.

What the age is for

Tires age whether or not they wear, and the age is why this decoder exists. NHTSA: "As tires age, they are more prone to failure. Some vehicle and tire manufacturers recommend replacing tires that are six to 10 years old, regardless of treadwear."

That is a recommendation from the tire and vehicle makers, not a legal limit. Your own tire's maker and your trailer's manual are the two documents that decide that limit, and both are worth reading before a long trip rather than after a blowout.

What this tool will not tell you

It reads the date. It does not judge the tire. Tread depth, cracking, bulges, repairs and how the tire has been stored all matter as much as the number, and none of them are visible in four digits. A tire inside the makers' age window can still be unsafe, and one past it can still look perfect.

And a four-digit code only exists on tires built from 2000, because the format changed then. If the code on your sidewall ends in three digits rather than four, that is a tire from before the change, and the answer is a tire shop rather than a decoder. The RV tire replacement guide covers what to look at besides the date.

Where to go next

The RV tire replacement guide covers the six-year rule in full and what happens to a tire that has gone past it. Trailer wheel bearings are the other job that comes with the wheel off, and the pre-trip walkaround is where a date check belongs before a long run. The parts page lists the tire and the wheel bearings as separate parts, with what fails on each. Replacing a tire past its date is a shop job, and the directory finds a mobile technician or a shop by state.
